1Collector, Land Acquisition, Anantnag v. Mst. Katiji

(1987) 2 SCC 107 · AIR 1987 SC 1353 · decided 19 Feb 1987
Supreme Court · M.P. Thakkar and B.C. Ray, JJ. (judgment by Thakkar J.)
Condonation of delay · S.5 · "sufficient cause"

In 30 seconds

Story: The State appealed just 4 days late. The High Court threw the appeal out as time-barred.

Held: Condone it. "Sufficient cause" must be read liberally; substantial justice beats technicality, and the State is treated like any other litigant.

🧠 4 days late, 6 golden rules

The story

The Jammu & Kashmir government acquired land at Anantnag for a public purpose. A lower court then raised the compensation from ₹1,000 to ₹8,000 per kanal, an increase of about 800%, adding nearly ₹14 lakh to the bill.

The State wanted to challenge this in the High Court, but its appeal was filed 4 days after the time limit. It asked the High Court to excuse the delay under Section 5. The High Court refused and dismissed the appeal as time-barred, without looking at whether the huge increase was right.

The State went to the Supreme Court.

The question

Should a short delay, without any bad intention, stop a court from hearing a serious appeal on its merits? And should the State be judged more harshly than a private person?

What the court held

The Supreme Court condoned the delay, set aside the High Court's order and sent the appeal back to be decided on merits after hearing both sides.

It laid down six principles that every court in India still follows when it reads "sufficient cause" in Section 5.

It added one more point: the State must be given the same treatment as every other litigant, no better and no worse. Government files move slowly through many hands, so courts may take a practical view of official delay.

Principles to quote

  1. Ordinarily, a litigant gains nothing by filing an appeal late.
  2. Refusing to condone delay can throw out a deserving case at the very threshold; condoning it only means the case is heard on merits.
  3. "Every day's delay must be explained" does not mean a pedantic, hair-splitting approach. Apply it in a rational, common-sense, practical way.
  4. When substantial justice and technical considerations clash, substantial justice wins. The other side has no vested right in an injustice caused by a non-deliberate delay.
  5. There is no presumption that delay is deliberate, due to culpable negligence or mala fides. A litigant actually runs a serious risk by delaying.
  6. Courts earn respect by removing injustice, not by legalising it on technical grounds.

Using it in the exam

Part ACondonation of delay: quote principle 3 or 4 and name this case.
Part BSufficient cause / extension of limitation: list 4–5 of the principles.
Part CAny 'appeal or application filed late' problem: apply S.5 + Katiji, then check whether the cause is genuine.
