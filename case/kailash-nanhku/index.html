1Kailash v. Nanhku

(2005) 4 SCC 480 · decided 6 Apr 2005
Supreme Court · R.C. Lahoti CJI, D.M. Dharmadhikari and P.K. Balasubramanyan, JJ. (judgment by Lahoti CJI)
O.VIII r.1 · 90-day limit is directory

In 30 seconds

Story: A written statement was filed 5 days after the 90-day limit because the advocate's clerk failed to file it; the High Court refused to accept it.

Held: Accepted with costs. The time limit is directory, not mandatory, but extensions are exceptional and need reasons.

🧠 Procedure is the handmaid of justice

The story

Kailash was declared elected to the Uttar Pradesh Legislative Council. Nanhku challenged his election in the High Court through an election petition, where the CPC procedure applies.

Kailash's written statement was due on 3 July 2004. His advocate's clerk was to file it that day but failed to do so; the advocate's nephew had died on 22 June 2004. It was filed on 8 July 2004, five days late, with an application to condone the delay.

The High Court rejected the written statement because it was filed beyond the 90 days allowed by the proviso to Order VIII Rule 1. Kailash appealed to the Supreme Court.

The question

Is the 90-day outer limit for filing a written statement in O.VIII r.1 mandatory, so that a court can never accept it later?

What the court held

No. The provision is procedural and therefore directory, though worded in mandatory form.

The time schedule should ordinarily be followed; extension beyond 90 days is allowed only as an exception, for reasons recorded, where grave injustice would otherwise result. The written statement was taken on record, subject to ₹5,000 costs.

Principles to quote

  1. "All the rules of procedure are the handmaid of justice." They are meant to advance justice, not defeat it.
  2. The purpose of the amendment was to stop delay, not to punish a defendant for a lapse beyond his control.
  3. Extension is an exception: the court must record reasons and may impose costs.
  4. In commercial suits the position is different: the 120-day limit is strict (SCG Contracts v. K.S. Chamankar, 2019).
