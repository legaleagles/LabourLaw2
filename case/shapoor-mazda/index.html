3Shapoor Fredoom Mazda v. Durga Prosad Chamaria

AIR 1961 SC 1236 · decided 1 Mar 1961
Supreme Court · P.B. Gajendragadkar and K.N. Wanchoo, JJ. (judgment by Gajendragadkar J.)
Acknowledgment of liability · S.18 (S.19 of the old 1908 Act)

In 30 seconds

Story: A borrower's letter said "a payment of 10,000 will stop the sale" and never said "I promise to pay".

Held: Still a valid acknowledgment. It must admit a present, subsisting liability; a promise to pay is not needed. The clock restarts from the date it is signed.

🧠 Admit the debt, restart the clock

The story

Durga Prosad Chamaria held a second (puisne) mortgage over property owned by J.C. Galstaun. A prior mortgagee had advertised the Chandni Bazar property for sale.

On 5 March 1932, Galstaun wrote and signed a letter to Chamaria saying that "a payment of 10,000 will stop the sale" and asking him to "take up the whole".

Chamaria sued Galstaun's heirs much later, on 18 May 1944. The suit could survive only if that 1932 letter counted as an acknowledgment of the mortgage debt, which would restart the limitation clock.

The trial judge said the letter was not an acknowledgment. The Calcutta High Court on appeal said it was. The auction purchaser of the property appealed to the Supreme Court.

The question

Does a letter count as an "acknowledgment of liability" even if it never says "I owe you" or "I promise to pay"?

What the court held

Yes. The Supreme Court dismissed the appeal: read in its context, the letter admitted the existing mortgagor–mortgagee relationship, so it was a valid acknowledgment and the suit was within time.

The case was decided under S.19 of the Limitation Act, 1908. The same rule is now S.18 of the Limitation Act, 1963.

Principles to quote

  1. The statement must relate to a present, subsisting liability.
  2. It must show a legal relationship such as debtor and creditor, and an intention, express or implied, to admit it.
  3. No promise to pay is needed. An acknowledgment only renews the debt; it does not create a new cause of action.
  4. Read the words in context, with the surrounding circumstances, but oral evidence of what the writer "meant" is not allowed.
  5. Courts should not stretch words to find an admission where none was made.

Using it in the exam

Part AAcknowledgment: give the 3 requirements (before expiry · in writing · signed) and this case's "subsisting liability" test.
Part BExtension of limitation: acknowledgment (S.18) and part payment (S.19) both start a fresh period.
Part CDebtor wrote a letter, SMS or signed note admitting the debt before the period ended: fresh 3 years from that date.
