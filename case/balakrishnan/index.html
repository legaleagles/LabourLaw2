2N. Balakrishnan v. M. Krishnamurthy

(1998) 7 SCC 123 · decided 3 Sep 1998
Supreme Court · K.T. Thomas and S. Saghir Ahmad, JJ. (judgment by Thomas J.)
Condonation of delay · S.5 · length of delay v explanation

In 30 seconds

Story: 883 days late, because his advocate took ₹2,000, did nothing and left practice without telling him.

Held: Condoned. "Length of delay is no matter, acceptability of the explanation is the only criterion." The other side was paid ₹10,000.

🧠 883 days, but an honest reason wins

The story

A suit for declaration of title was decided against the defendant ex parte (in his absence) on 28 October 1991. He applied to set aside that ex parte decree, but his application was itself dismissed for default on 17 February 1993.

His advocate never told him. In July 1995, when execution papers reached him, the same advocate took ₹2,000 and his signatures, and again did nothing. Only when an execution warrant was issued did he check the court records and learn the truth. The advocate had left practice to join a company.

He filed for restoration on 19 August 1995, a delay of 883 days. (He also won a consumer case against the advocate, who was ordered to pay ₹50,000.) The trial court accepted his explanation and condoned the delay. The Madras High Court reversed it in revision.

He went to the Supreme Court.

The question

Is a very long delay (883 days) automatically fatal, even when the reason is genuine and the fault was mainly the lawyer's?

What the court held

The Supreme Court set aside the High Court's order and restored the trial court's order condoning the delay, on condition that the appellant pay ₹10,000 to the other side within one month.

"Length of delay is no matter, acceptability of the explanation is the only criterion."

Principles to quote

  1. Condoning delay is a discretion of the court; what matters is whether the explanation is acceptable, not how many days have passed.
  2. Rules of limitation are not meant to destroy the rights of parties. They exist so that people do not use dilatory tactics and seek remedies promptly.
  3. "Sufficient cause" must be read liberally to advance substantial justice, when there is no mala fides or deliberate delay.
  4. Once the first court accepts an explanation, a higher court should not interfere unless that discretion was arbitrary or perverse.
  5. The court must also be fair to the other side. Where the delay is partly the applicant's own fault, he can be made to compensate the other side (here, ₹10,000).

Using it in the exam
