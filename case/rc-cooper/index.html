1Rustom Cavasjee Cooper v. Union of India (Bank Nationalisation case)

(1970) 1 SCC 248 · AIR 1970 SC 564 · decided 10 Feb 1970
Supreme Court · 11 judges (majority 10:1)
Bank nationalisation · compensation · fundamental rights

In 30 seconds

Story: Overnight, a law took over 14 big banks; a shareholder and depositor challenged it.

Held: The 1969 Act was struck down: the compensation principles were unfair and the named banks were wrongly barred from doing any banking in future. A new Act followed in 1970.

🧠 Even the State must pay fairly

The story

On 19 July 1969, the President issued an ordinance taking over 14 major banks, each with deposits of ₹50 crore or more; Parliament then passed the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1969.

Rustom Cavasjee Cooper, who held shares, accounts and directorships in some of these banks, challenged the law in the Supreme Court.

He argued that the compensation was illusory and that the law discriminated against the 14 banks by stopping them from carrying on banking at all.

The question

Was the 1969 nationalisation law valid under the Constitution?

What the court held

No. By majority, the Court held the Act void: the compensation was not fixed on relevant principles (for example, important assets were ignored), violating Article 31(2) as it then stood.

Barring the named banks from carrying on banking business in future was a hostile discrimination and an unreasonable restriction.

The Court also said fundamental rights must be judged by the effect of a law on the citizen, not merely its object. Parliament re-enacted nationalisation by the 1970 Act.

Principles to quote

  1. Nationalisation of banks is a valid policy, but it must follow the Constitution.
  2. The test of a law affecting fundamental rights is its direct effect.
  3. This is why the Acquisition Act of 1970 (and of 1980) governs nationalised banks today.
