Plaint and Pleadings

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

A suit is a story told to the court, and the plaint is its first chapter. The court will judge only the story that is written down, so the rules of pleading are the rules of good story-telling: tell facts, not law; tell material facts, not the evidence; tell them briefly, in numbered paragraphs.

Write the whole story once (O.II r.2): if Ravi sues Somu only for rent and forgets the damages, he cannot come back later for the damages. And if he made a genuine mistake in the story, the court lets him amend it, as long as he does not turn it into a different story or play tricks after the trial has begun.

Chapter 1 · asked 12× 🔥🔥🔥 Must do

Plaint: S.26, essential contents (O.VII r.1–8) · Return (r.10) and Rejection (r.11) of plaint

📖 The story: T. Arivandandam v. T.V. Satyapal

(1977) 4 SCC 467 · AIR 1977 SC 2421 · decided 14 Oct 1977

A landlord in Bangalore obtained an eviction order against a tenant firm. The tenant fought and lost at the trial, appellate and revisional levels; the High Court gave him six months to vacate.

Then the tenant's minor son, his partner in the firm, filed a suit claiming the eviction order was obtained by fraud and collusion, and asked for an injunction. When the tenant asked for more time to vacate, the landlord agreed on the understanding that the suit would be withdrawn. It was not.

Instead, a second suit, almost a copy of the first, was filed before another judge, and an ex parte injunction was obtained. The matter reached the Supreme Court.

❓ What should a trial court do with a plaint that is cleverly drafted to look like a cause of action but is really meant to abuse the process of the court?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Meaning. A plaint is the written statement of the plaintiff's claim, with which a suit begins. S.26: "Every suit shall be instituted by the presentation of a plaint or in such other manner as may be prescribed", and the facts in it must be verified by affidavit (S.26(2), added 2002). The date of presentation is the date of institution, which matters for limitation.

Contents (O.VII r.1). (a) name of the court; (b) name, description and residence of the plaintiff; (c) the same for the defendant, as far as known; (d) if either is a minor or of unsound mind, a statement to that effect; (e) facts constituting the cause of action and when it arose; (f) facts showing the court has jurisdiction; (g) the relief claimed; (h) any set-off or relinquishment allowed by the plaintiff; (i) a statement of the value of the subject matter for jurisdiction and court fee.

Further rules. r.2: money suits state the precise amount. r.3: immovable property described sufficiently to identify it (boundaries, survey numbers). r.4: representative capacity shown. r.6: if time-barred on its face, the ground of exemption from limitation must be shown. r.7: relief stated specifically; general relief need not be asked. r.8: reliefs on separate grounds stated separately. r.14: documents relied on are listed and produced.

Return of plaint (r.10). At any stage, the plaint is returned to be presented in the court in which it should have been filed, when the court lacks territorial, pecuniary or subject-matter jurisdiction. The judge endorses on it the dates of presentation and return, the party and the reasons. r.10A: the court may fix a date for appearance in the proper court.

Rejection of plaint (r.11). (a) it does not disclose a cause of action; (b) the relief is undervalued and not corrected within the time given; (c) insufficiently stamped and not made good; (d) the suit appears from the plaint to be barred by any law (e.g. limitation, res judicata); (e) not filed in duplicate; (f) r.9 not complied with. Only the plaint (and documents filed with it) is read, not the defence. r.13: rejection does not bar a fresh plaint on the same cause.

Return v. rejection (write as a table):

Groundwrong court · defect in the plaint (r.11 grounds)
Plainthanded back to be filed in the proper court · thrown out
Effectsuit continues elsewhere (time in the wrong court excluded: S.14 Lim. Act) · suit ends; fresh plaint allowed (r.13)
Naturean order, appealable (O.XLIII r.1(a)) · a decree (S.2(2)), appealable under S.96

Conclusion. A good plaint states a clear cause of action, the court's jurisdiction, the value and the relief; a bad one is returned or rejected at the threshold.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

The Supreme Court dismissed the special leave petition and condemned the abuse of process.

If on a meaningful (not formal) reading the plaint is manifestly vexatious and meritless, the court should reject it under O.VII r.11; if clever drafting creates an illusion of a cause of action, the court should "nip it in the bud at the first hearing by examining the party searchingly under Order X".

Why it matters:

  • Read the plaint meaningfully, not just formally.
  • Bogus litigation should be shot down at the first hearing; trial judges should not be soft with it.
  • Use O.VII r.11, O.X (examination of parties) and S.35A (compensatory costs) against vexatious suits.

  • Part CA plaint that discloses no real cause of action: reject under r.11(a), citing this case.

Chapter 2 · asked 9× 🔥🔥🔥 Must do

Pleadings: meaning, fundamental rules (O.VI r.1–16) · Amendment of pleadings (O.VI r.17–18)

📖 The story: No surprises at trial

At trial, a defendant suddenly says the suit property was gifted to him, something he never mentioned in his written statement.

❓ What does the law say?

⚖️ The law behind the story

Here is the law that decides it:

Meaning (r.1). "Pleading" means a plaint or a written statement. Object: to tell each side exactly what the other will prove, narrow the dispute to definite issues, and prevent surprise at the trial.

Fundamental rule (r.2). Every pleading states (1) facts, not law; (2) material facts only, (3) not the evidence by which they will be proved; (4) concisely; in paragraphs numbered consecutively, with dates, sums and numbers in figures and words.

Other rules. r.4: full particulars must be given of misrepresentation, fraud, breach of trust, wilful default or undue influence. r.6: performance of a condition precedent is implied. r.7: no new ground inconsistent with earlier pleadings, except by amendment. r.13: presumptions of law need not be pleaded. r.14: signed by the party and his pleader; r.15: verified (true to knowledge / information and belief). r.16: the court may strike out unnecessary, scandalous, frivolous or vexatious matter, or matter that abuses the process of the court.

💡 Example: "The defendant, by fraud, got the plaintiff's signature" is not enough: r.4 needs particulars: when, where, what was said, what was false. "Under S.10 of the Contract Act the agreement is valid" is law, not fact: leave it out.

Amendment (r.17). The court may at any stage allow either party to alter or amend his pleading as may be just, and all amendments necessary to decide the real questions in controversy shall be made. Proviso (2002): no amendment after the trial has commenced, unless the court concludes that, despite due diligence, the party could not have raised it before.

Principles applied by courts (Revajeetu Builders, 2009): allowed if (1) necessary to decide the real controversy; (2) bona fide; (3) the other side can be compensated by costs; (4) refusing it would cause injustice or multiple suits. Refused if it (5) changes the fundamental nature of the case, or (6) introduces a claim already time-barred on the date of the application, taking away a right that has accrued to the other side. Amendments to a written statement are viewed more liberally. r.18: if the party does not amend within the time fixed (or 14 days), he cannot do so later without leave.

Conclusion. Pleadings are the foundation of the suit; amendment repairs the foundation, but cannot replace the building.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ends

Pleadings (plaint and written statement) exist to tell each side exactly what the other will prove and prevent surprise.

He cannot spring the gift on the plaintiff: no plea, no proof, unless he amends.

  • Part AWrite the gold-edged lines above: meaning, section and one example or case.
  • Part BHeadings: Meaning → Fundamental rule (r.2) → Other rules → Amendment (r.17) → Principles. Memory trick: "Facts not law, material not evidence, brief".

🪝 Amend freely before trial; after trial begins, only if you could not have raised it earlier.

Chapter 3 · asked 6× 🔥🔥 Important

Cause of action · Frame of suit (O.II) · Settlement of disputes outside court (S.89)

📖 The story: Gurbux Singh v. Bhooralal

AIR 1964 SC 1810 · decided 22 Apr 1964

Bhooralal and his mother first sued Gurbux Singh in 1950 only for mesne profits (income from the property) for a past period, and got a decree.

In 1954 Bhooralal sued Gurbux Singh again, this time for possession of the same property and further mesne profits.

Gurbux Singh argued that Bhooralal should have claimed possession in the first suit, so O.II r.2 barred the second. The trial court agreed; the appellate court and the High Court did not. Gurbux Singh appealed.

❓ What must a defendant prove to succeed in a plea that a suit is barred by Order II Rule 2?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Cause of action. Not defined in the CPC. It is the bundle of facts which the plaintiff must prove, if traversed, to get a judgment. It includes the plaintiff's right and its infringement, but not the defence or the evidence. It decides jurisdiction (S.20(c)), limitation and the frame of the suit.

O.II r.2: include the whole claim. (1) A suit must include the whole claim arising from the cause of action. (2) If the plaintiff omits or relinquishes part of the claim, he cannot sue for it later. (3) If he omits one of several reliefs without the court's leave, he cannot sue for it later. Object: no one should be vexed twice for the same cause.

Other rules of O.II. r.3: several causes of action against the same defendant may be joined in one suit. r.4: in a suit for recovery of immovable property, only claims for mesne profits, arrears of rent, damages for breach of the contract under which the property was held, and claims for the same relief may be joined, unless the court allows others. r.6: the court may order separate trials if joinder embarrasses the trial. r.7: objections to misjoinder of causes of action must be taken early.

S.89: settlement outside court. Where it appears to the court that elements of a settlement acceptable to the parties exist, it refers the dispute to one of four routes: arbitration, conciliation, judicial settlement including Lok Adalat, or mediation. O.X r.1A–1C: after admissions and denials are recorded, the court directs the parties to opt for one; if the effort fails, they return to court.

How it works. In Afcons Infrastructure v. Cherian Varkey Construction (2010), the Supreme Court said the court need only summarise the disputes, not draft settlement terms; arbitration and conciliation need the consent of both parties, while Lok Adalat and mediation do not; some cases (e.g. fraud, criminal offences, election disputes, representative suits) are unsuitable. A Lok Adalat award is deemed a decree and final (S.21, Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987), and the court fee is refunded on settlement.

Conclusion. State the whole cause of action once, claim every relief once, and try settlement before trial.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

The Supreme Court dismissed the appeal.

The defendant must show that (1) the second suit is on the same cause of action as the first, (2) the plaintiff was entitled to more than one relief on it, and (3) he omitted the relief now claimed without leave of the court.

Since the pleadings of the first suit were not on record, the identity of the causes of action could not be found, so the plea failed.

Why it matters:

  • O.II r.2 is a technical bar and must be strictly proved.
  • The earlier plaint must be produced; the court cannot guess the earlier cause of action from the relief claimed.
  • The rule's object is to prevent splitting of claims and vexing a defendant twice for the same cause.

  • Part BRules of pleading or plaint: mention O.II r.2 and its strict proof. Headings: Cause of action → O.II r.2 → Other rules of O.II → S.89 ADR → How it works. Memory trick: "Whole claim once" (O.II r.2) · "A-C-L-M": Arbitration · Conciliation · Lok Adalat · Mediation (S.89).
  • Part CPlaintiff sued for one relief and now sues for another: barred only if the same cause of action is proved.

Part C workshop · 10 marks each

Solve the problem like a judge

Every Part C problem is a new story with the same law. Spot the issue, state the law, apply it to the facts, and conclude. Quote the matching case story to lift your answer.

Problem 1. A wants to sue B for recovery of ₹10,000 due on a promissory note. Draft a plaint.

Asked: 2020 · Feb 2024

Issue
Draft a plaint containing every particular required by O.VII r.1.
Law
S.26 and O.VII r.1–2, r.14: court, parties, cause of action, jurisdiction, limitation, valuation, relief; verification (O.VI r.15) and affidavit (S.26(2)). Art. 35 Lim. Act: 3 years.
Apply
IN THE COURT OF THE JUNIOR CIVIL JUDGE AT ______ · O.S. No. ___ of 20__. A, s/o ___, aged __, occupation, residing at ___ … Plaintiff; versus B, s/o ___, aged __, residing at ___ … Defendant. Suit for recovery of money on a promissory note. 1. Description of parties. 2. On __ (date) B borrowed ₹10,000 from A and executed a promissory note payable on demand with interest at __% p.a. 3. A demanded repayment; legal notice dated __; B has not paid. 4. Cause of action arose on the date of the note and on the date of demand, at ___, within this court's jurisdiction. 5. Jurisdiction: B resides / cause of action arose here (S.20). 6. Limitation: within 3 years (Art. 35). 7. Valuation: ₹10,000 + interest ₹___ = ₹___; court fee paid under the State Court-fees and Suits Valuation Act. 8. Relief: (a) decree for ₹___ with future interest; (b) costs; (c) other relief. Place, date; signatures of plaintiff and advocate. Verification: "I, A, verify that paras 1–8 are true to my knowledge and belief." List of documents: original pro-note, notice, postal receipt.
Conclusion
A plaint with all O.VII r.1 particulars, the original pro-note filed with it, verification and an affidavit under S.26(2).

Problem 2. A filed a declaration suit, but omitted some facts and made mistakes inadvertently. What remedy under the CPC?

Asked: 2014

Issue
Can a party correct his pleadings during the suit?
Law
O.VI r.17: amendment at any stage to decide the real controversy; proviso: after trial starts, only if he could not have raised it earlier with due diligence. O.VI r.18: amend within the time fixed or 14 days.
Apply
The mistakes were bona fide and do not change the nature of the suit; the defendant can be compensated by costs. If the trial has not started, the court should allow it; if it has, A must show why he could not discover it earlier.
Conclusion
A should apply under O.VI r.17 to amend the plaint; it will be allowed if bona fide and necessary, especially before trial, on costs.

Problem 3. A plaintiff whose cause of action is time-barred files a condone-delay petition along with his suit. Is it maintainable?

Asked: 2016

Issue
Can delay in filing a suit be condoned?
Law
S.5 Limitation Act applies only to appeals and applications, not suits. S.3: a time-barred suit shall be dismissed. O.VII r.11(d): plaint rejected if barred by law on its face; O.VII r.6: grounds of exemption must be shown in the plaint.
Apply
Unless the plaint shows a legal ground of exemption (disability, acknowledgment, part payment, S.14 exclusion), the court cannot extend time for a suit.
Conclusion
No. A condone-delay petition is not maintainable for a suit; the plaint will be rejected (O.VII r.11(d)) or the suit dismissed (S.3).

Problem 4. C was not a party to a partnership suit. In final decree proceedings he files an impleading petition claiming to own the property; it is dismissed. What is his remedy?

Asked: 2014

Issue
Remedy of a stranger whose property is affected by a decree between others.
Law
A decree binds only the parties (and those claiming under them). O.I r.10(2): a person may be added if necessary; refusal can be challenged by revision or under Article 227. O.XXI r.97–101: a stranger resisting delivery of possession can have his claim adjudicated in execution. He may also file a separate suit for declaration of his title.
Apply
The final decree between the partners cannot bind C's independent title.
Conclusion
C can challenge the dismissal in revision / Art. 227, file his own suit for declaration of title, or resist delivery under O.XXI r.97–101; the decree does not bind him.
