Negotiable Instruments: Notes, Bills and Holders

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

A negotiable instrument is money on paper that can travel. Ravi buys goods from Sita and gives her a written promise to pay ₹50,000 in three months. Sita needs cash today, so she signs the back and hands it to Gopal, who pays her ₹49,000. Gopal can now collect the full ₹50,000 from Ravi. The paper moved from hand to hand like a currency note, and each honest buyer got a clean title.

Three papers do this job under S.13 of the NI Act: the promissory note (a promise to pay: two parties), the bill of exchange (an order to pay: three parties) and the cheque (an order on a banker, payable on demand). The person who buys such paper honestly and for value, the holder in due course, is the hero of this Act.

Negotiable instrument: meaning, features and kinds

⚡ 30-second version · Bir Singh

📍A man gave a signed cheque to his tax consultant; the amount was filled in later. It bounced, and he said the consultant had filled it in himself.
❓Does the presumption of a debt apply when a signed blank cheque is filled in by the payee or someone else?
🏛️A signed cheque voluntarily handed over attracts the presumption of a debt even if someone else filled in the details; the drawer must disprove the debt.

🪝 Your signature speaks for you

📖 Read the full story 3 short paragraphs

Bir Singh v. Mukesh Kumar · (2019) 4 SCC 197

Mukesh Kumar gave Bir Singh, his tax consultant, a cheque that he had signed. The cheque was later presented for a large amount and was dishonoured.

Bir Singh filed a cheque-bounce complaint. The trial court convicted Mukesh Kumar and the appeal failed.

The High Court acquitted him in revision, reasoning that the consultant stood in a relationship of trust and had filled in the cheque himself.

⚖️ The law you must know Part A lines

Meaning

S.13 NI Act, 1881: a negotiable instrument means a promissory note, bill of exchange or cheque payable either to order or to bearer. "Negotiable" means transferable by delivery or by indorsement and delivery, so that the transferee gets the right to sue in his own name.

Features
  1. Easy transfer: bearer paper by mere delivery, order paper by indorsement and delivery (S.47, S.48).
  2. Better title: a holder in due course gets a title free from defects of earlier parties (S.9, S.53).
  3. Right to sue in his own name; no notice of transfer to the debtor is needed.
  4. Presumptions in favour of the holder (S.118).
  5. Always for a sum of money only, in writing and signed.
Kinds under the Act

Only three are named: promissory note (S.4), bill of exchange (S.5) and cheque (S.6). A cheque is a special bill drawn on a banker and payable on demand.

Inchoate instrument (S.20)

When a person signs and delivers a stamped paper blank or incomplete, he authorises the holder to complete it for any amount not exceeding what the stamp covers; he is liable to a holder in due course for that amount Bir Singh.

➕ More for Part B and examples 4 more points
💡 ExampleExample: a ₹10,000 cheque "pay Sita or order" can be indorsed by Sita to Gopal and by Gopal to Hari. Hari, if he took it honestly and for value, can sue every earlier signer if the cheque bounces.
Deemed negotiable by custom

S.13 is not exhaustive. By mercantile usage, hundis, bank drafts, dividend warrants, share warrants to bearer and bearer debentures are treated as negotiable. Postal orders, money orders, deposit receipts and share certificates are not negotiable.

Inland and foreign (S.11, S.12)

An instrument drawn or made in India and payable in India (or drawn on a person resident in India) is inland; any other is foreign. A foreign bill must be protested on dishonour where the law of the place where it was drawn requires it (S.104).

Presumptions (S.118)

Until the contrary is proved, it is presumed: (a) the instrument was made for consideration; (b) it was made on the date it bears; (c) a bill was accepted within a reasonable time after its date and before maturity; (d) transfer was before maturity; (e) indorsements were made in the order they appear; (f) a lost instrument was duly stamped; (g) the holder is a holder in due course. S.119: on proof of protest, the court presumes dishonour.

🏛️ Why the court decided so

Yes. If the drawer voluntarily signs and hands over a cheque, the payee may fill in the amount and other details; this does not invalidate the cheque.

The presumption that the cheque was given for a debt (S.139) applies, and the drawer must rebut it with evidence. The conviction was restored.

  • A signed blank cheque is like an inchoate instrument (S.20): the signer authorises completion.
  • Handwriting of the filled-in details is irrelevant if the signature is admitted.
  • The presumption can be rebutted only by proof that there was no debt, not by a bare denial.

Chapter 2 · asked 8× 🔥🔥🔥 Must do

Promissory note and bill of exchange: essentials, distinction and maturity

⚡ 30-second version · A promise on paper

📍A farmer signs: "I promise to pay Lakshmi Traders or order ₹1,00,000 on 1 December 2025."
⚖️S.4: a promissory note is an unconditional undertaking in writing, signed by the maker, to pay a certain sum of money to a certain person or his order.
✅This is a valid promissory note.

⚖️ The law you must know Part A lines

Promissory note (S.4)

An instrument in writing (not a bank or currency note) containing an unconditional undertaking, signed by the maker, to pay a certain sum of money only to, or to the order of, a certain person, or to the bearer.

Essentials
  1. In writing;
  2. an express promise, not a mere acknowledgment ("Mr B, I.O.U. ₹1,000" is not a note);
  3. unconditional;
  4. signed by the maker;
  5. maker and payee certain;
  6. sum certain;
  7. money only (S.4 illustrations: "and deliver my black horse" fails);
  8. properly stamped.
Bill of exchange (S.5)

An instrument in writing containing an unconditional order, signed by the maker, directing a certain person to pay a certain sum of money only to, or to the order of, a certain person or to the bearer. Parties: drawer (orders), drawee (ordered; becomes acceptor on signing) and payee (S.7).

At sight, on presentment, after sight (S.21)

"At sight" and "on presentment" mean on demand (no grace days). "After sight" means after acceptance or protest for non-acceptance (bill), or after presentment for sight (note).

➕ More for Part B and examples 3 more points
💡 ExampleS.4 illustrations to quote: "I promise to pay B or order ₹500" is a note. "I acknowledge myself indebted to B in ₹1,000, to be paid on demand, for value received" is also a note. But "I promise to pay B ₹500 seven days after my marriage with C" is not: the marriage may never happen.
Note v. bill
  1. Note = promise; bill = order.
  2. Note has two parties; bill has three.
  3. Maker of a note is primarily liable; drawer of a bill is liable only if the acceptor fails (S.30, S.32).
  4. A bill needs acceptance (unless on demand); a note does not.
  5. A note cannot be payable to bearer (RBI Act S.31 bars it); a bill on demand to bearer is also barred.
  6. Protest is mainly a requirement for foreign bills (S.104).
Maturity and days of grace (S.21–25)

A note or bill not payable on demand is at maturity on the third day after the day it is expressed to be payable (S.22: three days of grace). "So many months after date": the corresponding date of that month, or the last day if none (S.23). "So many days after date": exclude the day of the date (S.24). If maturity falls on a public holiday, it is payable on the preceding business day (S.25).

Chapter 3 · asked 6× 🔥🔥 Important

Holder, holder in due course and payment in due course

⚡ 30-second version · The finder is not a holder

📍A man finds a lost cheque payable "to Sita or order" on a bus and tries to cash it.
⚖️S.8: a holder is one entitled in his own name to possess and recover. A finder or thief of an order instrument is not a holder.
✅He is not a holder and gets no right to the money.

⚖️ The law you must know Part A lines

Holder (S.8)

The person entitled in his own name to possession of the instrument and to receive or recover the amount due. A thief or a finder of an order instrument is not a holder.

Holder in due course (S.9)

A person who, for consideration, became the possessor (bearer instrument) or the payee or indorsee (order instrument) before the amount became payable, and without sufficient cause to believe that any defect existed in the title of the person from whom he derived his title. Three tests: value, before maturity, good faith.

Payment in due course (S.10)

Payment according to the apparent tenor of the instrument, in good faith and without negligence, to any person in possession of it under circumstances which do not give reasonable ground to believe he is not entitled to payment. Such payment discharges the instrument and protects the payer (and the paying banker: S.85, S.128).

➕ More for Part B and examples 2 more points
💡 ExampleExample: A obtains B's acceptance to a bill by fraud and indorses it to C, who pays full value without knowing of the fraud. C is a holder in due course and can recover from B; the fraud is no defence against C (S.58 bars only the wrongdoer and those taking from him with notice).
Privileges of a holder in due course
  1. Gets a title free from defects of prior parties (S.9, S.58).
  2. Every prior party is liable to him until the instrument is satisfied (S.36).
  3. A later holder who takes from an HDC gets the same rights, even without value, unless he was party to a fraud (S.53).
  4. Estoppel: the maker, drawer or acceptor cannot deny the original validity of the instrument against him (S.120); the maker or acceptor cannot deny the payee's capacity to indorse (S.121).
  5. Even an inchoate instrument binds the signer to him (S.20).

HDC is a V-B-G customer: Value paid, Before maturity, Good faith. Payment in due course is the mirror: apparent tenor, good faith, no negligence.

Chapter 4 · asked 4× 🔥🔥 Important

Presentment, noting and protest, acceptance for honour and bills in sets

⚡ 30-second version · The bill nobody showed the buyer

📍A holder of a bill payable "60 days after sight" keeps it in his drawer for a year without presenting it for acceptance.
⚖️S.61: a bill payable after sight must be presented for acceptance within a reasonable time; otherwise no party is liable to that holder.
✅By his delay the holder loses his rights against the parties.

⚖️ The law you must know Part A lines

Presentment for acceptance (S.61)

A bill payable after sight must be presented to the drawee for acceptance within a reasonable time after it is drawn, on a business day and at a reasonable hour; in default, no party to the bill is liable to the holder who made the default. A note payable at a certain period after sight must be presented to the maker for sight (S.62).

Presentment for payment (S.64–75)

Every instrument must be presented for payment to the maker, acceptor or drawee by or on behalf of the holder, at the proper place (S.68–70) and during business hours (S.65); a cheque within a reasonable time to charge the drawer (S.72, S.84). Without proper presentment, the other parties are not liable to the holder.

Noting and protest (S.99, S.100)

On dishonour, the holder may get a notary public to record the fact of dishonour on the instrument (noting, within a reasonable time); the notary's formal certificate of dishonour is the protest. Protest is required for foreign bills where the law of the place of drawing requires it (S.104) and is useful evidence for inland ones (S.119 presumption).

➕ More for Part B and examples 3 more points
When presentment is excused (S.76)

If the maker or drawee intentionally prevents presentment; if the place of business is closed on the due date; if no person is there to pay; if the party has promised to pay notwithstanding non-presentment; or if, after diligent search, the drawee cannot be found. S.75A: delay is excused if caused by circumstances beyond the holder's control.

Acceptance and payment for honour (S.108–116)

When a bill has been noted or protested for non-acceptance, any person not already liable on it may, with the holder's consent, accept it for the honour of any party liable (the drawer or an indorser); he becomes liable to all parties after the party for whose honour he accepted. Payment for honour gives him the holder's rights against that party and prior parties.

Bills in sets (S.132, S.133)

Foreign bills are often drawn in a set of two or three parts, each sent separately, so that one at least arrives. All parts together make one bill; payment of one discharges all. The holder of the part first acquired is entitled to the others and to payment (S.133).

Problem 1. (a) "I promise to pay B ₹500 when B delivers the goods", signed by A. (b) A owes B ₹1,000 and writes a note acknowledging the debt. Is either a promissory note?

Asked: Nov 2022 · Jan 2025 · 2015

🤔 Try it in your head, then tap to see the answer
Issue
Do these writings satisfy S.4 NI Act?
Law
S.4: unconditional undertaking, signed, to pay a certain sum of money to a certain person. Illustrations: "I acknowledge myself indebted to B in ₹1,000, to be paid on demand, for value received" is a note; "Mr B, I.O.U. ₹1,000" is not; a promise on an uncertain event (after my marriage) is not.
Apply
(a) Payment depends on delivery of goods, an event that may or may not happen: the undertaking is conditional. (b) A bare acknowledgment ("I owe B ₹1,000") has no promise to pay; if the writing adds "to be paid on demand" or "I promise to pay", it becomes a note.
Conclusion
(a) Not a promissory note; it is an ordinary contract to pay on delivery. (b) A mere acknowledgment (I.O.U.) is not a note, but it is evidence of the debt and an acknowledgment under S.18 Limitation Act; if it contains a promise to pay, it is a note.

Problem 2. Umang lent money to Jayant in 2015. Later Jayant wrote a promissory note acknowledging the (time-barred) debt and promising to pay within a year. Umang indorsed it to Pratap for value. Jayant refuses: "no liability existed when I made the note". Decide.

Asked: Aug 2025

🤔 Try it in your head, then tap to see the answer
Issue
Is a promissory note given for a time-barred debt valid, and can the indorsee sue?
Law
Contract Act S.25(3): a written, signed promise to pay a time-barred debt is valid without fresh consideration. NI Act S.118(a): presumption of consideration; S.9: holder in due course; S.36: prior parties liable to HDC.
Apply
By the time the note was written, the 2015 loan was time-barred (3 years). But S.25(3) makes Jayant's written promise enforceable. Pratap took the note for value before maturity and in good faith, so he is a holder in due course.
Conclusion
The note is valid; Jayant's defence fails. Pratap can sue Jayant (maker) and, if needed, Umang (indorser).

Problem 3. A minor borrows ₹50,000 on a promissory note. On attaining majority he executes a fresh note "in consideration of" the earlier one. Is a suit maintainable on the fresh note?

Asked: Sep 2024

🤔 Try it in your head, then tap to see the answer
Issue
Can a note made on majority to pay a loan taken during minority be enforced?
Law
Contract Act S.11: a minor's agreement is void Mohori Bibee. NI Act S.26: a minor cannot bind himself. Consideration for the new promise must be real; a void earlier note is no consideration; ratification of a void agreement is not possible.
Apply
The first note was void from the start. The new note rests only on that void note (no new money), so it has no lawful consideration.
Conclusion
No. The fresh note is void for want of consideration and the suit is not maintainable. (If fresh money had been lent on majority, the new note would be valid to that extent.)

📖 Story to quote: Mohori Bibee

Problem 4. The Managing Director of a company borrows ₹1 lakh from A on a promissory note, without indicating that he signs for the company. Is the company liable?

Asked: Sep 2024

🤔 Try it in your head, then tap to see the answer
Issue
Who is liable when an agent signs without showing he signs as agent?
Law
S.27: an agent can bind his principal only if he signs in that capacity. S.28: an agent who signs without indicating that he signs as agent, and that he does not intend to incur personal liability, is personally liable, except to those who induced him to sign believing only the principal would be liable.
Apply
The note shows no words like "for and on behalf of the company". On the face of the instrument, the MD alone is the maker.
Conclusion
The MD is personally liable on the note; the company is not liable on the instrument. A may still sue the company on the loan itself if he proves the money was borrowed for and used by the company.

Problem 5. X executed a promissory note in favour of Z and then died. Can Z claim against X's legal heirs?

Asked: Feb 2024

🤔 Try it in your head, then tap to see the answer
Issue
Does liability on a note survive the maker's death?
Law
The maker's liability on the note passes to his estate. S.29 NI Act: a legal representative who himself signs an instrument is personally liable unless he limits it to the assets received. CPC S.50, S.52: a decree against legal representatives is executed only against the deceased's property in their hands.
Apply
The debt is a liability of X's estate. Z can sue the heirs as legal representatives, but they are not personally liable beyond what they inherited.
Conclusion
Yes. Z can recover from X's legal heirs, limited to the assets of X that came into their hands.

Problem 6. A note dated 31.07.2018 is payable "one month after date". (Variant: a note dated 1.2.2018 payable two months after date.) When is it payable?

Asked: Sep 2021 · Oct 2020

🤔 Try it in your head, then tap to see the answer
Issue
How is maturity computed?
Law
S.23: payable so many months after date, it falls due on the corresponding day of the month, or the last day if none. S.22: three days of grace. S.25: if the day is a public holiday, the preceding business day.
Apply
31.07.2018 + one month = 31.08.2018 (August has 31 days) + 3 days of grace = 3 September 2018. Variant: 1.2.2018 + two months = 1.4.2018 + 3 days = 4 April 2018.
Conclusion
The first note matures on 3 September 2018; the second on 4 April 2018. If that day is a public holiday under S.25, it is payable on the preceding business day.

Problem 7. A draws a bill of exchange in favour of B. Two days later the bill is missing. What is a bill, why does it matter to B, and what can B do?

Asked: Aug 2025 (Hons)

🤔 Try it in your head, then tap to see the answer
Issue
What are the rights of a holder whose bill is lost before maturity?
Law
S.5: bill of exchange. S.45A: a holder who loses a bill before it is overdue may ask the drawer for a duplicate of the same tenor, giving security (indemnity) if required. S.58: a finder gets no title.
Apply
The bill is B's right to receive money from the drawee; a finder cannot recover on it if it is an order bill. B should at once inform the drawee/acceptor not to pay, ask A for a duplicate under S.45A, and offer an indemnity.
Conclusion
B can obtain a duplicate bill from A under S.45A on giving security, and warn the drawee not to pay anyone else; if A refuses, B can sue on the original debt.
