Probation, Parole and Remission

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

Prison can turn a first-time offender into a hardened criminal: he goes in for a small mistake and comes out with "contacts". The Probation of Offenders Act, 1958 tries to keep beginners away from prison. For a minor first offence the court may simply warn him (admonition) or let him go home on a promise of good behaviour (probation), watched by a Probation Officer. As the Supreme Court said, "no one is a born criminal".

Parole and furlough are different: they are for people already serving a sentence. Parole is a short release for a reason (a death, illness, marriage in the family); furlough is a short release as a reward for good conduct over a long sentence. Remission cuts the sentence itself.

Probation of Offenders Act, 1958: salient features, grounds for release, powers and duties of probation officers

📖 The story: Jugal Kishore Prasad v. State of Bihar

(1972) 2 SCC 633 · decided 16 Aug 1972

Jugal Kishore Prasad was convicted of voluntarily causing grievous hurt with dangerous weapons, as a member of an unlawful assembly, and of rioting. The High Court reduced his main sentence to three years' rigorous imprisonment.

He said he was 19 at the time and claimed the benefit of Section 6 of the Probation of Offenders Act, which protects offenders under 21 from imprisonment.

❓ Can an offender under 21 claim the benefit of S.6 for an offence punishable with life imprisonment?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Meaning and object. Probation means releasing a convicted offender, without sending him to prison, on condition of good behaviour, under supervision. The Act aims to reform first and young offenders and prevent them from becoming hardened criminals through contact with jail.

Admonition (S.3). Where a person is found guilty of theft, theft in a dwelling house, theft by a clerk or servant, dishonest misappropriation of a deceased's property, cheating (now BNS 303, 305, 306, 315, 318), or any offence punishable with up to 2 years or fine, and no previous conviction is proved, the court may release him after due admonition (a formal warning), considering the circumstances, nature of the offence and his character.

Probation of good conduct (S.4). Where a person is found guilty of an offence not punishable with death or life imprisonment, the court may release him on a bond (with or without sureties) for up to 3 years to appear and receive sentence when called, and meanwhile keep the peace and be of good behaviour. The court considers the Probation Officer's report and may pass a supervision order (at least 1 year) with conditions (S.4(3)).

Offenders under 21 (S.6). A person under 21 found guilty of an offence punishable with imprisonment (but not life) shall not be sentenced to imprisonment unless the court, after calling for a Probation Officer's report, is satisfied that release under S.3 or S.4 is not desirable, and records its reasons.

💡 Case story Jugal Kishore Prasad: a young man of about 19 was convicted of causing grievous hurt with dangerous weapons, an offence punishable with life imprisonment, and asked for the benefit of S.6. The Supreme Court refused because the offence was punishable with life, but explained the Act's purpose: to "prevent the conversion of youthful offenders into obdurate criminals"; "no one is a born criminal".

Other features. S.5: the court may order compensation to the victim and costs. S.8: conditions can be varied. S.9: on breach of the bond, the court may issue a warrant and sentence him for the original offence, or impose a penalty. S.11: appellate and revisional courts can also grant probation. S.12: no disqualification attaches to the conviction. The Act prevails over S.401 BNSS where it is in force.

Probation officers (S.13–14). Appointed by the State or recognised societies. Duties: inquire into the offender's background and report to the court; supervise probationers; advise and assist them to find suitable employment; help them compensate the victim; and perform other prescribed duties. Their reports are confidential (S.7).

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

No. S.6 does not apply to offences punishable with life imprisonment, and grievous hurt with dangerous weapons is such an offence. The appeal was dismissed.

The Court explained the object of the Act in detail.

Why it matters:

  • The Act aims to "prevent the conversion of youthful offenders into obdurate criminals" through association with hardened criminals in jail.
  • Modern penology prefers reform to retribution; "no one is a born criminal".
  • The Act's protection is meant for young offenders who have not committed very serious offences.

Chapter 2 · asked 8× 🔥🔥🔥 Must do

Parole, furlough and remission: meaning, conditions, differences (S.473–475 BNSS)

📖 The story: Asfaq v. State of Rajasthan

(2017) 15 SCC 55 · decided 11 Sep 2017

Serial bomb blasts hit five trains on 6 December 1993. Asfaq was convicted under the anti-terror law (TADA) and sentenced to life imprisonment; the conviction became final.

In 2014 he applied for 20 days of regular parole. His application went back and forth between the parole committee, the State and the Union Government, and was rejected. The High Court said that in such a heinous crime parole cannot be claimed as of right.

He appealed to the Supreme Court.

❓ Can parole be refused only because the convict's crime was very serious? How do parole and furlough differ?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Parole. A temporary, conditional release of a convict for a specific reason, such as serious illness or death of a family member, marriage, or to maintain family ties. It is not in the BNSS; it is governed by State prison rules (e.g. the Telangana Prisons Rules) and executive orders. It is a concession, not a right.

Kinds and conditions. Custody parole (escorted, for a few hours, in emergencies) and regular parole (for a longer period, on an application). Conditions: good conduct in jail, surety or bond, reporting to the police station, not leaving the area, returning on time. Habitual offenders and those who may threaten public order can be refused.

Furlough. A short release, after a prisoner has served a minimum part of a long sentence, given periodically as a reward for good conduct and to break the monotony of prison life. No specific reason is needed.

💡 Case story Asfaq: a life convict in the 1993 serial train blasts case asked for 20 days' parole. The High Court refused because the crime was heinous. The Supreme Court said the nature of the crime alone cannot be a reason to deny parole, and explained the differences; but it upheld the refusal because police and officials reported real risks to witnesses and public peace.

Differences (table):

Reasonparole: a specific need · furlough: none; reward for good conduct
Sentenceparole: usually not counted · furlough: counted
Durationparole: short, as the need requires · furlough: fixed periods
Frequencyparole: when needed · furlough: periodic, after a minimum term

Remission and commutation. The appropriate Government may suspend or remit a sentence (S.473 BNSS) or commute it to a lighter one (S.474). But a life convict for an offence punishable with death must serve at least 14 years before release (S.475). The President (Art. 72) and Governor (Art. 161) have separate constitutional powers of pardon.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

The mere nature of the offence should not be the sole reason to deny parole; the authorities must examine whether the convict shows signs of reform and whether release endangers public interest.

On the facts, the refusal was upheld because officials reported real risks to witnesses, public peace and the convict's own life; he could apply again later.

Why it matters:

  • Parole: release for a specific reason, usually not counted in the sentence.
  • Furlough: periodic release for good conduct in a long sentence, counted in the sentence.
  • Maintaining family and social ties is a key reformative purpose of parole.
  • Prisoners keep their dignity and fundamental rights, subject to the limits of imprisonment.

  • Part CA serious offender seeks parole: decide on reform and public interest, not the crime alone.

Part C workshop · 10 marks each

Solve the problem like a judge

Every Part C problem is a new story with the same law. Spot the issue, state the law, apply it to the facts, and conclude. Quote the matching case story to lift your answer.

Problem 1. A killed his wife (by poison or a weapon) and pleads that it is his first offence and he should be released on probation. Decide.

Asked: 2013 · 2019 · Sep 2021 · Sep 2024 · Feb 2025

Issue
Can a first offender convicted of murder get probation?
Law
Murder: BNS 103, punishable with death or life imprisonment. S.4 Probation of Offenders Act applies only to offences not punishable with death or life; S.3 only to minor offences; S.6 only to offences not punishable with life Jugal Kishore Prasad.
Apply
Being a first offender does not change the punishment the law provides. Murder is excluded from every provision of the Act. (If he also hid the body: causing disappearance of evidence, BNS 238.)
Conclusion
No. Probation cannot be granted for murder, which is punishable with death or life imprisonment; he must be sentenced under BNS 103.

📖 Story to quote: Jugal Kishore Prasad

Problem 2. X, a professional gold smuggler aged 35, is convicted and asks for probation. Discuss.

Asked: May 2022

Issue
Is probation available to a habitual offender?
Law
S.4: discretionary, considering the circumstances, nature of the offence and the character of the offender. Probation is meant for first and young offenders capable of reform.
Apply
X is a professional offender with a long record; his character shows no promise of reform, and his age gives no S.6 protection.
Conclusion
Probation should be refused; the Act is for first and reformable offenders, not professional criminals.

Problem 3. Rajesh, from a respected family, was convicted of possessing an unlicensed gun and requests probation. Can it be granted?

Asked: Nov 2020

Issue
Does social status or a statutory minimum sentence affect probation?
Law
S.4: the court considers the nature of the offence and the offender's character, not his family status. The Arms Act, 1959 (S.25) prescribes a minimum sentence for such possession; courts have generally refused to use the 1958 Act to go below a minimum sentence fixed by a special law.
Apply
A respected family is not a ground. Illegal firearm possession endangers society, and the special law fixes a minimum term.
Conclusion
Probation is unlikely to be granted; family status is irrelevant, and the Arms Act's minimum sentence and the danger of the offence weigh against it.
