Banking, the Banking Regulation Act and RBI

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

A bank is a shop that deals in trust. People leave their savings with it because they trust they can take them back whenever they want; the bank lends that money to farmers, traders and home buyers. If even one big bank fails, panic spreads like fire. So the law places a strict referee over every bank: the Reserve Bank of India, armed with the Banking Regulation Act, 1949.

India's story moves from the village sahukar and hundi, to the British-era Presidency Banks, to the Imperial Bank and the State Bank of India, to the nationalisation of 1969 and 1980, and now to UPI on every phone. At each step, the aim was the same: safe deposits, credit for all, and public confidence.

Chapter 1 · asked 10× 🔥🔥🔥 Must do

Banking: meaning, banker, and the nature and evolution of banking in India

📖 The story: Rustom Cavasjee Cooper v. Union of India (Bank Nationalisation case)

(1970) 1 SCC 248 · AIR 1970 SC 564 · decided 10 Feb 1970

On 19 July 1969, the President issued an ordinance taking over 14 major banks, each with deposits of ₹50 crore or more; Parliament then passed the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1969.

Rustom Cavasjee Cooper, who held shares, accounts and directorships in some of these banks, challenged the law in the Supreme Court.

He argued that the compensation was illusory and that the law discriminated against the 14 banks by stopping them from carrying on banking at all.

❓ Was the 1969 nationalisation law valid under the Constitution?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Meaning (S.5(b) BR Act). "Banking" means accepting, for the purpose of lending or investment, deposits of money from the public, repayable on demand or otherwise, and withdrawable by cheque, draft, order or otherwise. Two tests: deposits from the public, and use of them for lending or investment.

Banker. S.3 NI Act: "banker" includes any person acting as a banker and any post office savings bank. A banking company is a company that transacts the business of banking in India (S.5(c) BR Act). Courts look at the real business, not the label.

Indigenous banking. Lending at interest is described in the Manusmriti and Kautilya's Arthashastra. Indigenous bankers (shroffs, sahukars, chettiars, multanis) accepted deposits and used hundis to send money across the country.

Presidency and Imperial banks. Bank of Hindustan (1770) was among the first European-style banks. The three Presidency Banks (Bengal 1806, Bombay 1840, Madras 1843) merged into the Imperial Bank of India in 1921. Indian-owned banks grew with the Swadeshi movement (e.g. Punjab National Bank, Bank of India, Canara Bank).

RBI and SBI. The RBI began work on 1 April 1935 under the RBI Act, 1934, and was nationalised in 1949. The Banking Companies Act, 1949 (renamed the Banking Regulation Act in 1966) gave RBI control over banks. The Imperial Bank became the State Bank of India in 1955.

Nationalisation. On 19 July 1969, 14 major banks were nationalised to take credit to agriculture, small industry and villages; the first law was struck down R.C. Cooper and re-enacted in 1970. Six more banks followed on 15 April 1980. Regional Rural Banks (1975) and NABARD (1982) took credit to the countryside.

Reforms and today. After 1991, the Narasimham Committees led to new private banks, prudential norms and recovery laws (DRT 1993, SARFAESI 2002). Payments banks and small finance banks came in 2015; public sector banks were merged (2019–2020). Technology (core banking, RTGS, NEFT, UPI) made banking available on the phone.

💡 Real-world example: under the Jan Dhan scheme (2014), crores of zero-balance accounts were opened, so that subsidies now reach people directly through their bank accounts. This is "social banking" at work.

Importance. Banks mobilise savings, create credit, finance trade and industry, help monetary policy reach the economy, enable payments, and support financial inclusion and government schemes.

🏛️ How the story ended

No. By majority, the Court held the Act void: the compensation was not fixed on relevant principles (for example, important assets were ignored), violating Article 31(2) as it then stood.

Barring the named banks from carrying on banking business in future was a hostile discrimination and an unreasonable restriction.

The Court also said fundamental rights must be judged by the effect of a law on the citizen, not merely its object. Parliament re-enacted nationalisation by the 1970 Act.

Why it matters:

  • Nationalisation of banks is a valid policy, but it must follow the Constitution.
  • The test of a law affecting fundamental rights is its direct effect.
  • This is why the Acquisition Act of 1970 (and of 1980) governs nationalised banks today.

Chapter 2 · asked 9× 🔥🔥🔥 Must do

Banking Regulation Act, 1949: salient features

📖 The story: The co-operative bank that failed

Depositors of an urban co-operative bank discover large hidden bad loans.

❓ What does the law say?

⚖️ The law behind the story

Here is the law that decides it:

Object and scope. A complete code to regulate banking companies, protect depositors and keep the banking system sound. It came into force in March 1949 as the Banking Companies Act, was renamed in 1966 and, through S.56, applies (with changes) to co-operative banks; the 2020 amendment brought them under closer RBI supervision.

Definitions. Banking (S.5(b)) and banking company (S.5(c)). Forms of business a bank may carry on (S.6): lending, discounting bills, guarantees, letters of credit, foreign exchange, safe deposit lockers, agency business and the like.

Licensing and branches. No company can carry on banking without an RBI licence (S.22); RBI may cancel it. New places of business need RBI permission (S.23). Only banking companies may use the words "bank", "banker" or "banking" in their name (S.7).

Business and prohibitions. A bank shall not trade in goods (S.8), except to realise a security; must dispose of immovable property not needed for its own use within seven years (S.9); cannot employ managing agents (S.10); at least a majority of directors must have special knowledge (S.10A); loans to its own directors are restricted (S.20).

Capital and reserves. Minimum paid-up capital and reserves (S.11); rules on share capital and a cap on the voting rights of any single shareholder (S.12); no charge on unpaid capital (S.14); dividends only after capitalised expenses are written off (S.15); 20% of profits to the reserve fund before dividend (S.17).

Liquidity and advances. Cash reserve for non-scheduled banks (S.18; scheduled banks under S.42 RBI Act); Statutory Liquidity Ratio in cash, gold or approved securities up to 40% of liabilities as RBI fixes (S.24); RBI may control advances: purpose, margins, interest (S.21).

RBI control. Inspection (S.35); binding directions in the public interest or depositors' interest (S.35A); directions to start insolvency proceedings against defaulters (S.35AA); removal of managerial staff (S.36AA); supersession of the board (S.36ACA); accounts and balance sheet (S.29) and penalties (S.46, S.47A).

Failure and depositors. RBI may seek a moratorium and a scheme of reconstruction or amalgamation (S.45), as in the rescue of Yes Bank (2020). Depositors may nominate up to four nominees for deposits, safe-custody articles and lockers (S.45ZA, S.45ZC, S.45ZE, as amended in 2025).

💡 Example: a chit-fund style company called "Sunrise Bank Finance" takes deposits from villagers. It breaks S.7 (use of "bank") and S.22 (no licence); RBI can act and the promoters face penalties.

🏛️ How the story ends

The BR Act is a complete code to regulate banks and protect depositors; through S.56 and the 2020 amendment, co-operative banks are under closer RBI supervision.

The RBI can step in with directions, inspection and supersession.

Chapter 3 · asked 9× 🔥🔥🔥 Must do

Kinds of banks and banking, and the role of technology

📖 The story: Scheduled or not?

A student asks why SBI and a big private bank get RBI refinance but a small local bank does not.

❓ What does the law say?

⚖️ The law behind the story

Here is the law that decides it:

Commercial banks. Accept deposits and give short and medium-term loans for profit. Kinds: public sector (SBI, nationalised banks), private sector, foreign banks and Regional Rural Banks. Scheduled banks are those in the Second Schedule to the RBI Act (S.42(6): adequate capital, affairs not harmful to depositors); they get RBI refinance and clearing facilities and keep CRR with RBI.

Co-operative banks. Owned by members, working on "one member, one vote": urban co-operative banks, state and district central co-operative banks, and primary agricultural credit societies. Registered under co-operative laws but regulated as banks by RBI (S.56 BR Act).

Development banks and NABARD. They give long-term finance for a sector: NABARD (set up 12 July 1982 under the NABARD Act, 1981) is the apex bank for agriculture and rural development: it refinances co-operative banks and RRBs, manages the Rural Infrastructure Development Fund and supervises RRBs and co-operative banks. Others: SIDBI (small industry), EXIM Bank (trade), NHB (housing).

New banks. Payments banks take small deposits but cannot lend; small finance banks serve small borrowers.

Types of banking. Retail (individuals) and wholesale/corporate; branch v. unit banking; mixed banking (deposit banking plus industrial finance); universal banking (banking, insurance, investment under one group); investment/merchant banking; social banking (priority sector lending, financial inclusion). Together they serve India's different needs: farmers, MSMEs, large industry, and the unbanked poor.

Technology. Core banking (any-branch banking), ATMs, internet and mobile banking, RTGS and NEFT for fund transfers, cheque truncation, and UPI (2016) for instant payments. Security: two-factor authentication, RBI rules limiting customer liability for unauthorised electronic transactions, and the IT Act, 2000. The NI Act recognises a cheque in electronic form and the image of a truncated cheque (S.6).

💡 Real-world example: a vegetable seller in a small town accepts payment by scanning a UPI QR code; the money moves from the buyer's bank to hers in seconds, without cash or cheques.

🏛️ How the story ends

Commercial banks may be public, private, foreign or RRBs. Scheduled banks are listed in the Second Schedule of the RBI Act and get RBI refinance and facilities.

The two big banks are scheduled; the small one is not.

Chapter 4 · asked 6× 🔥🔥 Important

RBI: functions, role in economic development and protection of bank customers (Ombudsman)

📖 The story: Internet and Mobile Association of India v. Reserve Bank of India

(2020) 10 SCC 274 · decided 4 Mar 2020

On 6 April 2018, RBI issued a circular directing all regulated entities, including banks, not to deal in virtual currencies or provide services to anyone dealing in them.

Crypto exchanges lost their bank accounts overnight. Their association, IAMAI, challenged the circular in the Supreme Court.

❓ Could RBI cut off banking services to virtual currency businesses?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Status. The RBI is India's central bank, set up under the RBI Act, 1934, working since 1 April 1935, owned by the Government since 1949.

Functions. (1) Issue of notes: sole right to issue bank notes (S.22 RBI Act). (2) Banker to the Government (S.20, S.21). (3) Bankers' bank and lender of last resort: holds banks' cash reserves, lends to them in a crisis. (4) Monetary policy: an inflation target set by the Government, rates fixed by the Monetary Policy Committee. (5) Regulator and supervisor of banks and NBFCs (BR Act, RBI Act). (6) Manager of foreign exchange (FEMA, 1999) and payment systems (Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007).

Role in economic development. Priority-sector lending to agriculture and small industry; setting up development institutions (IDBI, NABARD); financial inclusion; stable prices and a stable rupee, which encourage investment; and modern payment systems.

Customer protection. (1) RBI Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, 2026 (in force 1 July 2026; it replaced the 2021 Scheme, which had merged the Banking Ombudsman Scheme of 1995/2002/2006): complain first to the bank, which has 30 days to reply; then to the RBI Ombudsman within 90 days; the Ombudsman can award compensation for loss up to ₹30 lakh, plus up to ₹3 lakh for harassment; appeal lies within RBI. (2) Deposit insurance up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank (DICGC). (3) Zero liability for a customer who reports an unauthorised electronic transaction within three working days (RBI, 2017). (4) KYC rules, fair practice codes, the Charter of Customer Rights, and controls on recovery agents.

Banking Ombudsman (origin). The office was first created by RBI under S.35A BR Act through the Banking Ombudsman Scheme, 1995, revised in 2002 and 2006: a senior RBI officer for each area, receiving complaints of deficiency in banking service (delay in collecting cheques, refusal of deposits, charges without notice and the like), settling them by conciliation or passing an award. The courts too have pushed transparency: RBI must disclose inspection reports under the RTI Act, because it does not hold them in a fiduciary capacity (RBI v. Jayantilal N. Mistry, 2016).

🏛️ How the story ended

RBI has the power to regulate anything that can affect the payment and credit system, including virtual currencies.

But the measure was disproportionate: RBI had not shown any actual harm to the banks it regulated and had not considered less drastic options. The circular was set aside as an unreasonable restriction on the right to carry on business.

Why it matters:

  • RBI's regulatory powers (RBI Act, BR Act S.35A) are wide.
  • Even an expert regulator must act proportionately when it restricts a lawful business.
  • Courts give regulators room, but not unlimited room.

Part C workshop · 10 marks each

Solve the problem like a judge

Every Part C problem is a new story with the same law. Spot the issue, state the law, apply it to the facts, and conclude. Quote the matching case story to lift your answer.

Problem 1. "Sunrise Bank Finance Pvt. Ltd." collects deposits from villagers promising high interest. It has no RBI licence. Decide its legality.

Asked: Practice

Issue
Can a company without a licence use the word "bank" and accept public deposits?
Law
BR Act S.7: only a banking company may use "bank", "banker" or "banking" in its name. S.22: no company shall carry on banking business in India without an RBI licence. S.46: penalties. The Banning of Unregulated Deposit Schemes Act, 2019 also punishes unregulated deposit schemes.
Apply
Accepting public deposits for lending is "banking" under S.5(b). The company has no licence and misuses the word "bank".
Conclusion
Its business is illegal; RBI and the police can act, the directors face penalties, and depositors can claim their money back.

Problem 2. A bank's board decides to buy and sell rice and pulses to increase profits. Advise.

Asked: Practice

Issue
Can a banking company trade in goods?
Law
BR Act S.8: no banking company shall directly or indirectly deal in the buying, selling or bartering of goods, except in connection with the realisation of a security given to it, or in bills of exchange received for collection or negotiation.
Apply
Trading in rice is ordinary trade, not realisation of a security.
Conclusion
The decision is illegal under S.8. Only if the bank sells goods pledged by a defaulting borrower (realisation of security) is it permitted.

Problem 3. ₹40,000 is debited from a customer's account through an unauthorised card transaction. He reports it to the bank the next day. The bank refuses a refund. Advise.

Asked: Practice (Aug 2025: role of RBI)

Issue
Who bears the loss of an unauthorised electronic transaction and what is the remedy?
Law
RBI circular on limiting customer liability in unauthorised electronic banking transactions (2017): zero liability where the fraud is not the customer's fault and he reports within three working days. RBI Integrated Ombudsman Scheme, 2026.
Apply
He reported within one day and did not share his PIN or OTP. The bank must credit (shadow-reverse) the amount within ten working days.
Conclusion
The bank must refund the ₹40,000. If it refuses, he can complain to the bank, and after 30 days, to the RBI Ombudsman, who can award the loss plus compensation for harassment.
