Interim Protection

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

A suit can take years. Meanwhile the defendant may sell the land, pull down the house, empty the shop or run away. If the court did nothing until the final decree, the plaintiff might win a decree for something that no longer exists. Interim orders freeze the battlefield until the war is decided.

The court's tools, in order of strength: caveat (please hear me first), status quo (nobody move), temporary injunction (stop that act), receiver (the court takes charge of the property), commission (the court sends someone to look and measure), attachment before judgment (the property is locked till the decree), and arrest before judgment (for a defendant about to flee). Behind all of them stands S.151: the court's inherent power to do justice.

Chapter 1 · asked 10× 🔥🔥🔥 Must do

Temporary injunctions and interlocutory orders (O.XXXIX) · Status quo · Caveat (S.148A)

📖 The story: Mahadeo Savlaram Shelke v. Puna Municipal Corporation

Supreme Court · decided 23 Jan 1995

The Poona Municipal Corporation acquired a two-storeyed building at Kotwal Chowk to widen a road; it took possession in 1971.

The occupants stayed on under leave and licence agreements. After these expired, eviction orders were passed against them and were affirmed up to the Supreme Court.

The occupants then filed a civil suit for a perpetual injunction against being dispossessed, and asked for a temporary injunction. The trial court refused; the appellate judge granted it; the High Court restored the refusal. They appealed to the Supreme Court.

❓ Can a person whose eviction has been finally ordered get a temporary injunction to stay in possession?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Meaning. An injunction is a court order restraining a party from doing an act (or, if mandatory, directing him to do one). A temporary injunction lasts until the suit is decided or until further orders (S.94(c), O.XXXIX); a permanent injunction is granted by the decree (S.38, Specific Relief Act).

When (r.1, r.2). Where it is proved by affidavit or otherwise that (a) the property in dispute is in danger of being wasted, damaged or alienated by a party, or wrongfully sold in execution; (b) the defendant threatens to remove or dispose of his property to defraud creditors; (c) the defendant threatens to dispossess the plaintiff or otherwise cause injury to him in relation to the property in dispute; and (r.2) to restrain a breach of contract or other injury.

Three tests. (1) Prima facie case: a bona fide, substantial question that needs a trial; (2) balance of convenience: more harm to the applicant if refused than to the other side if granted; (3) irreparable injury: harm that money cannot adequately compensate (Dalpat Kumar v. Prahlad Singh, 1992).

💡 Case story Mahadeo Shelke: tenants evicted by a final order of the Pune corporation (affirmed up to the Supreme Court) filed a fresh suit for an injunction against being dispossessed. The Supreme Court refused it: possession after a final eviction order is unlawful, so there is no prima facie case, and neither balance of convenience nor irreparable injury favoured them; it even directed damages against them if the suit failed.

Ex parte injunction (r.3). Normally notice is given to the other side first. Without notice, only where delay would defeat the object; the court records reasons, and the applicant must at once send the other side copies of the application, affidavit and plaint and file an affidavit of compliance. r.3A: the court must try to decide the application within 30 days. Factors: serious mischief, conduct, delay, good faith, limited duration (Morgan Stanley Mutual Fund v. Kartick Das, 1994).

Other rules. r.2A: disobedience: attachment of property and civil prison up to 3 months. r.4: the order can be discharged, varied or set aside. r.5: an injunction against a corporation binds its officers. r.6–10: interlocutory orders: sale of perishable goods, detention, preservation, inspection, deposit of money. An order on a temporary injunction is appealable (O.XLIII r.1(r)).

Status quo. An order directing the parties to maintain the existing position (possession, construction, title) as on a date, until further orders. It is passed under O.XXXIX or S.151 when the court needs time to examine the matter.

Caveat (S.148A). A person who expects an application to be made against him in a suit may lodge a caveat, so that no order is passed without hearing him. He must serve notice of it on the expected applicant by registered post; the applicant must then serve the caveator with copies. A caveat is valid for 90 days.

Conclusion. Interim relief preserves the subject matter so that the final decree is not an empty paper.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

No. The Supreme Court dismissed the appeal with costs.

Applying the tests from Dalpat Kumar v. Prahlad Singh (1992), it held that their possession after the final eviction order was unlawful, so there was no prima facie case; neither balance of convenience nor irreparable injury favoured them. It also directed that, if the suit failed, the trial court should assess damages payable by them.

Why it matters:

  • Prima facie case: a substantial question raised bona fide, needing investigation and a decision on merits; but a prima facie case alone is not enough.
  • Balance of convenience: compare the harm to each side from granting or refusing.
  • Irreparable injury: material injury that cannot be adequately compensated by damages.
  • Injunction is an equitable, discretionary relief; it is not granted to protect unlawful possession against the true owner.

Chapter 2 · asked 1× 🔥 Good to know

Appointment of receiver (O.XL) and commissions (S.75, O.XXVI)

📖 The story: The disputed hotel

Two partners fight over a running hotel. Each accuses the other of taking cash from the counter.

❓ What does the law say?

⚖️ The law behind the story

Here is the law that decides it:

Receiver (O.XL r.1). Where it appears just and convenient, the court may appoint a receiver of any property, before or after the decree; remove any person from its possession or custody; commit it to the receiver; and give him powers to bring and defend suits, realise, manage, protect, preserve and improve the property, collect rents and profits, and execute documents. He is an officer of the court; his possession is the court's possession.

When used. Property in danger of waste; partnership or family business in dispute; rents and profits likely to be lost; mortgage suits. r.3: he gives security, submits accounts, pays the amounts due, and is personally liable for loss caused by wilful default or gross negligence. r.2: the court fixes his remuneration.

Commissions (S.75, O.XXVI). The court may issue a commission: (a) to examine a witness (sick, exempt under S.133, outside its jurisdiction: r.1–8); (b) for local investigation (e.g. measuring land, finding encroachment: r.9); (c) to examine or adjust accounts (r.11); (d) to make a partition (r.13); (e) for a scientific investigation (r.10A), a ministerial act (r.10B) or sale of movables (r.10C). The commissioner's report and evidence form part of the record.

💡 Example: B says A encroached 150 sq. yards while building. The court appoints an Advocate-Commissioner with a surveyor under O.XXVI r.9 to measure the plots and report, and meanwhile orders status quo on the construction.

Conclusion. A receiver protects the property; a commissioner brings the facts to the court.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ends

O.XL: where just and convenient, the court may appoint a receiver to manage the property, collect income and preserve it. He is an officer of the court.

A receiver runs the hotel and accounts for the money until the suit is decided.

Chapter 3 · asked 6× 🔥🔥 Important

Arrest and attachment before judgment (O.XXXVIII) · Compensation for wrongful orders (S.95)

📖 The story: Raman Tech. & Process Engg. Co. v. Solanki Traders

(2008) 2 SCC 302 · decided 20 Nov 2007

Solanki Traders sued Raman Tech before the Civil Judge (Junior Division), Medchal, near Hyderabad, to recover ₹99,200 for material supplied.

It applied under Order XXXVIII Rule 5 for an order directing the defendants to furnish security, or else for attachment of their property before judgment. The trial court dismissed the application, finding no prima facie case: the plaintiff had not even given details of the dishonoured post-dated cheques.

In revision, the High Court noted that the defendants were moving machinery out of the area and ordered them to furnish security for the suit amount within four weeks. The defendants appealed.

❓ When can a court order a defendant to furnish security or attach his property before judgment?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Arrest before judgment (r.1). In any suit other than a suit for immovable property under S.16(a)–(d), if the defendant, with intent to delay the plaintiff, avoid the court or obstruct execution, (i) has absconded or left the court's limits, (ii) is about to abscond, or (iii) has disposed of or removed property; or is about to leave India in circumstances likely to obstruct execution, the court may issue a warrant to bring him before it to show cause why he should not furnish security for his appearance. r.2–4: if he fails to give security, he may be committed to civil prison until the suit is decided (at most six months).

Attachment before judgment (r.5). If the defendant, with intent to obstruct or delay the execution of any decree, is about to dispose of the whole or part of his property, or remove it from the court's limits, the court may direct him to furnish security or produce the property, or show cause; it may also order conditional attachment. The plaintiff must specify the property and its value. Attachment without following r.5(1) is void (r.5(4)).

💡 Case story Raman Tech: a supplier sued at the Medchal court for ₹99,200 and sought security or attachment because the defendants were moving machinery. The High Court ordered security; the Supreme Court set it aside: attachment before judgment is a drastic power, not a device to convert an unsecured debt into a secured one; the plaintiff must show a prima facie case and an intent to defeat the decree, and shifting machinery to new premises is not enough.

Further rules. r.6: attachment if no cause is shown and no security given. r.8: claims of third parties to the attached property are decided as in execution. r.9: attachment withdrawn when security is given or the suit is dismissed. r.10: it does not affect the rights of strangers or prior encumbrances. r.11: attached property need not be re-attached in execution. r.12: no attachment of agricultural produce in an agriculturist's possession. S.95: if arrest, attachment or injunction was obtained on insufficient grounds, the court may award compensation up to ₹50,000 to the defendant.

Conclusion. These are strong weapons against a defendant planning to cheat the decree, so courts use them only on clear proof.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

The Supreme Court allowed the appeal, set aside the High Court's order and restored the trial court's dismissal.

The power under O.XXXVIII r.5 is drastic and extraordinary and must be used sparingly. The plaintiff must show a prima facie case and that the defendant is acting with intent to obstruct or delay execution by removing or disposing of property. Merely shifting a business or machinery to other premises is not enough.

Why it matters:

  • The object is to stop a defendant defeating a likely decree, not to convert an unsecured debt into a secured debt.
  • Courts must not use it as a lever to force a defendant to settle.
  • Specific allegations and material showing intent are required; vague apprehension is not enough.

🪝 "A suit is not a mortgage." Prove intent to defeat the decree.

Chapter 4 · asked 1× 🔥 Good to know

Inherent powers of civil courts (S.151) · Restitution (S.144)

📖 The story: The order obtained by fraud

A party gets an order by producing a forged document. The other side discovers the forgery months later. No specific rule covers recalling it.

❓ What does the law say?

⚖️ The law behind the story

Here is the law that decides it:

S.151. "Nothing in this Code shall be deemed to limit or otherwise affect the inherent power of the court to make such orders as may be necessary for the ends of justice or to prevent abuse of the process of the court." It is not a new power given by the Code; it is inherent in every court.

Limits. (1) It cannot be used where the Code expressly provides a remedy, or against its provisions; (2) it is procedural and cannot create substantive rights (Padam Sen v. State of U.P., 1961); (3) used with care, for reasons recorded. But the Code's provisions are not exhaustive: a court can grant an injunction in a case not covered by O.XXXIX (Manohar Lal Chopra v. Seth Hiralal, 1962).

Examples. Recalling an order obtained by fraud; consolidating suits; staying a suit outside S.10; restoring a proceeding; setting aside an order passed without notice; correcting the court's own mistake. Companion sections: S.148 (enlarge time, up to 30 days in total), S.152 (clerical or arithmetical errors), S.153 (amendment of proceedings).

Restitution (S.144). Where a decree or order is varied or reversed in appeal, revision or other proceeding, the court of first instance shall, on application, restore the parties to the position they would have occupied but for that decree, including refund with interest, damages and mesne profits. S.144(2): no separate suit for this. Principle: actus curiae neminem gravabit (an act of the court shall prejudice no one).

Conclusion. S.151 is the court's reserve power for justice where the Code is silent.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ends

S.151: the court's inherent power to make orders necessary for the ends of justice or to prevent abuse of process.

The court recalls its own order under S.151.

Part C workshop · 10 marks each

Solve the problem like a judge

Every Part C problem is a new story with the same law. Spot the issue, state the law, apply it to the facts, and conclude. Quote the matching case story to lift your answer.

Problem 1. Smt. Sudha sues Mr. Kondal for a perpetual injunction to stop him and his men entering her house. Pending the suit she wants a temporary injunction. Discuss.

Asked: Nov 2022

Issue
Is Sudha entitled to a temporary injunction pending the suit?
Law
O.XXXIX r.1(c) and r.2: injunction where the defendant threatens to dispossess or injure the plaintiff in relation to the property. Three tests: prima facie case, balance of convenience, irreparable injury Mahadeo Shelke. r.3: ex parte only if delay defeats the object.
Apply
If she shows title or settled possession (documents, tax receipts), the threat of entry, that her family will suffer more than Kondal if refused, and that forcible entry cannot be compensated in money, all three tests are met.
Conclusion
Yes, she can get a temporary injunction under O.XXXIX r.1–2 restraining Kondal till the suit is decided; ex parte if the threat is immediate, with r.3 compliance.

📖 Story to quote: Mahadeo Shelke

Problem 2. P sues D for dissolution of partnership and accounts. D is hurriedly shifting the firm's goods and assets. What can P do?

Asked: Aug 2023

Issue
What interim protection is available against a defendant removing assets?
Law
O.XXXVIII r.5: security or attachment before judgment if D, with intent to obstruct execution, is about to remove property Raman Tech. O.XXXIX r.1(a)–(b): injunction against wasting or removing property. O.XL r.1: receiver.
Apply
The assets belong to the partnership, which is the subject matter of the suit; D's hurried removal shows intent to defeat P's share.
Conclusion
P can seek an injunction restraining removal, the appointment of a receiver for the firm's assets, and security or attachment before judgment under O.XXXVIII r.5.

📖 Story to quote: Raman Tech

Problem 3. X wants the defendant arrested before judgment in a suit for recovery of immovable property. Can he?

Asked: 2018 · Feb 2025

Issue
Is arrest before judgment available in a suit for immovable property?
Law
O.XXXVIII r.1: arrest before judgment lies in any suit "other than a suit of the nature referred to in section 16, clauses (a) to (d)", i.e. suits for recovery, partition, mortgage or other rights in immovable property.
Apply
X's suit is for recovery of immovable property, which falls in S.16(a). The land cannot run away.
Conclusion
No. Arrest before judgment is barred in such suits; X can seek a temporary injunction or a receiver instead.

Problem 4. A agreed to sell his property to B and took a good advance. Prices rose; A refuses to register unless B pays the current market price. B's remedy?

Asked: 2014

Issue
What remedy and immediate protection does the buyer have?
Law
S.10, Specific Relief Act: suit for specific performance. O.XXXIX r.1(a): injunction against alienating the property pending the suit. S.52 TPA: lis pendens binds a transferee pending the suit.
Apply
B has paid an advance and is ready and willing to pay the agreed price; A is trying to change the bargain and may sell to someone else.
Conclusion
B should sue for specific performance and immediately seek a temporary injunction restraining A from alienating the property; any sale during the suit is subject to its result (lis pendens).

Problem 5. A, while building on his plot, encroaches 150 sq. yards of B's adjacent plot and refuses to stop. B's remedy and immediate step?

Asked: 2014

Issue
How to stop ongoing construction on an encroached portion.
Law
Suit for declaration, mandatory injunction (removal) and permanent injunction (S.38–39 SRA). O.XXXIX r.1(c), r.2: temporary injunction. O.XXVI r.9: commission for local investigation.
Apply
Construction, once completed, creates hardship and more litigation, so it must be stopped at once; the boundary dispute needs measurement.
Conclusion
B should sue for mandatory and permanent injunction, and immediately seek an ex parte temporary injunction (or status quo) stopping construction, with an Advocate-Commissioner to measure the plots.

Problem 6. B lodged a caveat. The plaintiff obtained an ex parte temporary injunction without notice to B. Valid?

Asked: (Part A "Caveat": 2017 · Nov 2020)

Issue
Can an order be passed without hearing a caveator?
Law
S.148A: where a caveat has been lodged, the applicant must serve notice of his application on the caveator, and the court must give notice to the caveator before passing an order; a caveat is valid for 90 days.
Apply
The caveat was in force, and B was not heard.
Conclusion
The order is liable to be set aside; B can apply to vacate it (O.XXXIX r.4) and it should be heard afresh with notice to him.

Problem 7. The defendant disobeys a temporary injunction and continues construction. What can the plaintiff do?

Asked: (Part A "Injunctions": Nov 2020 · Feb 2024)

Issue
What are the consequences of disobeying an injunction?
Law
O.XXXIX r.2A: the court may order attachment of the property of the person guilty of disobedience and detention in civil prison for up to 3 months; the attached property may be sold if the disobedience continues for a year. The court can also order the construction to be demolished to restore the position.
Apply
Construction in breach of an injunction is a clear violation.
Conclusion
Apply under O.XXXIX r.2A for attachment and civil prison, and for restoration of the original position.
