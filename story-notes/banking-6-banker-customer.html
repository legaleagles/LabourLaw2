Banker and Customer

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

When Lakshmi deposits ₹1 lakh in a bank, she feels she has kept her money "in safe custody". In law, she has lent it to the bank. The bank becomes her debtor and she its creditor; the bank may use the money as it likes, so long as it repays her on demand.

But the relationship has many hats. When the bank collects her cheque, it is her agent; when it keeps her jewellery in safe custody, it is a bailee; when it rents her a locker, it is a lessor; and it must always keep her affairs secret and deal with her in good faith. One relationship, many roles.

Chapter 1 · asked 11× 🔥🔥🔥 Must do

Banker and customer: meaning and the general (debtor-creditor) relationship

📖 The story: Foley v. Hill

(1848) 2 HLC 28 · 9 ER 1002 · House of Lords

Foley deposited a sum of money with the banking firm of Hill and others in 1829, on terms that the bankers would pay him interest at 3%.

No interest was ever credited. More than six years later, Foley filed a bill in the Court of Chancery for an account, arguing that the bankers held his money as trustees or agents, so the ordinary time limit for debts did not apply.

❓ Is a banker a trustee or agent of the money deposited by a customer?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Banker. One who carries on the business of banking: accepting deposits from the public for lending or investment, repayable on demand and withdrawable by cheque (S.5(b) BR Act; S.3 NI Act).

Customer. Not defined by statute. A customer is a person who has an account with the bank, or for whom the bank has agreed to open one, and whose dealings are of a banking nature. Duration does not matter: one becomes a customer when the account is opened (Commissioners of Taxation v. English, Scottish and Australian Bank, 1920). A person who merely cashes cheques at the counter without an account is not a customer (Great Western Railway v. London and County Banking Co., 1901).

Debtor and creditor. When a customer deposits money, the bank becomes his debtor and he its creditor. The money becomes the bank's property; it can use it as it likes and need not tell the customer how; it only promises to repay an equal sum Foley v. Hill.

💡 Example: Lakshmi deposits ₹1 lakh in a savings account. The bank lends it to a trader the same day. Lakshmi cannot object; she can only demand ₹1 lakh (plus interest) back.

Special features of this debt. (1) The customer must make a demand before the bank is bound to repay; the demand must be at the branch where the account is kept (Joachimson v. Swiss Bank Corporation, 1921). (2) So limitation runs from the date of demand (Art. 22 Limitation Act), not from the deposit. (3) Demand must be in the proper form (cheque, withdrawal slip). (4) The bank is not a trustee of the money.

Roles reverse. When the customer borrows or overdraws, the bank becomes the creditor and the customer the debtor, usually secured by pledge, mortgage or hypothecation, with the bank's rights of lien and set-off.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

No. Money paid into a bank ceases to be the customer's money; it becomes the banker's, who may use it as his own.

The banker is a debtor bound to repay an equal sum when asked. The customer had an ordinary claim at law, not a right to an account in equity.

Why it matters:

  • Debtor-creditor is the basic relationship between banker and customer.
  • The bank need not tell depositors how it uses their money.
  • A trust arises only where money is given for a specific purpose.

Chapter 2 · asked 10× 🔥🔥🔥 Must do

Special relationships, and the rights and duties of a banker

📖 The story: Money for a specific purpose

A customer hands the bank ₹50,000 with written instructions to pay it to his supplier. Before paying, the bank wants to adjust it against the customer's overdue loan.

❓ What does the law say?

⚖️ The law behind the story

Here is the law that decides it:

Trustee. When money or securities are given to the bank for a specific purpose (e.g. "pay this to my supplier", "buy shares for me"), the bank holds them in trust until the purpose is carried out; it cannot treat them as its own or set them off.

Agent. When the bank collects cheques, dividends or bills, pays insurance premia under standing instructions, or buys and sells securities for the customer, it is his agent and owes him an agent's duties.

Bailee. Valuables (jewellery, documents) left for safe custody make the bank a bailee (S.148 Contract Act); it must take the care a prudent man takes of his own goods (S.151) and has no lien on them.

Lessor (locker). A locker is rented: the bank does not know the contents. Under RBI locker rules (2022), its liability for loss due to fire, theft, building collapse or fraud by its employees is up to 100 times the annual rent.

Fiduciary. The relationship rests on trust and good faith: secrecy, honest advice, no misuse of confidential information. Where a customer relies on the bank's advice, the bank must not take unfair advantage (Lloyds Bank v. Bundy, 1975: an elderly farmer's guarantee and mortgage given on the bank manager's advice were set aside).

Guarantor. The bank issues bank guarantees and letters of credit on the customer's behalf; the customer counter-indemnifies it.

Rights of banker. General lien (S.171 Contract Act); set-off; appropriation of payments; to charge interest, commission and service charges; to close an account by reasonable notice.

Duties of banker. To honour cheques (S.31 NI Act); to keep secrecy; to collect cheques with diligence; to follow the customer's mandate and instructions; to keep proper accounts; to give notice before closing an account; KYC and anti-money-laundering duties; fair dealing (RBI codes).

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ends

Money given for a specific purpose is held in trust; the bank cannot treat it as its own or set it off.

It must pay the supplier.

Chapter 3 · asked 3× 🔥 Good to know

Secrecy of customer accounts: the duty and its exceptions

📖 The story: Tournier v. National Provincial and Union Bank of England

[1924] 1 KB 461 · Court of Appeal

Tournier, a bank customer, had an overdraft and agreed to repay it by instalments. He fell behind.

The branch manager telephoned Tournier's employers, ostensibly to find his address, and told them about the overdraft. He also said that Tournier appeared to be dealing with bookmakers, which the bank had learnt from a cheque it handled.

His employers did not renew his contract. Tournier sued the bank for breach of its duty of secrecy.

❓ Does a banker owe a legal duty to keep a customer's affairs secret, and when may he disclose them?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

The duty. A banker must not disclose the state of a customer's account or his dealings. It is a legal duty arising from the contract, continuing even after the account is closed Tournier. Statutes add oaths of secrecy for public sector banks (SBI Act S.44; Banking Companies (Acquisition) Act, 1970, S.13). Breach makes the bank liable for damages.

Exception 1: compulsion of law. Court orders and summons (BNSS S.94; CPC); certified copies under the Bankers' Books Evidence Act, 1891; income-tax (S.131, S.133(6) IT Act); PMLA reporting; RBI returns; credit information companies.

Exception 2: duty to the public. Where there is a danger to the State or public (e.g. a customer financing the enemy or terror), the bank may disclose.

Exception 3: bank's own interest. E.g. when the bank sues the customer for an overdraft, or a guarantor must be told the amount due.

Exception 4: consent. Express (customer asks the bank to give a reference) or implied (bankers' opinions on creditworthiness in general terms).

Privacy today. The Supreme Court has held that a customer's records in a bank are part of his right to privacy; a law letting officials inspect bank records without safeguards is invalid Canara Bank (privacy). Privacy is now a fundamental right (K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, 2017), and the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 adds data duties.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

Yes. The duty of secrecy is an implied term of the contract between banker and customer; it covers the account and information learnt through the banking relationship, and continues after the account is closed.

It is not absolute. Disclosure is allowed (1) under compulsion of law, (2) where there is a duty to the public, (3) where the bank's own interests require it, and (4) with the customer's express or implied consent. A new trial was ordered.

Why it matters:

  • The four Tournier exceptions are applied by Indian courts and textbooks.
  • Information learnt from other customers' transactions is also covered.
  • Breach gives the customer a claim for damages.

📚 More stories on this topic

District Registrar and Collector, Hyderabad v. Canara Bank: A State law let any person authorised by the Collector enter a bank and inspect and impound customers' documents to check stamp duty. Held: The provision was struck down: customers' records with a bank are part of their privacy, and the power had no safeguards. 🪝 Bank records are private

Part C workshop · 10 marks each

Solve the problem like a judge

Every Part C problem is a new story with the same law. Spot the issue, state the law, apply it to the facts, and conclude. Quote the matching case story to lift your answer.

Problem 1. Mr P, a customer of Banban Bank, demands that the bank show how his deposit is being used. He says he is the principal and the bank his agent. Can he compel it?

Asked: Jan 2025

Issue
Is a bank the agent or trustee of its depositor?
Law
The relationship is debtor and creditor: deposited money becomes the bank's money; it need not account for its use Foley v. Hill. The bank is an agent only for specific services such as collection.
Apply
P made an ordinary deposit, not a deposit for a specific purpose. He is a creditor, entitled to repayment on demand with interest.
Conclusion
No. The bank is not his agent for the deposit and need not show how the money is used; P can only demand repayment. (Public information about the bank comes through its published accounts and RBI disclosure rules.)

📖 Story to quote: Foley v. Hill

Problem 2. An income-tax officer writes directly to a bank asking for details of a customer's account. Advise the bank.

Asked: 2014 · 2015

Issue
Can the bank disclose without breaching secrecy?
Law
Exception of compulsion of law Tournier. S.133(6) IT Act: the assessing officer (and certain higher authorities) may require any person, including a banking company, to furnish information relevant to an enquiry or proceeding. S.131: powers of a civil court to summon documents.
Apply
If the letter is a written requisition under the proper section, by an authorised officer, the bank must comply; disclosure is then not a breach. A mere informal request is not enough.
Conclusion
The bank should verify that the request is a lawful written notice under the IT Act from an authorised officer, then disclose only the information asked for. Without such authority, it must refuse.

📖 Story to quote: Tournier

Problem 3. X and Y have a joint savings account. Y informs the bank that X has died, produces the death certificate and asks for the whole balance. What would you do as banker?

Asked: Aug 2023

Issue
Who is entitled to the balance on the death of one joint holder?
Law
The account mandate decides. "Either or survivor" / "former or survivor": the survivor is paid. Without a survivorship clause: the survivor and the legal heirs of the deceased jointly. A nominee under S.45ZA comes in only after all joint holders die. Payment to the survivor or nominee discharges the bank but does not decide ownership among the heirs (Ram Chander Talwar v. Devender Kumar Talwar, 2010).
Apply
Verify the death certificate and the mandate. If it is "either or survivor", pay Y. If the mandate is "jointly" with no survivorship, pay Y together with X's legal heirs (or as per RBI's simplified claim procedure).
Conclusion
With a survivorship mandate, pay the balance to Y; otherwise pay Y and X's legal heirs jointly. Either way, the bank should record the death and stop operations until the claim is settled.

Problem 4. X and Y hire a locker jointly, to be operated jointly. They have nominated S and W. X dies. Advise the bank.

Asked: Nov 2022 · May 2023

Issue
Who gets access to a jointly hired locker on the death of one hirer?
Law
S.45ZE(2) BR Act: where a locker is hired jointly and the hirers have made a nomination, on the death of a hirer the bank may give access and allow removal of contents to the nominee(s) jointly with the surviving hirer(s). S.45ZE(4): an inventory is prepared and signed; S.45ZE(5): the bank's liability then ends.
Apply
X has died. The bank should give access to Y together with the nominee(s), after verifying the death certificate and identities.
Conclusion
The bank should allow Y and the nominee(s) jointly to open the locker, prepare and get signed an inventory of the contents, and hand them over; it is then discharged. Without a nomination, Y and X's legal heirs would have to act jointly.
