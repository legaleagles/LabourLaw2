Conciliation and Mediation

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

A cement company and its supplier fall out over late deliveries. Neither wants a long fight; they still need each other. One sends the other a written invitation to conciliate; the other accepts. A retired judge, the conciliator, meets them together and separately, listens, and finally suggests: "Supplier gives a 5% discount; company drops the penalty." Both agree and sign. That signed settlement agreement now has the same force as an arbitral award and can be enforced like a court decree.

Mediation is the same journey with a lighter hand: the mediator helps the parties talk and find their own answer. In family disputes, a skilled mediator can save a marriage, or at least make the parting peaceful and protect the children.

Conciliation: meaning, features and the procedure for settlement of disputes (S.61–81)

⚡ 30-second version · Haresh Dayaram Thakur

📍A retired judge acting as conciliator in a flat dispute between two brothers wrote out the settlement himself, without the parties signing it; the High Court enforced it.
❓Can a conciliator's own settlement, not signed by the parties, bind them?
🏛️Conciliation must follow Part III strictly: only a settlement agreement drawn up and signed by the parties (and authenticated by the conciliator) binds. The order was set aside.

🪝 No signature, no settlement

📖 Read the full story 3 short paragraphs

Haresh Dayaram Thakur v. State of Maharashtra · (2000) 6 SCC 179 · decided 5 May 2000

Two brothers were in a dispute over the ownership and possession of a flat in Mumbai; the State housing authority was also a party.

The matter was sent to conciliation before a retired High Court judge. The conciliator recorded terms of settlement on his own, without a written agreement signed by the parties.

The High Court accepted the conciliator's report as a binding settlement. One brother appealed to the Supreme Court.

⚖️ The law you must know Part A lines

Meaning

Conciliation is a process in which a neutral third person (conciliator) assists the parties to reach an amicable settlement and may himself propose terms. Part III of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 (S.61–81), based on the UNCITRAL Conciliation Rules, 1980, governs it. It applies to disputes arising out of any legal relationship, contractual or not (S.61).

Features

Voluntary; non-binding until the parties sign; informal (not bound by CPC or the Evidence Act: S.66); confidential (S.75); the conciliator can propose a settlement (S.67(4)); the result has the force of an arbitral award (S.74).

Commencement (S.62)

One party sends a written invitation to conciliate, briefly identifying the dispute. Proceedings begin when the other party accepts in writing. If he rejects it, or does not reply within 30 days, the invitation is treated as rejected.

Settlement (S.73, S.74)

When elements of a settlement acceptable to the parties appear, the conciliator formulates terms and gives them to the parties; after their observations he may reformulate. If they agree, they draw up and sign a written settlement agreement, which the conciliator authenticates; it is final and binding on them (S.73). It has the same status and effect as an arbitral award on agreed terms under S.30 (S.74), so it can be enforced as a decree.

➕ More for Part B and examples 4 more points
Conciliators (S.63, S.64)

One conciliator unless the parties agree on two or three (who act jointly). The parties appoint them, or may ask an institution or person to recommend or appoint; independence and impartiality must be kept in mind.

Proceedings (S.65–72)

Each party submits a brief written statement and then further statements and documents (S.65); the conciliator may meet them together or separately (S.69) and may share factual information received from one with the other, unless asked to keep it confidential (S.70); the parties cooperate in good faith (S.71) and may themselves suggest terms (S.72).

Termination (S.76)

Proceedings end on: signing of the settlement agreement; a written declaration by the conciliator that further efforts are not justified; a written declaration by the parties, or by one party, that the proceedings are terminated.

Safeguards

Confidentiality of all matters (S.75); no arbitral or judicial proceedings during conciliation, except to preserve rights (S.77); the conciliator shall not later act as arbitrator, representative or counsel, or be a witness, in the same dispute (S.80); views, admissions and proposals made in conciliation cannot be relied on later (S.81). Note: the Mediation Act, 2023 proposes to bring conciliation under its scheme once its remaining provisions are notified; until then Part III applies.

🏛️ Why the court decided so

No. Under Part III of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, a settlement binds only when the parties themselves draw up and sign a written settlement agreement, authenticated by the conciliator (S.73, S.74).

A conciliator facilitates; he has no power to decide. The High Court's order was set aside and the dispute sent back to be decided on merits.

  • The parties' consent, in writing and signed, is the heart of conciliation.
  • The conciliator may propose, but not impose.
  • Strict compliance with S.61–81 is required.

Chapter 2 · asked 5× 🔥🔥 Important

Role, powers and duties of a conciliator

🔁 Flashback: Haresh Dayaram Thakur v. State of Maharashtra. A retired judge acting as conciliator in a flat dispute between two brothers wrote out the settlement himself, without the parties signing it; the High Court enforced it. Held: Conciliation must follow Part III strictly: only a settlement agreement drawn up and signed by the parties (and authenticated by the conciliator) binds. The order was set aside. 🪝 No signature, no settlement

⚡ The answer in one glance

1Assist impartially (S.67(1))2Guiding principles (S.67(2))3Manner of conduct (S.67(3))4Proposals (S.67(4))5Communication and disclosure (S.69, S.70)6Limits on the conciliator

🧠 "All Good Men Pray, Chant, Listen": Assist · Guiding principles · Manner · Proposals · Communication · Limits.

⚖️ The law you must know Part A lines

Assist impartially (S.67(1))

The conciliator shall assist the parties in an independent and impartial manner in their attempt to reach an amicable settlement.

Guiding principles (S.67(2))

He is guided by objectivity, fairness and justice, considering the rights and obligations of the parties, the usages of the trade concerned, and the circumstances of the dispute, including previous business practices between them.

Proposals (S.67(4))

He may, at any stage, make proposals for settlement; they need not be in writing or give reasons. This is what separates a conciliator from a mediator. Under S.73 he formulates and reformulates the terms of settlement.

➕ More for Part B and examples 4 more points
Manner of conduct (S.67(3))

He may conduct the proceedings as he considers appropriate, keeping in view the circumstances, the wishes of the parties (including a request to hear oral statements) and the need for a speedy settlement. He is not bound by the CPC or the Evidence Act (S.66), and may fix meetings and the place (S.69).

Communication and disclosure (S.69, S.70)

He may communicate with the parties jointly or separately. Factual information received from one party is disclosed to the other so that it can respond, unless given on condition of confidence. Administrative assistance may be arranged (S.68).

💡 ExampleExample: in a builder-buyer dispute, the conciliator meets the buyer separately and learns that what he really wants is quick possession, not money. He then proposes: "possession in 90 days, penalty waived". Both accept and sign.
Limits on the conciliator

He cannot impose a decision Haresh Dayaram Thakur; must keep everything confidential (S.75); cannot act as arbitrator, representative or counsel, or be a witness, in later proceedings on the same dispute (S.80); costs are fixed on termination (S.78).

Mediation and family mediation; the Mediation Act, 2023

⚡ 30-second version · K. Srinivas Rao

📍A marriage broke down within two days of the wedding; more than ten years of cases followed between the spouses.
❓Should the marriage be dissolved, and how can courts prevent such long matrimonial litigation?
🏛️The marriage was dissolved, and the Court directed Family Courts and criminal courts to refer suitable matrimonial disputes to mediation, with pre-litigation desks at mediation centres.

🪝 Mediate before you litigate

📖 Read the full story 3 short paragraphs

K. Srinivas Rao v. D.A. Deepa · (2013) 5 SCC 226 · decided 22 Feb 2013

The couple married in April 1999. Quarrels between the two families broke out at once, and they separated within two days without living together.

Years of litigation followed: complaints, criminal cases, a petition for divorce by the husband and for restitution by the wife. The Family Court granted divorce; the High Court reversed it.

The Supreme Court found that the wife's repeated complaints and cases had caused mental cruelty and that the marriage had broken down beyond repair.

⚖️ The law you must know Part A lines

Meaning

Mediation is a voluntary, confidential process in which a neutral mediator helps the parties communicate and reach their own settlement; he facilitates, but does not decide. Forms: court-annexed (S.89 CPC), private, pre-litigation, community and online mediation.

Process
  1. Introduction: the mediator explains the process and ground rules;
  2. joint session: each side tells its story;
  3. separate sessions (caucus): interests and options explored privately;
  4. negotiation of options;
  5. settlement written and signed, or closure if none.
Family mediation

Matrimonial, custody, maintenance and property disputes within families are ideal for mediation: the parties have a continuing relationship (as parents), and court battles harm children. Family Courts must try settlement (S.9, Family Courts Act); courts refer couples to mediation centres. The Supreme Court directed Family Courts and criminal courts (in cruelty cases) to refer suitable matrimonial disputes to mediation and asked mediation centres to open pre-litigation desks K. Srinivas Rao. Success is high where the issues are custody and maintenance; divorce status itself is decided by the court.

➕ More for Part B and examples 4 more points
Mediator's role

Neutral and impartial; builds trust; listens actively; reframes issues; separates positions from interests; generates options; keeps confidences; does not give legal advice or impose solutions.

💡 ExampleExample: a separated couple fights over their 6-year-old son. In mediation, they agree on a parenting plan: weekdays with the mother, weekends with the father, school fees shared. The Family Court records it in a decree, and the child is spared a bitter trial.
Mediation Act, 2023

India's first law on mediation (assent 14 September 2023). It provides for voluntary pre-litigation mediation, completion within 120 days (extendable by up to 60 days), a mediated settlement agreement enforceable like a court judgment and challengeable only on limited grounds (fraud, corruption, impersonation, non-mediable dispute) within 90 days, online and community mediation, and the Mediation Council of India. Only some provisions (including the Mediation Council) are in force so far (from 9 October 2023); the rest await notification.

Mediation v. conciliation

A conciliator may propose terms (S.67(4)); a mediator usually does not. Conciliation rests on the 1996 Act (Part III); mediation on S.89 CPC, court rules and the Mediation Act, 2023. In practice the words are often used interchangeably.

🏛️ Why the court decided so

The marriage was dissolved on the ground of mental cruelty, and permanent alimony was ordered.

Family Courts should first try mediation in matrimonial disputes; criminal courts dealing with cruelty complaints may refer suitable cases to mediation; and mediation centres should set up pre-litigation desks so disputes can be settled before cases are filed.

  • Mediation is the preferred first step in matrimonial disputes.
  • Pre-litigation mediation can save years of bitter cases.
  • Directions are now part of the practice of Family Courts across India.

One-line recall: what must you remember?

Mediation is "help them talk"; conciliation is "help them talk, and suggest"; arbitration is "hear them, then decide".
