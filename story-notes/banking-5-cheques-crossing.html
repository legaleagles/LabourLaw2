Cheques, Crossing and Drafts

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

An open cheque is like cash in an envelope: whoever holds it can walk to the counter and take the money. Draw two parallel lines across its corner and it becomes a cheque that can be paid only into a bank account. Now if it is stolen, the money leaves a trail, because it must pass through some bank account.

Add the words "Account Payee" and only the payee's own account can receive it. Add "Not Negotiable" and nobody who takes it can get a better title than the person who gave it to him. These small marks are the cheque's seat belts.

Chapter 1 · asked 6× 🔥🔥 Important

Cheque: definition, essentials, kinds and how it differs from a bill of exchange

📖 The story: The cheque on your phone

A shop deposits a customer's cheque at its bank, which sends only a scanned image for clearing; the paper stays at the branch.

❓ What does the law say?

⚖️ The law behind the story

Here is the law that decides it:

Definition (S.6). A cheque is a bill of exchange drawn on a specified banker and not expressed to be payable otherwise than on demand; it includes the electronic image of a truncated cheque and a cheque in electronic form.

Essentials. (1) In writing; (2) an unconditional order; (3) drawn on a specified banker; (4) payable on demand; (5) signed by the drawer; (6) a certain sum of money; (7) a certain payee (or bearer); (8) dated (an undated cheque is incomplete). No stamp is needed.

Kinds. Bearer (anyone holding it; transfer by delivery), order (payee or his order; transfer by indorsement), crossed and open, ante-dated and post-dated, stale (older than three months), mutilated, self cheque, banker's cheque / pay order, electronic and truncated cheques (cheque truncation system clears the image, not the paper).

Bill v. cheque. (1) A cheque is always drawn on a banker; a bill on anyone. (2) A cheque is payable on demand only; a bill may be payable after a period. (3) A cheque needs no acceptance. (4) No days of grace for a cheque. (5) A cheque can be crossed; a bill cannot. (6) A cheque needs no stamp. (7) The drawer of a cheque can stop payment; an accepted bill cannot be countermanded. (8) Delay in presenting a cheque discharges the drawer only to the extent of his actual loss (S.84); delay in presenting a bill discharges the drawer fully.

💡 Example: Sita gives Gopal a cheque "Pay Gopal or order ₹10,000" on her SBI account. SBI (drawee) must pay Gopal on demand if Sita has funds; if SBI wrongly refuses, it must compensate Sita (S.31), and Gopal can sue Sita (S.30).

Liability of the drawee (S.31). The drawee bank, having sufficient funds of the drawer properly applicable, must pay the cheque when duly required and, in default, compensate the drawer. The bank owes no duty to the payee or holder (no privity), who must sue the drawer.

🏛️ How the story ends

S.6: a cheque is a bill drawn on a specified banker, payable on demand, and includes the electronic image of a truncated cheque.

The image is cleared as the cheque itself.

Chapter 2 · asked 12× 🔥🔥🔥 Must do

Crossing of cheques: meaning, objects, kinds, who may cross, and "account payee"

📖 The story: Two lines across the top

A father sends his son's college fee by a cheque with two parallel lines across its corner.

❓ What does the law say?

⚖️ The law behind the story

Here is the law that decides it:

Meaning. Crossing is two parallel transverse lines on the face of a cheque, with or without words, which direct the paying banker to pay the amount only to a banker, not over the counter.

Objects. (1) Safety: a thief or finder cannot take cash at the counter; (2) traceability: the money goes into an account, so the receiver can be traced; (3) protection of the true owner and of honest bankers (S.128, S.131).

General crossing (S.123). Two parallel lines, with or without the words "and company" or any abbreviation, and with or without "not negotiable". Effect: paid only to a banker (S.126).

Special crossing (S.124). The name of a banker is added (lines are not essential). Effect: paid only to the banker named, or his agent for collection (S.126). A cheque crossed specially to two banks is refused unless one is the other's agent (S.127).

"Not negotiable" crossing (S.130). The cheque can still be transferred, but the transferee cannot get or give a better title than his transferor. If it was stolen, even an honest buyer gets no title.

"Account payee" crossing. Not in the Act; it is a banking practice backed by RBI directions: the collecting banker must credit the proceeds only to the payee's account. Collecting it for anyone else is negligence and costs the bank its S.131 protection. Tax law requires account payee cheques or electronic transfer for large loans and deposits.

💡 Example: a cheque crossed "A/c payee, Ravi" falls into a stranger's hands. He cannot cash it at the counter and no bank may credit it to his account. Ravi's money is safe.

Who may cross (S.125). The drawer; the holder (may cross an open cheque generally or specially, turn a general crossing into a special one, and add "not negotiable"); and a banker to whom a cheque is crossed specially, who may cross it specially to another banker as its agent for collection.

Paying banker's duty. Paying a crossed cheque against the crossing makes the banker liable to the true owner for any loss (S.129); paying in due course according to the crossing protects it (S.128).

Chapter 3 · asked 7× 🔥🔥 Important

Bank drafts, dividend warrants and other instruments; deemed negotiable instruments

📖 The story: Paying the university by draft

A student must pay admission fees to a university in another city, and the university will not accept a personal cheque.

❓ What does the law say?

⚖️ The law behind the story

Here is the law that decides it:

Demand draft. An order by one office of a bank on another office of the same bank to pay a sum on demand to a named person or his order. The buyer pays the bank first, so the payee gets near-certain payment.

Negotiability of a draft. A draft payable to order is a negotiable instrument by mercantile usage (it is a bill of exchange drawn by a bank). It can be indorsed and crossed; the crossing rules and the collecting banker's protection apply to drafts (S.131A), and the paying office is protected by S.85A. Since the bank itself is the drawer, the buyer usually cannot stop payment once it has been delivered to the payee.

Dividend warrant. An order by a company on its banker to pay a shareholder his dividend. It is treated like a cheque (payable on demand, can be crossed and collected through a bank).

Other bank instruments. Banker's cheque / pay order (for local payment); traveller's cheque (prepaid, signed once on purchase and again on encashment; now rare); hundis (indigenous instruments governed by local usage, saved by S.1 NI Act); bearer debentures and share warrants to bearer (negotiable by custom).

Not negotiable. Postal orders, money orders, fixed deposit receipts and share certificates. They may be transferred or encashed, but they do not carry the special features of negotiable instruments (better title, presumptions).

🏛️ How the story ends

A demand draft is an order by one bank office on another to pay on demand; the buyer pays the bank first, so the payee gets near-certain payment.

She buys a DD, which the university can safely accept.

Part C workshop · 10 marks each

Solve the problem like a judge

Every Part C problem is a new story with the same law. Spot the issue, state the law, apply it to the facts, and conclude. Quote the matching case story to lift your answer.

Problem 1. A, indebted to B, gives B a cheque, which B presents in due time. Payment is refused because the bank has failed. What are B's rights?

Asked: Sep 2021

Issue
Who bears the loss when a cheque presented in time is not paid because the bank failed?
Law
S.30: the drawer is liable to the holder if the cheque is dishonoured. S.84: only if the holder delays presentment and the drawer suffers loss through the bank's failure is the drawer discharged to that extent, the holder becoming a creditor of the bank.
Apply
B presented in due time, so S.84 does not help A. The cheque was dishonoured; A's original debt revives.
Conclusion
B can sue A on the cheque and on the original debt. A must claim his balance from the failed bank's liquidator. (Had B delayed, the loss would have fallen on B to the extent of A's loss.)

Problem 2. Ram could not present a cheque from his friend because he was busy. The last day of its validity (or limitation) falls on a holiday. Advise Ram.

Asked: Aug 2023

Issue
What happens if the last day for presenting a cheque or suing falls on a holiday?
Law
RBI rule: a cheque is valid for three months from its date; there is no grace for a holiday, so it should be presented on the last working day before. Limitation Act S.4: if the period for a suit expires on a day the court is closed, it may be filed on reopening. General Clauses Act S.10 applies to S.142 complaints. The debt itself is recoverable within three years.
Apply
Ram should present the cheque before the holiday (e.g. through a drop box or electronic deposit). If it becomes stale, the cheque cannot be presented, but the debt survives.
Conclusion
Present the cheque on the preceding working day; if it lapses, ask the friend to revalidate it or issue a fresh cheque, or sue for the debt within limitation. A court filing that falls due on a holiday can be made on the next working day.

Problem 3. A cheque payable to "X or bearer" is lost. Y finds it and (a) cashes it at the bank, or (b) gives it to Z, who takes it honestly for value. Rights of X?

Asked: 2017 · 2018

Issue
What title does a finder or a later holder get to a bearer cheque?
Law
S.85(2): a banker paying a bearer cheque in due course is discharged. S.58: a finder gets no title. S.47, S.9: a bearer cheque passes by delivery; a holder in due course gets a good title even from a finder.
Apply
(a) The bank paid the bearer in due course before any stop order: it is protected. (b) Z is a holder in due course and gets a good title.
Conclusion
X cannot recover from the bank or from Z; he can recover from Y. Had X informed the bank before payment, the bank would have been liable if it paid. Lesson: always cross cheques.

Problem 4. A cheque crossed "A/c payee — Ravi" is indorsed by Ravi to Shyam. Shyam's bank credits it to Shyam's account. Ravi later says his signature was forged. Is Shyam's bank protected?

Asked: Practice (Nov 2022, Aug 2023: account payee)

Issue
Can a collecting banker credit an account payee cheque to someone other than the payee?
Law
"Account payee" is a direction to the collecting banker to credit only the payee's account. S.131: protection only if the banker acts without negligence.
Apply
Crediting an account payee cheque to a third party's account, without enquiry, is negligence.
Conclusion
Shyam's bank loses S.131 protection and is liable to Ravi (the true owner) for conversion.
