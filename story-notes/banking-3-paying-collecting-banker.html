Paying Banker and Collecting Banker

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

Every cheque has two banks in its life. The paying banker is the drawer's bank, which pays out of the drawer's account. The collecting banker is the payee's bank, which collects the money and credits it to its customer. Both handle lakhs of cheques a day, and neither can check whether every signature on the back is genuine.

So the law makes a deal with them: pay or collect honestly and carefully, and you are protected even if a thief was somewhere in the chain. But a bank that is careless, or pays a cheque whose drawer's own signature is forged, must bear the loss itself.

Chapter 1 · asked 6× 🔥🔥 Important

Paying banker: duty to honour cheques, when the bank must or may refuse, and wrongful dishonour

📖 The story: Money in the account, cheque returned

A shopkeeper has ₹2 lakh in his current account. His bank returns his ₹50,000 cheque to a supplier, marked "insufficient funds" by a clerical error.

❓ What does the law say?

⚖️ The law behind the story

Here is the law that decides it:

Duty to honour (S.31). The drawee of a cheque having sufficient funds of the drawer, properly applicable to its payment, must pay the cheque when duly required, and in default must compensate the drawer for any loss or damage. The duty is owed to the drawer (customer), not to the payee.

Conditions. (1) Sufficient, available funds (or an agreed overdraft); (2) cheque properly drawn and signed as per mandate; (3) presented at the branch where the account is kept (or through clearing), in business hours; (4) presented within three months of its date (otherwise stale); (5) no legal bar.

When the mandate ends. The bank must stop payment on: (1) the drawer's countermand (stop-payment order); (2) notice of the customer's death, insanity or insolvency; (3) a garnishee order or other attachment (court, income-tax); (4) notice of a defect in the holder's title (lost or stolen cheque); (5) winding-up of a company customer; (6) closure of the account.

Must or may refuse. Post-dated cheque before its date; stale cheque; material alteration not authenticated by the drawer; signature not matching; amount in words and figures differ (S.18: words prevail, but banks return such cheques); mutilated cheque; a crossed cheque presented at the counter for cash; insufficient funds (the bank cannot pay part).

Garnishee order (S.60, O.XXI r.46 CPC). A creditor who has a decree against the customer asks the court to attach the customer's balance in the bank. The court first issues an order nisi (show cause); from service of it, the bank must stop payments out of the attached amount; then an order absolute directs payment to the court or creditor. It attaches the balance at the time of service, not future credits; a joint account is not attached for the debt of one holder alone; money held in trust is not attachable.

Wrongful dishonour. If the bank wrongly returns a cheque, it is liable to the customer for damages to his credit and reputation; he need not prove actual loss (Marzetti v. Williams, 1830), and a trader gets substantial damages for injury to his credit. The customer may also go to the RBI Ombudsman or a consumer commission.

💡 Example: Ravi has ₹1,00,000 and issues a cheque for ₹1,10,000. The bank must return it: it cannot pay ₹1,00,000 in part. But if Ravi had ₹1,20,000 and the bank returned the cheque by mistake, it would pay damages to Ravi for the insult to his credit.

🏛️ How the story ends

S.31: a drawee bank with sufficient funds properly applicable must pay the cheque when duly required, or compensate the drawer. The duty is owed to the drawer, not the payee.

The bank is liable to the shopkeeper; the supplier cannot sue the bank.

Chapter 2 · asked 8× 🔥🔥🔥 Must do

Statutory protection to the paying banker; payment in due course; forged cheques

📖 The story: Canara Bank v. Canara Sales Corporation

(1987) 2 SCC 666 · AIR 1987 SC 1603

Canara Sales Corporation kept a current account with Canara Bank. Its cheques were to be signed by its managing partner.

Over several years, the firm's accountant forged the managing partner's signature on dozens of cheques and the bank paid them, debiting the firm's account.

When the fraud came to light, the firm sued the bank for the money. The bank argued that the firm was negligent: it had received passbooks and statements and never noticed the debits.

❓ Is a bank liable for paying cheques bearing a forged drawer's signature, if the customer did not check its statements?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Why protection. A bank can verify its own customer's signature, but not the signatures of strangers who indorse a cheque. To keep cheques moving, the NI Act protects a bank that pays in due course.

Payment in due course (S.10). Payment (1) according to the apparent tenor, (2) in good faith and without negligence, (3) to the person in possession, (4) where there is no reasonable ground to believe he is not entitled.

Order cheques (S.85(1)). Where a cheque payable to order purports to be indorsed by or on behalf of the payee, the drawee is discharged by payment in due course. The bank need not prove the indorsement was genuine; it must only be regular on its face.

Bearer cheques (S.85(2)). A cheque originally payable to bearer can be paid to the bearer in due course notwithstanding any indorsement on it ("once a bearer, always a bearer").

Drafts (S.85A). A draft drawn by one office of a bank on another office, payable to order, gets the same protection when the indorsement purports to be by the payee.

Crossed cheques (S.128). A banker who pays a crossed cheque in due course to a banker (generally) or to the named banker (specially) is discharged, and the drawer is in the same position as if the true owner had been paid.

Material alteration not apparent (S.89). If a cheque has been materially altered but does not appear to be, payment according to its apparent tenor at the time is a valid discharge.

No protection for a forged drawer's signature. A cheque with a forged drawer's signature is not the customer's order at all; the bank pays out of its own money. The customer is not estopped merely because he did not check his statements Canara Bank v. Canara Sales; he is liable only if he knew and kept quiet, or was negligent in drawing the cheque Macmillan and Arthur.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

Yes, the bank is liable. A cheque with a forged signature is not the customer's mandate, and the bank paid it out of its own money.

The customer's mere failure to examine its passbook or statements does not create an estoppel against it; there is no such general duty on the customer.

Why it matters:

  • The bank must know its customer's signature; the statutory protections do not cover a forged drawer's signature.
  • A customer loses his claim only if he knew of the forgery and kept silent, or his own negligence in drawing the cheque caused the loss.
  • Carelessness by the customer's own employee is not, by itself, the customer's negligence towards the bank.

📚 More stories on this topic

London Joint Stock Bank Ltd v. Macmillan and Arthur: A clerk got a cheque signed for £2 with blank spaces, then altered it to £120 and cashed it. Held: The customer owes the bank a duty to draw cheques so as not to invite fraud; having left the gaps, the firm bore the loss. 🪝 Leave no gaps

Chapter 3 · asked 9× 🔥🔥🔥 Must do

Collecting banker: meaning, duties and statutory protection (S.131)

📖 The story: Lloyds Bank Ltd v. E.B. Savory and Co.

[1933] AC 201 · House of Lords

Two clerks employed by the stockbrokers E.B. Savory and Co. stole cheques that the firm had drawn in favour of other brokers.

They paid them into their own accounts, and one into his wife's account, at branches of Lloyds Bank, using a system under which the paying-in branch did not see the account holder's details.

The firm sued the bank for conversion. The bank relied on the statutory protection for collecting bankers.

❓ Was the collecting bank negligent, and so unprotected?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Meaning. A collecting banker is the bank that collects a cheque on behalf of its customer from the paying banker and credits the proceeds to the customer's account.

Agent or holder for value. Usually it acts as the customer's agent. It becomes a holder for value if it pays the customer before collection (lets him draw against the uncleared cheque) or has a lien on it; it can then sue the parties in its own name.

Duties. Present the cheque promptly (delay making it liable for loss); give the customer prompt notice of dishonour; credit proceeds at once; follow the crossing (an "account payee" cheque only to the payee's account); follow KYC rules when opening accounts.

Conversion risk. If the customer had no title (stolen cheque, forged indorsement), the true owner can sue the collecting banker for conversion, since it handled his property.

S.131 protection. A banker who has, in good faith and without negligence, received payment for a customer of a cheque crossed generally or specially to itself incurs no liability to the true owner merely because the customer's title was defective. Crediting the account before receiving payment does not take away this protection (Explanation I); it covers electronic images of truncated cheques too. S.131A extends it to drafts.

Conditions. (1) Crossed cheque; (2) collected for a customer (a person with an account; duration does not matter); (3) as agent; (4) good faith; (5) without negligence. The bank must prove the absence of negligence.

Negligence. Opening an account without proper introduction or KYC; collecting an employer's or company's cheque into an employee's or director's personal account without enquiry Lloyds Bank v. Savory; collecting an account payee cheque for someone else; ignoring an irregular indorsement; amounts out of line with the customer's known means.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

Yes. When opening the accounts, the bank had not asked who the customers' employers were (or the husband's employer), and its system prevented any check.

A bank that does not take such ordinary precautions is not acting "without negligence" and loses the protection.

Why it matters:

  • Collecting banks must know their customers, including their occupation and employer.
  • Cheques of an employer paid into an employee's account are a warning sign.
  • This is the basis of the 'without negligence' test in S.131 NI Act.

Part C workshop · 10 marks each

Solve the problem like a judge

Problem 1. A cheque payable to "R or order" is stolen; the thief forges R's indorsement and the bank pays it in due course. Can R recover from the bank?

Asked: Feb 2024 · 2013 · 2014 · 2017 · 2018

Issue
Is the paying banker liable to the true owner when it pays on a forged indorsement?
Law
S.85(1): an order cheque that purports to be indorsed by the payee, paid in due course, discharges the banker. S.10: payment in due course. S.58: the thief gets no title.
Apply
The indorsement purported to be R's and nothing looked suspicious. The bank paid in good faith and without negligence. A bank is not expected to know the signature of a payee who is not its customer.
Conclusion
R cannot recover from the paying bank. He can sue the thief, and the person who received the money (and, if the cheque was collected through a careless collecting bank, that bank for conversion).

Problem 2. Uday signs a bearer cheque for ₹20,000 and gives it to his office boy to deposit. The boy adds a zero and encashes ₹2,00,000. Is the bank liable?

Asked: Aug 2025

Issue
Who bears the loss of a fraudulent alteration of the amount?
Law
S.87: a material alteration voids the instrument. S.89: payment of an altered cheque whose alteration is not apparent, according to its apparent tenor, is protected. S.18: amount in words prevails. Customer's duty to draw carefully Macmillan and Arthur.
Apply
If the words still read "twenty thousand", the alteration was apparent: paying ₹2 lakh was not according to the apparent tenor and was negligent, so the bank bears the loss. If Uday left gaps that let the boy alter both words and figures cleanly, he breached his duty of care and must bear the loss.
Conclusion
The bank is liable if the alteration was visible or the words did not match; Uday bears the loss if his careless drawing made a clean alteration possible. Either way, the office boy is liable for forgery and cheating (BNS 318, 336).

📖 Story to quote: Macmillan and Arthur

Problem 3. Banker A pays customer B's cheque before B's stop-payment order reaches the bank. Is the banker liable?

Asked: Nov 2022 · May 2023

Issue
When does a countermand take effect?
Law
S.31 and the end of the mandate: a countermand must be clear, from the drawer, and must actually reach the branch where the account is kept. There is no "constructive" countermand Curtice.
Apply
The cheque was paid before the order reached the bank. At that time the bank still had B's mandate.
Conclusion
The banker is not liable; the payment is valid and can be debited to B.

📖 Story to quote: Curtice

Problem 4. The bank made false (excess) credit entries in A's passbook. Relying on them, A issued a post-dated cheque, which the bank dishonoured. Position of the banker?

Asked: Nov 2022

Issue
Is the bank bound by wrong credit entries in the passbook?
Law
Entries in a passbook are the bank's statements of account. If the customer, in good faith, relies on a wrong credit and changes his position, the bank is estopped from reversing it to his prejudice (Skyring v. Greenwood, 1825); dishonouring a cheque drawn within the apparent balance is wrongful dishonour (Holland v. Manchester and Liverpool District Banking Co., 1909). A customer who knew of the error cannot take advantage of it.
Apply
A acted in good faith on the bank's own entries and issued a cheque within the balance shown.
Conclusion
The bank can correct its error for the future, but it is liable for wrongful dishonour of A's cheque and cannot recover sums A spent in good faith relying on the entries.

Problem 5. A customer has ₹1,00,000 in his account and issues a cheque for ₹1,10,000 to Z. The bank refuses to pay. Advise Z.

Asked: Oct 2020 · 2019

Issue
Can Z sue the bank, and what are his remedies?
Law
S.31: the duty to pay is owed to the drawer only when funds are sufficient; a bank cannot pay part. S.30: the drawer is liable to the holder on dishonour. S.138: dishonour for insufficient funds is an offence.
Apply
Funds were insufficient, so the refusal was correct. Z has no claim against the bank (no privity).
Conclusion
Z cannot sue the bank. He should send a demand notice to the drawer within 30 days of the return memo, and if unpaid within 15 days, file a complaint under S.138; he can also sue for the amount.

Problem 6. On 1 January 2025, X gives Y a post-dated cheque dated 26 January 2025. Y presents it on 20 April 2025. Explaining the essentials of a cheque, decide whether it is valid.

Asked: Aug 2025 (Hons)

Issue
Is a post-dated cheque valid, and is it stale on 20 April?
Law
S.6: a cheque is a bill of exchange drawn on a specified banker and payable only on demand (including electronic forms). Essentials: writing, unconditional order, drawn on a banker, signed, certain sum, certain payee, payable on demand. A post-dated cheque is a bill until its date and a cheque from that date Anil Kumar Sawhney. RBI: a cheque is valid for three months from its date.
Apply
Its date is 26 January 2025, so three months run from that date, not from 1 January. 20 April 2025 is within three months.
Conclusion
The cheque is valid and must be paid if funds are sufficient; if dishonoured for want of funds, Y can proceed under S.138.

📖 Story to quote: Anil Kumar Sawhney

Problem 7. Z deposits a demand draft issued by YZ Bank into his account with AB Bank. AB credits him; YZ pays AB; Z withdraws. YZ later says the draft was issued to a different institution and blames AB for not verifying its customer. Decide the liability of each bank.

Asked: Jan 2025

Issue
Who bears the loss when a draft is collected for a person who was not its payee?
Law
S.85A: the issuing (paying) bank is protected if it paid a draft in due course on an indorsement that purports to be by the payee. S.131, S.131A: the collecting bank is protected only if it acted in good faith and without negligence for a customer. Negligence includes crediting an instrument payable to an institution into an individual's account without enquiry Lloyds Bank v. Savory.
Apply
YZ paid AB in due course, so YZ is protected. AB credited a draft payable to an institution into Z's personal account without asking why: that is negligence, so AB loses S.131A protection and is liable to the true owner for conversion.
Conclusion
The loss falls on AB Bank, not YZ Bank. AB can recover from Z, who is also liable for cheating and forgery.

📖 Story to quote: Lloyds Bank v. Savory
