Bar Councils and the Advocates Act, 1961

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

Doctors have the National Medical Commission, chartered accountants their Institute. Lawyers in India govern themselves through elected Bar Councils: one in each State (and one or two shared by small States) and the Bar Council of India at the top. Advocates elect the members; the Councils enrol new lawyers, keep the roll, frame the rules of conduct, and discipline those who break them.

All this comes from one law: the Advocates Act, 1961, which replaced a patchwork of colonial laws with an autonomous, unified All-India Bar.

Bar Council of India and State Bar Councils: constitution, powers and functions

⚖️ The law behind the story

The law, step by step:

Nature. Bar Councils are statutory, elected bodies corporate under the Advocates Act, 1961, with perpetual succession and a common seal (S.5). They make the legal profession self-regulating. Their forerunners were the Bar Councils under the Indian Bar Councils Act, 1926, which were only advisory.

State Bar Council: constitution (S.3). For each State (some share one). Members: the Advocate-General ex officio (in Delhi, the Additional Solicitor-General), and 15, 20 or 25 elected members, depending on the number of advocates on its roll, elected by the advocates by single transferable vote. It elects its own Chairman and Vice-Chairman. Term: five years (S.8).

State Bar Council: functions (S.6). Admit persons as advocates on its roll; prepare and maintain the roll; entertain and determine cases of misconduct; safeguard the rights, privileges and interests of advocates; promote Bar associations, law reform, seminars and legal aid; manage its funds and welfare schemes for advocates; conduct elections; inspect universities when the BCI directs.

BCI: constitution (S.4). The Attorney-General and the Solicitor-General of India ex officio, and one member elected by each State Bar Council from among its members. It elects its Chairman and Vice-Chairman.

BCI: functions (S.7). Lay down standards of professional conduct and etiquette; lay down the procedure for disciplinary committees; safeguard advocates' rights; promote law reform; deal with matters referred by State Councils; exercise general supervision and control over State Bar Councils; promote legal education and lay down its standards, recognise universities whose law degree qualifies for enrolment and inspect them; organise legal aid; recognise foreign qualifications on reciprocity; hear disciplinary appeals (S.37). Under its rules it also conducts the All India Bar Examination.

💡 Example: a new law college in Telangana wants its LL.B. recognised for enrolment. The BCI inspects it and grants approval; its graduates then apply to the Bar Council of Telangana for enrolment and, after enrolment, take the AIBE conducted by the BCI.

Committees and rules. Disciplinary committees (S.9); legal aid committees (S.9A); executive and enrolment committees of State Councils, and executive and legal education committees of the BCI (S.10). State Councils make rules with BCI approval (S.15); the BCI makes rules (S.49), including the BCI Rules on conduct, enrolment and legal education. The BCI may give directions to State Councils (S.48B).

Chapter 2 · asked 4× 🔥🔥 Important

Advocates Act, 1961: salient features

⚖️ The law behind the story

The law, step by step:

Background. Enacted on the recommendations of the All India Bar Committee (1951) and the 14th Report of the Law Commission (1958). It consolidated the law on legal practitioners and repealed the Indian Bar Councils Act, 1926 and the relevant parts of the Legal Practitioners Act, 1879.

Unified Bar. One class of legal practitioners: advocates (S.29), replacing vakils, pleaders, mukhtars and others; advocates are either senior advocates or other advocates (S.16).

Bar Councils. Autonomous, elected State Bar Councils and the Bar Council of India (Chapter II), with defined functions (S.6, S.7) and committees.

Enrolment and roll. Qualifications for enrolment (S.24: citizen, 21 years, recognised law degree, fee), disqualifications (S.24A), State rolls (S.17), transfer between rolls (S.18) and enrolment procedure (S.26).

Right to practise. Only advocates may practise (S.29, S.33); every advocate may practise throughout India in all courts, tribunals and before authorities (S.30, in force 2011); illegal practice punishable (S.45).

Discipline. Disciplinary committees for professional or other misconduct (S.35), with appeals to the BCI (S.37) and the Supreme Court (S.38).

💡 Example: before 1961, a pleader in a district court could not argue in the High Court. After the Act, a young advocate enrolled in Hyderabad can argue the same week in a Munsif court in Warangal and, subject to court rules, in the Supreme Court.

Other features. Rule-making powers of Bar Councils (S.15, S.49); funds and welfare schemes; legal aid committees (S.9A); penalty for illegal practice (S.45); the BCI's powers of revision and directions (S.48A, S.48B); the Central Government's rule-making power (S.49A).
