Seven Lamps of Advocacy and Elements of Advocacy

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

In 1923, an English judge, Sir Edward Abbott Parry, wrote a small book called "The Seven Lamps of Advocacy" (echoing Ruskin's Seven Lamps of Architecture). He said a good advocate's path is lit by seven lamps: Honesty, Courage, Industry, Wit, Eloquence, Judgment and Fellowship. A century later, examiners still ask about them in almost every paper.

Think of Bhulabhai Desai in 1945, defending officers of the Indian National Army at the Red Fort against the might of the British Empire: courage to take the brief, industry in preparation, eloquence in argument, judgment in strategy, and honesty throughout. That is advocacy at its best.

Seven lamps of advocacy

⚖️ The law behind the story

The law, step by step:

Origin. Judge Edward Abbott Parry (England) in The Seven Lamps of Advocacy (1923) described seven qualities that light the path of a good advocate. Indian courts and the BCI Rules reflect the same values.

1. Honesty. The foundation of advocacy. An advocate must never mislead the court, misstate facts or law, or deceive his client. He may defend a person he believes guilty, but must not invent a false defence. Honesty earns the trust of the court, which is the advocate's greatest asset.

2. Courage. Fearlessness to take up unpopular causes, stand up to powerful opponents and even to the judge (respectfully), and tell the client unpleasant truths. Example: Bhulabhai Desai's defence of INA officers (1945); lawyers defending persons in custody against the State. BCI Rule 15 asks advocates to uphold clients' interests fearlessly.

3. Industry. Hard work and thorough preparation: knowing every fact, every document, every precedent. "There is no royal road to success at the Bar." Briefs are won in chambers before they are won in court.

4. Wit. Quick thinking and presence of mind, with a touch of humour, to handle an unexpected question from the Bench or a turn in cross-examination. It must never become rudeness or ridicule.

5. Eloquence. The ability to speak clearly, simply and persuasively. Not flowery speech, but the right words, well ordered, suited to the court and the case.

6. Judgment. Sound sense and wisdom: choosing the right points to argue, the right questions to ask (and not ask), when to settle, and how to advise the client realistically.

7. Fellowship. Comradeship and courtesy towards fellow advocates: fight hard in court, but stay friends outside; respect juniors and seniors; help colleagues. It keeps the Bar united and dignified.

💡 Why they matter today: clients judge lawyers by results, but courts judge them by character. A young advocate who is honest, well prepared and courteous earns the Bench's confidence; that, more than eloquence alone, wins cases over a career.

Chapter 2 · asked 5× 🔥🔥 Important

Elements of advocacy

⚖️ The law behind the story

The law, step by step:

Meaning. Advocacy is the art of presenting a client's case persuasively before a court or tribunal, within the law and ethics. Its elements are the skills and qualities that make an effective advocate.

Knowledge of law. Sound grasp of substantive and procedural law, the latest judgments, and the rules of the court. An advocate must know where to find the law quickly.

Knowledge of facts. Mastery of the facts and documents of the case, often more important than the law: most cases are won or lost on facts.

Preparation and research. Case analysis, identifying issues, preparing a list of dates, compilations and short written notes; anticipating the other side's points.

Drafting. Clear, precise pleadings, petitions, affidavits and written submissions: a well-drafted pleading wins half the battle.

Presentation and examination. Clear and brief oral argument, answering the Bench's questions directly, skilful examination-in-chief and cross-examination of witnesses, and persuasion through logic and precedent.

💡 Example: in a cheque-bounce trial, the advocate who has studied the bank memo dates, the notice and the postal receipt (facts), knows the presumption cases (law), and cross-examines the complainant crisply on the debt, will do better than one relying only on eloquence.

Ethics and temperament. Honesty, courtesy to the court and opponents, patience, punctuality, self-control and tact: the seven lamps in daily practice. Without ethics, skill becomes a danger.
