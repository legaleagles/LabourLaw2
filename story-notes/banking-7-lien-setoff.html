Banker's Lien, Set-off and Appropriation

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

Ramesh owes his bank ₹2 lakh on an overdraft. In the same bank he has left some company debentures, a fixed deposit of ₹1 lakh, and a locker full of jewellery. Can the bank hold on to any of these until he pays?

The law gives a bank three tools. Lien: the right to retain the customer's securities that came into its hands as a banker. Set-off: the right to adjust money it owes him against money he owes it. Appropriation: the rules on which debt a payment goes to. But each tool has limits: the jewellery in safe custody and the locker are not available to the bank at all.

Banker's general lien: meaning, features, when it is not available, and precautions

📖 The story: Brandao v. Barnett

(1846) 12 Cl & F 787 · House of Lords

Exchequer bills belonging to Brandao were kept with the banking firm of Barnett, Hoare and Co. in a box, the key of which was held by Brandao's agent. The bankers took the bills out from time to time only to receive the interest and exchange them for new bills, then put the new bills back in the box.

The agent's own account with the bankers was overdrawn, and the bankers claimed a lien over the bills for that balance.

❓ Did the bankers have a lien on the bills?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Meaning. Lien is the right of a creditor in possession of the debtor's goods to retain them until the debt is paid. A particular lien covers only the debt connected with those goods; a general lien covers the general balance of all dealings.

Legal basis (S.171 Contract Act). Bankers, factors, wharfingers, attorneys of a High Court and policy-brokers may, in the absence of a contract to the contrary, retain as security for a general balance of account any goods bailed to them. It is part of the law merchant Brandao v. Barnett.

Implied pledge. An ordinary lien only allows retention. A banker's lien is treated as an implied pledge, so the bank may sell the securities after reasonable notice to the customer (S.176), and it extends to fixed deposit receipts held by the bank Syndicate Bank v. Vijay Kumar.

Conditions. (1) The property must come into the bank's possession as a banker, in the ordinary course of banking business; (2) it must belong to the customer (in the same capacity as the debt); (3) a debt must be due; (4) there must be no contrary agreement or inconsistent purpose.

Not available on: (1) articles left for safe custody (the bank is a bailee); (2) property given for a specific purpose (e.g. securities to be sold and the proceeds sent to someone); (3) money or securities held in trust; (4) securities left with the bank by mistake or after a loan is repaid; (5) a locker's contents; (6) third-party property.

💡 Example: Ramesh leaves gold for safe custody and also hands the bank debentures to collect interest. His overdraft goes unpaid. The bank may retain (and after notice sell) the debentures, but must return the gold if he asks; for the gold, it can only sue and seek attachment through court.

Precautions. Take a written letter of lien/pledge; check the customer's title; make sure the debt is due; keep safe-custody items separate; give reasonable notice before selling.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

Bankers do have a general lien on all securities deposited with them as bankers by a customer, unless there is an express contract, or circumstances implying a contract, inconsistent with the lien. This is part of the law merchant.

Here, the bills were in the box for custody and were handled only for a special purpose, and they belonged to Brandao, not to the indebted agent, so there was no lien.

Why it matters:

  • The source of the banker's general lien (now S.171 Contract Act in India).
  • Lien attaches only to property received as banker in the course of banking.
  • Safe custody or a specific purpose excludes the lien.

📚 More stories on this topic

Syndicate Bank v. Vijay Kumar: A customer's fixed deposits were attached by his creditor in execution of a decree; the bank said it could hold them for the customer's liabilities to it. Held: A banker has a general lien over all forms of deposits and securities received in the course of banking; it is an implied pledge, and the bank may hold them against the customer's liabilities. 🪝 The bank holds first

Right of set-off; set-off v. lien; appropriation of payments

📖 The story: Devaynes v. Noble (Clayton's case)

(1816) 1 Mer 572 · Court of Chancery

Clayton was a customer of a banking partnership. One of the partners, Devaynes, died. At that date the firm owed Clayton a large balance.

Clayton kept dealing with the surviving partners: he drew money out and paid fresh money in. Later the firm became bankrupt.

Clayton claimed that Devaynes's estate was liable for the balance that existed at the date of the death.

❓ How are payments in and out of a running account appropriated?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Meaning. Set-off is the bank's right to combine accounts: adjust a credit balance in one account of the customer against a debit balance in another, so only the net is payable. It is a banker's own right, exercised without going to court (different from legal set-off in a suit, O.VIII r.6 CPC).

Conditions. (1) Mutual debts between the bank and the customer; (2) in the same right/capacity (personal v. personal); (3) certain in amount and due (a fixed deposit can be set off only on maturity, unless agreed); (4) no agreement against it; (5) reasonable notice to the customer is good practice.

Not available when. Trust accounts against personal debts; a joint account against one holder's personal debt; an account in a different capacity (executor, guardian); debts not yet due or contingent (an unenforced guarantee); money deposited for a specific purpose; after a garnishee order, only debts due before it can be set off.

💡 Example: Ramesh has ₹50,000 in his savings account and owes ₹80,000 on an overdue personal loan. The bank may set off the ₹50,000 and claim the balance ₹30,000. But it cannot touch the ₹1 lakh in his joint account with his wife, nor money he holds as trustee for his nephew.

Lien v. set-off. (1) Lien is over the customer's goods and securities; set-off is over money (debts). (2) Lien means retention (and sale as an implied pledge); set-off means adjustment of accounts. (3) Lien needs possession; set-off needs mutual debts due. (4) Lien can secure a general balance; set-off needs ascertained, matured amounts.

Appropriation of payments (S.59–61 Contract Act). When a debtor owing several debts pays money: (1) the debtor may say which debt it is for (S.59); (2) if he does not, the creditor may apply it to any lawful debt due, even a time-barred one (S.60); (3) if neither does, it goes to debts in order of time (S.61).

Clayton's rule. In a running account, the first payment in is set against the first debit out ("first in, first out") Clayton's case. So when a guarantor dies or a partner retires, the bank must stop (rule off) the account and open a new one; otherwise later deposits will wipe out the old debt for which the guarantor or the old partner was liable.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

In a current account, in the absence of any other appropriation, the first item on the debit side is discharged by the first item on the credit side, in order of time.

Clayton's withdrawals after the death had exhausted the old balance; what remained due was a new debt of the surviving partners only, so Devaynes's estate was not liable for it.

Why it matters:

  • Basis of S.61 Contract Act (appropriation in order of time).
  • Banks must rule off an account when a guarantor dies or a partner retires, to keep their claims alive.
  • Can be displaced by agreement.

Part C workshop · 10 marks each

Solve the problem like a judge

Every Part C problem is a new story with the same law. Spot the issue, state the law, apply it to the facts, and conclude. Quote the matching case story to lift your answer.

Problem 1. X has deposited jewels worth ₹50,000 with the bank for safe custody. Later he becomes a debtor to the bank. Explain the bank's rights.

Asked: Feb 2024 · May 2023 · 2014 · 2015

Issue
Can the bank exercise its lien over articles kept for safe custody?
Law
S.171 Contract Act gives a general lien over goods bailed to a banker as banker; but articles deposited for safe custody are held as a bailee for a specific purpose, which is inconsistent with lien Brandao v. Barnett. S.151: bailee's duty of care. O.XXXVIII r.5 CPC: attachment before judgment.
Apply
The jewels were given only for safe custody, before any loan, and not as security. The purpose excludes lien.
Conclusion
The bank has no lien on the jewels and must return them on demand. Its remedy is to sue X for the debt and, if needed, seek attachment of the jewels through the court; it can also set off any credit balance X holds.

📖 Story to quote: Brandao v. Barnett

Problem 2. C instructs his banker to buy certain debentures for him, keep them and collect the interest. Later C's current account is overdrawn. Can the banker exercise a lien over the debentures?

Asked: Aug 2023

Issue
Are securities held for collection of interest subject to the banker's lien?
Law
S.171: lien over securities that come to the banker in the ordinary course of banking business. Securities held for collection of interest or dividends are held as banker, so lien attaches (In re United Service Co., Johnson's Claim, 1870). But where securities are kept only for safe custody, e.g. in a sealed box, there is no lien Brandao v. Barnett.
Apply
Buying debentures and collecting interest are banking services; the debentures are in the bank's hands as banker, not merely in safe custody, and no contrary agreement exists.
Conclusion
Yes. The banker can retain the debentures for the overdraft and, after reasonable notice, sell them; it must account for any surplus. If the debentures had been lodged in a sealed cover purely for safe custody, there would be no lien.

📖 Story to quote: Brandao v. Barnett

Problem 3. Legal position of the banker: (a) B deposits gold ornaments worth ₹50,000 for safe custody; (b) D deposits ₹40,000 as a two-year fixed deposit.

Asked: Aug 2023

Issue
What relationship and rights arise in each case?
Law
(a) Bailment (S.148, S.151 Contract Act): bailee, no lien. (b) Debtor-creditor: the fixed deposit is a debt of the bank payable at maturity Foley v. Hill; general lien / set-off over fixed deposits Syndicate Bank v. Vijay Kumar.
Apply
(a) The bank must keep the ornaments with reasonable care and return them on demand; if they are lost through its negligence, it must compensate B. (b) The bank owns the ₹40,000 and must repay it with interest on maturity (or earlier, as agreed, with a penalty); if D owes the bank money, it may set it off once the deposit matures or as agreed.
Conclusion
(a) Bailee: duty of care, no lien. (b) Debtor: repay with interest at maturity; it may grant a loan against the deposit and exercise lien/set-off for D's dues.

📖 Story to quote: Syndicate Bank v. Vijay Kumar, Foley v. Hill

Problem 4. Ramesh has ₹50,000 in his savings account, an overdue personal loan of ₹80,000, and a joint account with his wife with ₹1,00,000. The bank debits both accounts to recover the loan. Advise Ramesh.

Asked: Practice (Aug 2025 Hons: when can set-off be used)

Issue
Can the bank set off a joint account against one holder's personal debt?
Law
Set-off requires mutual debts in the same right. A joint account belongs to the holders together; it is not a debt owed to Ramesh alone.
Apply
Setting off his own savings account (same right, loan overdue) is valid. Debiting the joint account is not.
Conclusion
The bank may adjust ₹50,000 from his savings account but must reverse the debit to the joint account; Ramesh can complain to the bank and then the RBI Ombudsman.
