Endorsement and Dishonour of Cheques (S.138)

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

An endorsement is a signature that passes the baton. Sita writes "Pay Gopal or order" and signs on the back of her cheque: Gopal now owns it, and Sita has promised to pay him if the bank does not. Each signature on the back adds one more person who will answer if the cheque fails.

And when a cheque bounces, the law does more than allow a civil suit. Since 1989, S.138 makes it a crime, because a cheque is a promise people rely on. But the law is fair to the drawer too: he first gets a written notice and 15 days to pay. Only then can the payee go to court.

Chapter 1 · asked 7× 🔥🔥 Important

Endorsement: meaning, essentials, kinds and effects

📖 The story: Signing on the back

A contractor receives a cheque "Pay Ramesh or order", signs his name on the back, and hands it to his cement supplier.

❓ What does the law say?

⚖️ The law behind the story

Here is the law that decides it:

Meaning (S.15). When the maker or holder signs on the back or face of an instrument (or on a slip attached, an allonge) for the purpose of negotiation, he is said to indorse it; he is the indorser, and the person to whom it is indorsed is the indorsee.

Essentials. (1) Signed by the holder; (2) on the instrument or an allonge; (3) for the whole amount; (4) followed by delivery (S.46, S.48); (5) signature matches the name of the payee as written.

Blank and full (S.16). Blank (general): only the signature; the instrument becomes payable to bearer. Full (special): "Pay Gopal or order" + signature; payable to the named indorsee or his order. A holder may convert a blank indorsement into full by writing a direction above the signature (S.49).

💡 Example: a cheque "Pay Sita or order". Sita signs only "Sita" on the back and hands it to Gopal: that is a blank indorsement and Gopal can pass it on by mere delivery. If Sita writes "Pay Gopal or order, Sita", it is a full indorsement and Gopal must indorse it to pass it on.

Restrictive (S.50). Words that stop further negotiation or limit the indorsee's authority: "Pay Gopal only", "Pay Gopal for my use", "Pay Gopal or order for collection".

Sans recours and facultative (S.52). The indorser may exclude his own liability: "Pay Gopal or order, sans recours (without recourse to me)". A facultative indorsement gives up a right of the indorser, e.g. "notice of dishonour waived".

Conditional (S.52). The indorser may make his liability, or the indorsee's right to receive the amount, depend on a specified event, even one that may never happen: "Pay Gopal on his marriage".

Partial (S.56). An indorsement transferring only part of the amount is not valid for negotiation. If part has been paid, that may be noted and the instrument negotiated for the balance.

Effect. The property in the instrument passes to the indorsee, with the right to negotiate it further (S.50); the indorser becomes liable to every later holder if it is dishonoured, provided notice of dishonour is given (S.35, S.93). Estoppel: an indorser cannot deny the genuineness of earlier signatures to a holder in due course (S.122).

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ends

S.15: signing on the instrument (or an allonge) for the purpose of negotiation is an indorsement; he is the indorser, the supplier the indorsee.

The cement supplier becomes the indorsee and new holder.

Chapter 2 · asked 6× 🔥🔥 Important

Dishonour of cheques: S.138 and the penal provisions of the NI Act

📖 The story: Meters and Instruments Pvt. Ltd. v. Kanchan Mehta

(2018) 1 SCC 560 · decided 5 Oct 2017

Cheques issued by a company were dishonoured and complaints under S.138 were filed against it and its officers.

The accused offered to pay the amount with costs, but the complainant did not agree to compound. The question reached the Supreme Court: can the court close the case if the complainant has been fully compensated?

The Court used the occasion to look at why cheque cases take years and how to speed them up.

❓ Can a S.138 case be closed when the accused pays fair compensation, even without the complainant's consent?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Object. Chapter XVII (S.138–148), added in 1989, gives credibility to cheques by making dishonour an offence. It is basically a civil wrong with a criminal sanction Meters and Instruments.

Ingredients (S.138). (1) A cheque drawn by a person on an account maintained by him; (2) for the discharge of a legally enforceable debt or liability (not a gift or donation); (3) presented within its validity (three months); (4) returned unpaid because the amount is insufficient or exceeds the arrangement. Courts include "account closed", "payment stopped" and "signature differs" when caused by the drawer Laxmi Dyechem.

Notice and time limits. The payee must send a written demand within 30 days of receiving the bank's return memo; the drawer has 15 days from receipt of the notice to pay. The offence is complete only when he fails to pay within those 15 days.

Punishment. Imprisonment up to two years, or fine up to twice the cheque amount, or both. It is not a defence that the drawer had no reason to believe the cheque would bounce (S.140).

💡 Case story Rangappa: the drawer admitted his signature but said the cheque was a lost blank cheque that had been misused. The Supreme Court held that S.139 presumes not only that the cheque was received for a debt, but that a legally enforceable debt existed; the drawer must rebut it with probable evidence, and his bare story failed.

Offences by companies (S.141). The company and every person in charge of and responsible for its business at the time are liable, unless they prove lack of knowledge or due diligence. Nominee directors of government or financial institutions are excluded. The company itself must be made an accused for directors to be prosecuted (Aneeta Hada v. Godfather Travels, 2012).

Procedure (S.142–147). Cognizance only on a written complaint by the payee or holder in due course, within one month of the cause of action (delay may be condoned); triable by a Metropolitan Magistrate or Judicial Magistrate of the first class; jurisdiction where the payee's bank branch is (S.142(2), 2015). Summary trial (S.143); summons by post or courier (S.144); evidence on affidavit (S.145); the bank's return memo is proof of dishonour (S.146); the offence is compoundable (S.147).

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

Yes. The offence is primarily a civil wrong; its object is to compensate the payee. If the accused pays the cheque amount with interest and costs as assessed by the court, the court may close the proceedings.

Courts should use summary trial, evidence on affidavit, service by post or email, and encourage early payment and settlement.

Why it matters:

  • Compensation, not punishment, is the main aim of S.138.
  • Early payment should be encouraged by lower costs; late settlement costs more (Damodar S. Prabhu guidelines).
  • Summary procedure is the norm.

📚 More stories on this topic

Laxmi Dyechem v. State of Gujarat: Cheques given to a company came back with remarks like "drawer's signature incomplete" and "image not found"; the High Court quashed the complaints. Held: Dishonour for such reasons can also be an offence under S.138 if the drawer caused it to avoid payment; "insufficient funds" is the genus, these are its species. 🪝 A bounce is a bounce
Rangappa v. Sri Mohan: A drawer admitted signing a bounced cheque but said it was a lost blank cheque that the complainant had misused. Held: S.139 presumes that a legally enforceable debt existed; the drawer can rebut it by probable evidence, but a bare story is not enough. 🪝 Admit the signature, explain the debt

Chapter 3 · asked 2× 🔥 Good to know

The Negotiable Instruments (Amendment) Act, 2018: salient features

📖 The story: Years of waiting

A small supplier has waited 5 years for a cheque case to end while the drawer keeps seeking adjournments.

❓ What does the law say?

⚖️ The law behind the story

Here is the law that decides it:

Why. Cheque cases were clogging criminal courts and drawers used delay tactics (appeals, adjournments) while the payee waited years. The amendment, in force from 1 September 2018, makes the drawer pay something up front.

Interim compensation (S.143A). The trial court may order the drawer to pay interim compensation of up to 20% of the cheque amount: in a summary or summons trial when he pleads not guilty, and in other cases when the charge is framed. It must be paid within 60 days (extendable by 30 days).

Deposit in appeal (S.148). When a convicted drawer appeals, the appellate court may order him to deposit at least 20% of the fine or compensation awarded by the trial court (in addition to any interim compensation), within 60 days (extendable by 30 days). The court may release it to the complainant during the appeal.

Refund and recovery. If the drawer is acquitted, the complainant must refund the interim compensation with interest at the RBI bank rate, within 60 days. Interim compensation is recoverable like a fine; any final fine or compensation is reduced by it.

Courts on the amendment. S.143A is prospective: it does not apply to complaints filed before 1 September 2018 (G.J. Raja v. Tejraj Surana, 2019). S.148 applies to appeals filed after that date even if the complaint was older (Surinder Singh Deswal v. Virender Gandhi, 2019); deposit is the rule, but the appellate court may exempt in an exceptional case for recorded reasons (Jamboo Bhandari v. M.P. State Industrial Development Corporation, 2023).

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ends

The 2018 amendment (from 1 September 2018) makes the drawer pay something up front to stop delay tactics.

The supplier can now get some money during the case.

Part C workshop · 10 marks each

Solve the problem like a judge

Every Part C problem is a new story with the same law. Spot the issue, state the law, apply it to the facts, and conclude. Quote the matching case story to lift your answer.

Problem 1. An indorsement on a cheque says "Pay C if he returns from London". C has not returned but wants to claim the amount. Can he succeed?

Asked: Oct 2020 · 2016 · 2018

Issue
What is the effect of a conditional indorsement?
Law
S.52: an indorser may, by express words, make his liability or the indorsee's right to receive the amount depend on a specified event, although it may never happen.
Apply
The indorser made C's right to receive conditional on his return from London. The event has not happened.
Conclusion
No. Until C returns from London, he cannot claim on the indorsement or hold the indorser liable. (A paying banker that pays in due course is still discharged; but C has no enforceable right.)

Problem 2. The holder of a promissory note for ₹1,000 writes on it "Pay B ₹500" and indorses it. Is this valid?

Asked: Sep 2024 · 2017 · 2019

Issue
Can an instrument be indorsed for part of its amount?
Law
S.56: a writing that transfers only part of the amount due is not valid for negotiation; but where part has been paid, that may be noted and the instrument negotiated for the balance.
Apply
Nothing has been paid on the note. The indorsement tries to split ₹1,000 into ₹500.
Conclusion
The partial indorsement is invalid for negotiation: B does not become a holder and cannot sue on the note. The note itself remains valid in the original holder's hands. If ₹500 had been paid, he could note it and indorse the balance.

Problem 3. A is the holder of a bill payable to the order of B. Explain the steps of an endorsement in blank.

Asked: Sep 2024

Issue
How is a bill negotiated by blank indorsement?
Law
S.15, S.16: indorsement in blank = only the signature. S.46, S.48: delivery completes negotiation. S.49: conversion into full indorsement. S.35: liability of indorser.
Apply
(1) The payee/holder B signs his name on the back exactly as written; (2) delivers the bill to the transferee; (3) the bill now becomes payable to bearer and can pass by mere delivery; (4) any holder may convert it to a full indorsement by writing "Pay X or order" above B's signature.
Conclusion
A blank indorsement is just the holder's signature plus delivery; it turns the bill into a bearer instrument, and the indorser is liable to every later holder.

Problem 4. B accepted a bill drawn in Paris. The indorsee, with the drawer's consent, altered the place of drawing from Paris to Rome. Is B liable?

Asked: Feb 2024 · 2017

Issue
Is changing the place of drawing a material alteration, and what is its effect?
Law
S.87: a material alteration makes the instrument void against any party who was a party at the time of the alteration and did not consent. Changing the place of drawing alters the law that governs the bill, so it is material (Koch v. Dicks, 1933).
Apply
B was a party (acceptor) and did not consent. The drawer and the indorsee consented.
Conclusion
B is not liable on the bill. The drawer and the indorsee, who agreed to the change, remain liable.

Problem 5. X accepts a bill for the accommodation of A (drawer). A transfers it to B, and B to C, without consideration. C transfers it to D for value. D transfers it to E without consideration. X dishonours it. Rights of A, B, C, D and E?

Asked: Sep 2021

Issue
Who can sue whom on an accommodation bill passed with and without consideration?
Law
S.43: a party who gets an instrument without consideration has no rights against the party who gave it to him; but if any party transferred it to a holder for consideration, that holder and every later holder deriving title from him may recover from the transferor for consideration and any prior party. An accommodation party is liable to a holder for value.
Apply
A, B and C gave nothing, so none of them can sue the person he took from, and A cannot sue X (the accommodating party). D paid value to C: D can sue C, B, A and X. E paid nothing to D, so he cannot sue D, but he derives title from D (a holder for value), so he can sue C, B, A and X.
Conclusion
A, B and C have no claims. D can recover from C, B, A and X. E can recover from C, B, A and X, but not from D.

Problem 6. A signs a cheque "A, Agent". It is dishonoured. Is A personally liable?

Asked: Sep 2021

Issue
Does adding the word "agent" protect the signer?
Law
S.28: an agent who signs without indicating that he signs as agent and that he does not intend to incur personal liability is personally liable. S.27: to bind the principal, the agent must sign "for and on behalf of" him.
Apply
"A, Agent" does not name any principal; the word "agent" merely describes A. It does not show that he signed only for someone else.
Conclusion
Yes, A is personally liable. He should have signed "for and on behalf of P, A, Agent".

Problem 7. Amit gives a private school a cheque for ₹40,000 for fees. It returns unpaid: "Account closed". What can the school do?

Asked: Oct 2020

Issue
Is "account closed" a dishonour under S.138, and what are the steps?
Law
S.138 covers a cheque returned because the account has been closed (NEPC Micon Ltd v. Magma Leasing Ltd, 1999) Laxmi Dyechem. S.142: complaint within one month; jurisdiction where the payee's bank is. S.143A: interim compensation. Civil remedy: summary suit under O.XXXVII CPC.
Apply
Fees were a legally enforceable liability. The return memo is proof of dishonour (S.146).
Conclusion
The school should send Amit a written demand within 30 days of the return memo; if he does not pay within 15 days of receiving it, file a complaint under S.138 within the next month, ask for interim compensation, and may also file a summary civil suit.

📖 Story to quote: Laxmi Dyechem

Problem 8. A gives B a cheque on 24 August 2023. On 21 September the bank receives an order nisi (garnishee order) attaching A's account. The same day B presents the cheque; it is returned because of the garnishee order. Was the bank right? Remedies of B?

Asked: Aug 2025

Issue
Can a bank dishonour a cheque because of a garnishee order, and is A liable?
Law
O.XXI r.46 CPC: from service of the order nisi, the bank must not pay out of the attached account. S.31 NI Act: duty to pay exists only when funds are "properly applicable". S.30: drawer's civil liability on dishonour. S.138 applies where the cheque returns because the drawer's funds are insufficient.
Apply
Once the order nisi was served, A's balance was frozen by law; the bank had to refuse payment. B has no claim against the bank. A remains liable to B on the cheque and the debt. Whether S.138 applies depends on why the account could not pay: if the attachment arose from A's own default and left no funds for B, a court may treat it as insufficiency; if A had arranged funds and the freeze was beyond his control, the criminal charge is weak.
Conclusion
The bank was right to dishonour; B cannot sue the bank. B can recover from A by a civil suit (summary suit), and can send a S.138 notice; if A does not pay within 15 days, B may file a complaint, which will turn on whether A had arranged funds.
