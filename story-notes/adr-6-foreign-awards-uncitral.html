Foreign Awards and UNCITRAL

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

A German company wins an arbitration in London against an Indian buyer: ₹20 crore. The award is useless unless it can be enforced where the buyer's money is: in India. That is what Part II of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act does. India and 170-odd other countries have joined the New York Convention, 1958, promising to enforce each other's arbitral awards with only a few, narrow grounds for refusal.

The Indian court does not re-hear the case. It checks the papers, asks a few basic questions (was there a valid agreement, a fair hearing, is the matter arbitrable, is enforcement against India's public policy?) and, if satisfied, treats the foreign award as its own decree.

Foreign award: meaning and its enforcement in India (New York and Geneva Conventions)

📖 The story: Renusagar Power Co. Ltd. v. General Electric Co.

1994 Supp (1) SCC 644 · AIR 1994 SC 860

General Electric of the USA agreed to supply equipment and services for Renusagar Power's thermal power plant in Uttar Pradesh.

Disputes over payments went to arbitration under the rules of the International Chamber of Commerce, and the tribunal made an award in GE's favour, with interest.

When GE sought to enforce the award in India under the Foreign Awards Act, 1961, Renusagar argued that enforcement would violate Indian foreign-exchange law and so the public policy of India.

❓ When can an Indian court refuse to enforce a foreign award as being contrary to public policy?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Meaning (S.44). A "foreign award" is an arbitral award on differences between persons arising out of legal relationships, contractual or not, considered commercial under Indian law, made on or after 11 October 1960, in pursuance of a written arbitration agreement to which the New York Convention applies, in a territory that the Central Government has notified as a reciprocating territory.

Conventions and reservations. The Geneva Protocol (1923) and Geneva Convention (1927) were the first international arrangements; the New York Convention (1958) replaced them for most countries and is now near-universal. India enforced them first through the Arbitration (Protocol and Convention) Act, 1937 and the Foreign Awards (Recognition and Enforcement) Act, 1961; today Part II of the 1996 Act does so. India made two reservations: reciprocity and commercial relationships only.

Referral to arbitration (S.45). If a party sues in India on a matter covered by an agreement under the Convention, the court shall refer the parties to arbitration unless it prima facie finds the agreement null and void, inoperative or incapable of being performed.

Application and evidence (S.47). The party seeking enforcement applies to the High Court having jurisdiction (over the subject matter or the debtor's assets) and produces: (a) the original award or a duly authenticated copy; (b) the original arbitration agreement or a certified copy; (c) evidence that it is a foreign award; with translations if not in English.

Grounds for refusal (S.48). Enforcement may be refused only if the party resisting proves: (a) incapacity or an invalid agreement; (b) no proper notice of the arbitrator's appointment or proceedings, or he was otherwise unable to present his case; (c) the award goes beyond the submission (severable); (d) the composition or procedure was not as agreed or as per the law of the seat; (e) the award is not yet binding, or has been set aside or suspended in the country where it was made. Or the court finds: (a) the subject matter is not arbitrable in India; or (b) enforcement is contrary to the public policy of India (fraud or corruption, fundamental policy of Indian law, most basic notions of morality or justice), with no review on merits.

💡 Case story Renusagar Power: General Electric won an ICC award against Renusagar Power. Renusagar argued that enforcing it, with interest, broke Indian foreign-exchange law and so public policy. The Supreme Court enforced it: public policy means the fundamental policy of Indian law, the interests of India, or justice and morality, not every breach of an Indian law.

Deemed decree and appeal (S.49, S.50). If the court is satisfied that the award is enforceable, it is deemed to be a decree of that court and executed like one. An appeal lies only against an order refusing to refer the parties to arbitration (S.45) or refusing to enforce the award (S.48); no second appeal, though an appeal to the Supreme Court is possible by special leave.

Geneva awards (S.53–60). Awards under the Geneva Protocol/Convention (from countries not party to the New York Convention but notified under the Geneva Convention) are enforced on similar lines: evidence (S.56), conditions for enforcement (S.57), deemed decree (S.58) and appeal (S.59). In practice, almost all foreign awards now come under the New York Convention.

🏛️ How the story ended

Public policy in the context of enforcing a foreign award has a narrow meaning, as in private international law.

Enforcement can be refused only if it would be contrary to (1) the fundamental policy of Indian law, (2) the interests of India, or (3) justice or morality. A mere contravention of some Indian law is not enough. The award was enforced.

Why it matters:

  • Courts do not review the merits of a foreign award.
  • This test was later written into S.48 (and S.34) by the 2015 amendment, with 'interests of India' dropped.
  • Pro-enforcement approach of the New York Convention.

UNCITRAL and the UNCITRAL Model Law

⚖️ The law behind the story

The law, step by step:

What UNCITRAL is. The United Nations Commission on International Trade Law, set up by the UN General Assembly in 1966 to harmonise and modernise the law of international trade.

Model Law, 1985. The UNCITRAL Model Law on International Commercial Arbitration (adopted 1985, amended 2006) is a template for national arbitration laws: party autonomy, minimal court intervention, competence-competence, equal treatment, limited grounds to set aside, and recognition of awards. Many countries have adopted it.

Arbitration and Conciliation Rules. The UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules (1976, revised 2010 and 2013) are widely used in ad hoc arbitration, including investor-State disputes; the UNCITRAL Conciliation Rules (1980) shaped Part III of the 1996 Act. Newer texts include the Model Law on International Commercial Mediation (2018) and the Singapore Convention on Mediation (2019), which India has signed.

Influence on India. The Preamble of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 expressly says it takes into account the Model Law and the UNCITRAL Conciliation Rules. Hence S.5 (minimal intervention), S.16 (competence-competence), S.18 (equal treatment), S.34 (limited grounds) and S.48 all follow the Model Law.
