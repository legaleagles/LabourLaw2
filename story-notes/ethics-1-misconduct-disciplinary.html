Professional Misconduct and Disciplinary Committees

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

An elderly couple hand their lawyer some promissory notes and money for court fees. "File the suits," they say. Months later he tells them the suits are filed and decrees obtained. Nothing was ever filed. When they complain to the State Bar Council, the lawyer faces the profession's own court: the disciplinary committee.

The Advocates Act, 1961 lets the profession police itself. A complaint goes to the State Bar Council, its disciplinary committee hears both sides like a court, and it can reprimand, suspend or remove the advocate from the roll. Appeals go to the Bar Council of India and then to the Supreme Court.

Professional misconduct: meaning, kinds and examples

📖 The story: V.C. Rangadurai v. D. Gopalan

(1979) 1 SCC 308 · decided 4 Oct 1978

An elderly couple engaged the advocate V.C. Rangadurai to recover money due on promissory notes. They handed him the notes and money for court fees.

He told them that suits had been filed and that decrees had been passed. In truth, nothing had been filed.

The couple complained to the Bar Council. The disciplinary committee found him guilty and imposed a long suspension; he appealed to the Supreme Court.

❓ Was the advocate guilty of professional misconduct, and what is the right approach to punishment?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Meaning. "Misconduct" is not defined in the Advocates Act, 1961. Broadly, it is any act or omission by an advocate that is dishonourable, unbecoming of the profession, or breaks the standards of professional conduct laid down by the Bar Council of India (BCI Rules, Part VI, Chapter II). The Supreme Court has said misconduct includes conduct that, by the standards of the profession, is disgraceful or dishonourable.

Professional and other misconduct. S.35 speaks of "professional or other misconduct": an advocate can be punished not only for acts in his work but also for serious misbehaviour outside it, if it shows he is unfit to be an advocate.

Towards the client. Misappropriating client's money; taking fees and not doing the work; negligence causing loss; disclosing confidences; appearing for the other side; demanding a share of the result (contingency fee); misleading the client.

💡 Case story V.C. Rangadurai: an advocate took promissory notes and money from an elderly couple to file suits, then falsely told them the suits were filed and decreed. The Supreme Court upheld the finding of gross professional misconduct, but shaped the punishment to reform him, including free legal-aid work.

Towards the court. Misleading the court, fabricating evidence or affidavits, influencing judges, abusive or threatening behaviour, boycotting courts on strike (Ex-Capt. Harish Uppal v. Union of India, 2003), and interfering with witnesses (R.K. Anand v. Registrar, Delhi High Court, 2009).

Towards the profession. Soliciting work through touts or advertising (BCI Rule 36), undercutting fees, carrying on a business while practising, full-time salaried employment while practising (Rule 49), accepting a brief where another advocate is engaged without his consent.

Other misconduct. Criminal acts involving moral turpitude, cheating, forgery, violence, and similar conduct outside court that lowers the profession in public esteem.

Consequences. The disciplinary committee may dismiss the complaint, reprimand, suspend for a period, or remove the name from the roll (S.35(3)); it may also order costs. A forged or fraudulent act is treated very seriously: misappropriating a client's compensation money led to removal from the rolls (Harish Chandra Tiwari v. Baiju, 2002).

🏛️ How the story ended

Yes. Deceiving clients and failing to do the work for which money was taken is gross professional misconduct; a lawyer's relationship with his client is one of trust.

But disciplinary punishment should also aim at reform. The Court reduced the suspension ordered below and required him to serve free legal aid for the poor.

Why it matters:

  • The client-lawyer relationship is fiduciary; betrayal of trust is the gravest misconduct.
  • Disciplinary proceedings are not purely punitive; they protect the public and reform the advocate.
  • Krishna Iyer J.: the profession must practise in a spirit of public service, not money-getting.

Disciplinary committees: constitution, powers, procedure and appeals

⚖️ The law behind the story

The law, step by step:

Constitution (S.9). Every State Bar Council and the Bar Council of India constitute one or more disciplinary committees of three members: two elected by the Council from its members and one co-opted from advocates with at least ten years' standing who are not members. The senior-most advocate among them is the chairman.

Who can complain (S.35(1)). On receipt of a complaint "or otherwise" (on its own motion, or on a court's reference), if the State Bar Council has reason to believe that an advocate on its roll is guilty of professional or other misconduct, it refers the case to its disciplinary committee. It may withdraw a case from one committee and give it to another (S.35(1A)).

Procedure. The committee fixes a date and gives notice to the advocate and to the Advocate-General of the State (S.35(2)); the advocate may file a reply and lead evidence; hearings follow natural justice and BCI Rules (Part VII). Proceedings are judicial proceedings for the purposes of the Penal Code provisions on false evidence (S.42(2)).

Powers (S.42). The powers of a civil court under the CPC for summoning and enforcing attendance and examining witnesses on oath, discovery and production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavits, requisitioning public records, and issuing commissions. The BCI can call for records of any proceeding and pass orders (S.36, S.48A revision).

Orders (S.35(3)). After hearing, the committee may: (a) dismiss the complaint (or file the proceedings if started by the Council); (b) reprimand the advocate; (c) suspend him from practice for a period; (d) remove his name from the State roll. It may award costs.

💡 Example: a client complains that her lawyer kept ₹2 lakh of decree money paid into court. The State Bar Council refers it to its disciplinary committee, which summons bank records, hears both, and orders the advocate's name removed from the roll and the money refunded.

BCI and time limit (S.36, S.36B). The BCI's disciplinary committee hears cases against advocates not on any State roll, and may withdraw any pending case from a State Bar Council (S.36). A State committee must decide a complaint within one year; otherwise the case is transferred to the BCI (S.36B).

Appeals and review (S.37, S.38, S.44). A person aggrieved by a State committee's order (including the Advocate-General) may appeal to the BCI within 60 days (S.37); from the BCI's order, an appeal lies to the Supreme Court within 60 days (S.38). A committee may review its own order on its own motion or otherwise (S.44). An order of suspension or removal is communicated to the Supreme Court and all High Courts.
