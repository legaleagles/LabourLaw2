Letters of Credit and Bank Guarantees

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

A textile exporter in Tiruppur sells shirts to a buyer in Germany. The exporter fears: "What if I ship and he does not pay?" The buyer fears: "What if I pay and he does not ship?" A letter of credit solves both fears: the buyer's bank promises to pay the seller as soon as the seller hands over the right shipping documents. The bank looks only at the papers, never at the shirts.

A bank guarantee works the other way round: a contractor building a bridge for a State corporation gives a guarantee from his bank, which promises to pay the corporation on demand if he fails to perform. Courts treat both as promises the bank must keep, whatever the quarrel between buyer and seller.

Chapter 1 · asked 9× 🔥🔥🔥 Must do

Letter of credit: meaning, parties, working, kinds and legal principles

📖 The story: United Commercial Bank v. Bank of India

(1981) 2 SCC 766 · AIR 1981 SC 1426

A documentary letter of credit was opened for a commercial sale, and documents were dealt with between two nationalised banks, United Commercial Bank and Bank of India.

A dispute arose about the underlying transaction and about which bank had to bear the payment. In the Calcutta High Court, an interim injunction held up the payment under the credit.

The matter came to the Supreme Court.

❓ Should courts restrain a bank from paying under a letter of credit because of disputes about the underlying contract?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Meaning. A letter of credit (documentary credit) is a written undertaking by a bank (the issuing bank), at the request of the buyer, to pay the seller a stated sum, provided the seller presents the stipulated documents within the stated time. It replaces the buyer's credit with the bank's credit. It is usually governed by the ICC's UCP 600.

Parties. Applicant (buyer/importer); issuing bank (buyer's bank); beneficiary (seller/exporter); advising bank (informs the seller); confirming bank (adds its own promise to pay); nominated/negotiating bank (pays or buys the documents); reimbursing bank.

How it works. Sale contract → buyer applies for the LC → issuing bank opens it → advising bank informs the seller → seller ships and presents documents (bill of exchange, bill of lading, invoice, insurance policy, certificate of origin) → the bank checks them (within five banking days) and pays, accepts or negotiates → the issuing bank reimburses → the buyer pays the issuing bank and gets the documents to take delivery.

Autonomy (independence). The credit is separate from the sale contract; banks deal with documents, not goods (UCP 600 Arts. 4, 5). A quarrel about the quality of goods is no ground for the bank to refuse, or for a court to stop, payment against conforming documents UCB v. Bank of India. Only established fraud is an exception.

Strict compliance. The documents must conform exactly to the terms of the credit: "there is no room for documents which are almost the same, or which will do just as well" (Equitable Trust Co. of New York v. Dawson Partners, 1927). The bank may refuse discrepant documents, unless the buyer waives the discrepancy.

Kinds. Irrevocable (UCP 600: all credits are irrevocable) / revocable; confirmed / unconfirmed; sight (pay on presentation) / usance (pay later: acceptance or deferred payment); transferable (seller can transfer to a supplier); back-to-back (a second LC backed by the first); revolving (renews automatically); red clause (advance to seller before shipment) and green clause (advance plus storage); standby LC (works like a guarantee); documentary / clean.

💡 Example: a Tiruppur exporter ships shirts under an irrevocable LC confirmed by an Indian bank. He presents a clean bill of lading and invoice exactly as required and is paid in India within days, even if the German buyer later complains about the colour.

Merits. Seller: assured payment, can get pre-shipment finance. Buyer: pays only against proof of shipment, gets credit time. Demerits: cost, paperwork, and payment can be refused for a small discrepancy; it does not guarantee the quality of goods.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

No. A letter of credit is a separate contract, independent of the sale between buyer and seller; the bank's obligation depends only on the documents.

Courts should not interfere with the obligations of banks under letters of credit or guarantees by interim injunctions, except in cases of fraud or irretrievable injustice. The injunction was set aside.

Why it matters:

  • Autonomy of letters of credit (UCP: banks deal in documents).
  • Commercial certainty in banking must be protected.
  • Fraud is the main exception.

Bank guarantee: meaning, kinds, nature, invocation and injunction; guarantee v. letter of credit

📖 The story: Hindustan Steelworks Construction Ltd. v. Tarapore and Co.

(1996) 5 SCC 34

Hindustan Steelworks Construction Ltd. gave a construction contract to Tarapore and Co., which furnished bank guarantees.

Disputes arose about the work and its payment. When Hindustan Steelworks sought to invoke the guarantees, Tarapore obtained an injunction from the High Court, pointing to the pending disputes.

Hindustan Steelworks appealed to the Supreme Court.

❓ Can disputes under the construction contract justify restraining the invocation of an unconditional bank guarantee?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Meaning. A bank guarantee is a contract by which a bank (surety) promises to pay a beneficiary if its customer (principal debtor) fails to perform or pay (S.126 Contract Act). In practice most are unconditional "on demand" guarantees: the bank pays on a written demand within the validity period.

Kinds. Performance guarantee (completion of a contract); financial guarantee (payment of a debt); advance payment guarantee; bid bond / earnest money guarantee; deferred payment guarantee; guarantees for customs, excise and courts; conditional and unconditional.

Independent promise. An unconditional guarantee is independent of the underlying contract. The bank must pay according to its terms when the beneficiary demands; it cannot look into the dispute between its customer and the beneficiary Hindustan Steelworks.

When courts stop payment. Only in two exceptional cases: (1) fraud of an egregious nature, of which the bank has notice; (2) irretrievable injury or injustice (special equities) U.P. State Sugar Corp.. A mere dispute, or even a strong case on the main contract, is not enough.

💡 Example: a contractor builds a canal for a State corporation and gives a ₹1 crore performance guarantee. The corporation says the work is defective and invokes it. The contractor says the corporation itself caused the delay. The bank must still pay; the contractor must fight the dispute in arbitration or court and recover later if he wins.

Guarantor's rights (Contract Act). A continuing guarantee may be revoked for future transactions (S.129, S.130); the surety is discharged by a variance in the contract made without his consent (S.133), by release of the principal debtor (S.134), or by the creditor losing a security (S.141); on paying, he steps into the creditor's shoes (S.140). A bank guarantee may validly fix a claim period of not less than one year after the event (S.28, Exception 3).

Guarantee v. LC. (1) An LC is a means of payment in a sale; a guarantee is security against default. (2) Under an LC the bank pays against documents showing performance; under a guarantee, against a demand alleging non-performance. (3) LC: bank's primary obligation; traditional guarantee: secondary (but on-demand guarantees work like primary obligations). Both are independent of the underlying contract.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

No. A bank guarantee is an independent and distinct contract between the bank and the beneficiary; it is not qualified by the underlying transaction.

Unless there is fraud or special equity, the beneficiary can invoke it according to its terms. The injunction was set aside.

Why it matters:

  • The bank need not decide who is right in the main contract.
  • The terms of the guarantee decide whether the invocation is valid.
  • Follows the same rule as letters of credit.

📚 More stories on this topic

U.P. State Sugar Corporation v. Sumac International Ltd.: A supplier who had given bank guarantees obtained an injunction stopping a State corporation from encashing them because of a contract dispute. Held: An unconditional bank guarantee must be honoured on its terms; injunction only for egregious fraud known to the bank or irretrievable injustice. The injunction was set aside. 🪝 A guarantee is a promise kept

Part C workshop · 10 marks each

Solve the problem like a judge

Every Part C problem is a new story with the same law. Spot the issue, state the law, apply it to the facts, and conclude. Quote the matching case story to lift your answer.

Problem 1. Company A and Company B enter into a contract for which B furnishes two bank guarantees. A dispute arises about completion of the contract. Will the guarantees be affected?

Asked: Aug 2025 (Hons)

Issue
Can a dispute under the main contract stop the encashment of a bank guarantee?
Law
A bank guarantee is an independent contract; the bank must pay according to its terms Hindustan Steelworks. Injunction only for egregious fraud known to the bank or irretrievable injustice U.P. State Sugar Corp. (also U.P. Co-operative Federation v. Singh Consultants, 1988; Svenska Handelsbanken v. Indian Charge Chrome, 1994).
Apply
The dispute concerns completion of the contract: an ordinary contractual dispute. Unless B can show fraud of the kind described, or irreparable injustice, the guarantees stand.
Conclusion
No. If A invokes the guarantees according to their terms, the bank must pay; B must resolve the dispute separately and claim the money back if it succeeds.

📖 Story to quote: Hindustan Steelworks

Problem 2. Under an LC requiring shipment by 31 March, the seller presents a bill of lading dated 2 April. The bank refuses to pay. The seller says the two-day delay is immaterial. Decide.

Asked: Practice (Nov 2022: legal incidents of LCs)

Issue
Must the bank pay against documents that do not strictly comply?
Law
Doctrine of strict compliance (Equitable Trust Co. v. Dawson Partners, 1927); UCP 600: banks examine documents on their face; a complying presentation alone obliges payment; the applicant may waive discrepancies.
Apply
The latest shipment date is a term of the credit. A bill of lading dated after it is a discrepancy, however small.
Conclusion
The bank is right to refuse. The seller can ask the buyer to waive the discrepancy (or amend the credit); otherwise he must recover the price from the buyer under the sale contract.

Problem 3. After an LC is opened, the buyer finds that the goods shipped are of poor quality and asks the court to restrain the bank from paying the seller. Advise.

Asked: Practice (Jan 2025: letters of credit)

Issue
Can a court restrain payment under an LC because of a dispute about the goods?
Law
Autonomy principle: an LC is independent of the sale contract UCB v. Bank of India; courts interfere only in cases of established fraud known to the bank, or irretrievable injustice.
Apply
Poor quality is a breach of the sale contract, not fraud in the documents.
Conclusion
The court will not restrain payment. The buyer must pay the bank and sue the seller for breach of contract. If the seller had forged the shipping documents (no goods shipped at all), the fraud exception could apply.

📖 Story to quote: UCB v. Bank of India
