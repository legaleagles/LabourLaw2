Defence and Trial

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

The plaintiff has fired the first shot (the plaint). Now the defendant gets three choices: defend (written statement), hit back (set-off or counter-claim), or stay away (and face an ex parte decree). Whatever he picks, the court then lines up the real points of fight (issues), hears the evidence and decides.

Imagine a cricket match: the plaint and written statement are the two team sheets, the issues are the rules of the match, the hearing is the play, and adjournments are rain breaks. Too many breaks and the match never ends, so the Code now allows a party only three.

Chapter 1 · asked 1× 🔥 Good to know

Written statement (O.VIII r.1–5, 10)

📖 The story: Kailash v. Nanhku

(2005) 4 SCC 480 · decided 6 Apr 2005

Kailash was declared elected to the Uttar Pradesh Legislative Council. Nanhku challenged his election in the High Court through an election petition, where the CPC procedure applies.

Kailash's written statement was due on 3 July 2004. His advocate's clerk was to file it that day but failed to do so; the advocate's nephew had died on 22 June 2004. It was filed on 8 July 2004, five days late, with an application to condone the delay.

The High Court rejected the written statement because it was filed beyond the 90 days allowed by the proviso to Order VIII Rule 1. Kailash appealed to the Supreme Court.

❓ Is the 90-day outer limit for filing a written statement in O.VIII r.1 mandatory, so that a court can never accept it later?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Meaning. The defendant's reply to the plaint: his pleading, admitting or denying each allegation and stating new facts in defence. It is a "pleading" under O.VI r.1, so it states material facts, not evidence.

Time (r.1). Within 30 days from service of summons; the court may allow more time for reasons recorded, but not beyond 90 days. In commercial suits, the outer limit of 120 days is strict.

💡 Case story Kailash v. Nanhku: in an election case, the winning candidate's written statement was filed 5 days late because his advocate's clerk failed to file it (the advocate's nephew had just died). The High Court refused to take it. The Supreme Court took it on record with ₹5,000 costs: the 90-day rule is directory, because "all the rules of procedure are the handmaid of justice", but extensions are an exception, for recorded reasons.

Contents. r.1A: documents relied on are filed with it. r.2: new facts must be specially pleaded, e.g. fraud, limitation, release, payment, that the suit is not maintainable. r.3: denial must be specific; a general denial is not enough. r.4: evasive denial is no denial. r.5: every allegation not denied specifically or by necessary implication is taken as admitted (except against a person under disability).

Failure to file (r.10). If the defendant does not file it within the time allowed, the court shall pronounce judgment against him, or make such order as it thinks fit, and a decree follows.

Other rules. r.7: each defence on separate grounds stated separately. r.8: a new ground arising after the written statement may be raised. r.9: no further pleading after the written statement except with the court's leave (a "replication" by the plaintiff). r.6–6G: set-off and counter-claim (next answer).

Conclusion. Deny specifically, plead new facts expressly, and file in time: what is not denied is admitted.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

No. The provision is procedural and therefore directory, though worded in mandatory form.

The time schedule should ordinarily be followed; extension beyond 90 days is allowed only as an exception, for reasons recorded, where grave injustice would otherwise result. The written statement was taken on record, subject to ₹5,000 costs.

Why it matters:

  • "All the rules of procedure are the handmaid of justice." They are meant to advance justice, not defeat it.
  • The purpose of the amendment was to stop delay, not to punish a defendant for a lapse beyond his control.
  • Extension is an exception: the court must record reasons and may impose costs.
  • In commercial suits the position is different: the 120-day limit is strict (SCG Contracts v. K.S. Chamankar, 2019).

  • Part AWritten statement: 30/90 days + this case (directory).
  • Part BStages of a suit / written statement: mention the time limit and this case. Headings: Meaning → Time (r.1) → Contents and denial → Failure to file (r.10) → Other rules. Memory trick: "30 → 90 → 120": 30 days, at most 90; 120 in commercial suits.
  • Part CDefendant filed the written statement late: the court may accept it with costs, for reasons.

Chapter 2 · asked 16× 🔥🔥🔥 Must do

Set-off and counter-claim: conditions, legal and equitable set-off, differences (O.VIII r.6, r.6A–6G)

📖 The story: Two bills between two traders

A wholesaler sues a retailer for ₹80,000 for goods. The wholesaler already owes the retailer a fixed ₹30,000 refund for returned stock.

❓ What does the law say?

⚖️ The law behind the story

Here is the law that decides it:

Set-off (r.6). A cross-claim for money by the defendant against the plaintiff, which the defendant asks the court to deduct from the plaintiff's claim. It has the same effect as a plaint in a cross-suit, so the court passes one decree for the balance.

Conditions of legal set-off: (1) the suit is for recovery of money; (2) the sum is ascertained; (3) legally recoverable (not time-barred); (4) recoverable by the defendant (all defendants) from the plaintiff (all plaintiffs); (5) within the court's pecuniary limits; (6) both parties fill the same character in both claims.

💡 Illustrations to r.6: A sues B on a bill of exchange for ₹500; B holds a judgment against A for ₹1,000: both are definite money claims, so B can set off. But if B claims unascertained damages for A's failure to insure his goods, he cannot. A and B together sue C for ₹1,000: C cannot set off a debt owed to him by A alone.

Equitable set-off. Allowed by courts even for an unascertained sum, when both claims arise out of the same transaction and it would be unfair to make the defendant pay first and sue later. It is a matter of the court's discretion, not a right. Example: an agent sues for his commission; the principal claims damages for the agent's negligence in the same agency.

Counter-claim (r.6A). The defendant may set up any right or claim against the plaintiff, whether for damages or not, on a cause of action accruing before or after the suit but before he files his defence (or before the time for it expires), within the court's pecuniary limits. It is treated as a plaint; the court passes one final judgment on both claims (r.6A(2)). The counter-claim need not be connected with the plaintiff's cause of action (Jag Mohan Chawla v. Dera Radha Swami Satsang, 1996).

Further rules. r.6B: stated in the written statement. r.6C: the plaintiff may ask that it be excluded and tried as a separate suit (before issues are framed). r.6D: it survives even if the main suit is stayed, discontinued or dismissed. r.6E: if the plaintiff does not reply, judgment may be given on it. r.6F: if the defendant gets the balance, a decree is passed in his favour. r.6G: rules of written statement apply to the reply.

Differences (write as a table):

Set-off · Counter-claim
Naturea shield: reduces the claim · a sword: an independent cross-suit
Suit typeonly money suits · any suit
Claimascertained money (legal) · any claim, money or otherwise
Causeexisting at the suit date · may arise even after the suit, before the defence
Amountcan at most wipe out the claim · can exceed it; decree for the defendant
If suit dismissedusually falls with it · continues (r.6D)

Conclusion. Set-off is a shield, counter-claim is a sword; both save a second suit.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ends

Set-off (r.6): an ascertained money claim by the defendant against the plaintiff, deducted from the claim, with one decree for the balance.

The retailer sets off ₹30,000, and the court passes a decree for ₹50,000.

  • Part AWrite the gold-edged lines above: meaning, section and one example or case.
  • Part BHeadings: Set-off → Conditions → Equitable set-off → Counter-claim → Further rules → Differences. Memory trick: "Shield and Sword": set-off shields, counter-claim strikes.

🪝 Six conditions of legal set-off: money suit, ascertained, recoverable, same parties, within limits, same character.

Chapter 3 · asked 6× 🔥🔥 Important

Ex parte decree: consequences of non-appearance, remedies, setting aside (O.IX)

📖 The story: Kailash v. Nanhku

(2005) 4 SCC 480 · decided 6 Apr 2005

Kailash was declared elected to the Uttar Pradesh Legislative Council. Nanhku challenged his election in the High Court through an election petition, where the CPC procedure applies.

Kailash's written statement was due on 3 July 2004. His advocate's clerk was to file it that day but failed to do so; the advocate's nephew had died on 22 June 2004. It was filed on 8 July 2004, five days late, with an application to condone the delay.

The High Court rejected the written statement because it was filed beyond the 90 days allowed by the proviso to Order VIII Rule 1. Kailash appealed to the Supreme Court.

❓ Is the 90-day outer limit for filing a written statement in O.VIII r.1 mandatory, so that a court can never accept it later?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Meaning. A decree passed in the absence of the defendant, after he was duly served but did not appear (O.IX r.6(1)(a)). It is a valid decree, binding until set aside.

Who is absent, what follows. r.3: neither party appears: suit may be dismissed; fresh suit or restoration allowed (r.4). r.6: only the plaintiff appears and summons was duly served: the court proceeds ex parte; if not duly served, fresh summons. r.7: the defendant may later appear and, on showing good cause, be heard from that stage. r.8: only the defendant appears: suit dismissed (unless he admits part). r.9: then no fresh suit on the same cause; plaintiff may apply to restore it for sufficient cause (Art. 122: 30 days).

Setting aside (r.13). The defendant applies to the court that passed it, within 30 days of the decree, or of his knowledge of it if summons was not duly served (Art. 123), and shows that (1) summons was not duly served, or (2) he was prevented by sufficient cause from appearing. Proviso: a mere irregularity in service is no ground if he knew of the date in time. Explanation: no r.13 application once an appeal against the decree has been decided on any ground other than withdrawal.

Remedies against an ex parte decree: (1) application under O.IX r.13; (2) appeal under S.96(2); (3) review (O.XLVII); (4) a suit to set it aside if obtained by fraud. The r.13 application and the appeal can be pursued side by side (Bhanu Kumar Jain v. Archana Kumar, 2005).

Sufficient cause is read liberally: illness, accident, the advocate's mistake, wrong information about the date. The court may set aside on terms as to costs or payment into court (r.13). Notice to the opposite party is compulsory before setting aside (r.14). If the delay is beyond 30 days, file with an S.5 Limitation Act application Kailash v. Nanhku.

💡 Example: Balu fell ill and missed the hearing; an ex parte decree was passed. He applies under O.IX r.13 with a medical certificate within 30 days. The court sets aside the decree on his paying ₹2,000 costs to the plaintiff, and the suit is heard afresh.

Conclusion. An ex parte decree is not the end: the defendant gets a second chance if he moves quickly and honestly.

🏛️ How the story ended

The time schedule should ordinarily be followed; extension beyond 90 days is allowed only as an exception, for reasons recorded, where grave injustice would otherwise result. The written statement was taken on record, subject to ₹5,000 costs.

Why it matters:

  • "All the rules of procedure are the handmaid of justice." They are meant to advance justice, not defeat it.
  • The purpose of the amendment was to stop delay, not to punish a defendant for a lapse beyond his control.
  • Extension is an exception: the court must record reasons and may impose costs.
  • In commercial suits the position is different: the 120-day limit is strict (SCG Contracts v. K.S. Chamankar, 2019).

Chapter 4 · asked 7× 🔥🔥 Important

Framing of issues · Stages of a suit · Hearing, affidavits and adjournments (O.XIV, O.XVIII, O.XIX, O.XVII)

📖 The story: Salem Advocate Bar Association, Tamil Nadu v. Union of India

(2003) 1 SCC 49 and (2005) 6 SCC 344

Parliament amended the CPC in 1999 and 2002 to cut delay: strict time limits for written statements, a limit of three adjournments, evidence by affidavit, and Section 89 to send cases to arbitration, conciliation, Lok Adalat or mediation.

The Salem Advocate Bar Association in Tamil Nadu challenged these amendments in the Supreme Court as unworkable and unfair.

In 2002 the Court upheld them but set up a committee headed by Justice M. Jagannadha Rao to suggest how they should work in practice. In 2005 it considered the committee's reports.

❓ Are the amendments limiting adjournments and introducing compulsory consideration of ADR valid, and how should courts apply them?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Issues (O.XIV r.1). Issues arise when a material proposition of fact or law is affirmed by one party and denied by the other. Material propositions are those the plaintiff must allege to show a right to sue, or the defendant must allege to make out his defence. Each forms a separate issue: of fact or of law. They are framed at the first hearing, after reading the pleadings and examining the parties.

Materials (r.3). The court frames issues from: (a) allegations on oath by the parties or persons present for them; (b) allegations in the pleadings or answers to interrogatories; (c) contents of documents produced by either party. r.4: it may examine witnesses or documents first. r.5: it may amend, add or strike out issues before the decree.

Preliminary issue (r.2). The court must decide all issues, but where an issue of law on jurisdiction or a legal bar (e.g. limitation, res judicata) can dispose of the suit, it may try that first.

💡 Example: Ravi sues Somu for ₹50,000 on a pro-note. Somu says the signature is forged and the suit is time-barred. Issues: (1) Did Somu execute the pro-note? (2) Is the suit within limitation? (3) To what relief is Ravi entitled? Issue 2 can be tried first under r.2.

Stages of a suit. (1) Plaint (S.26, O.VII) → (2) summons (S.27, O.V) → (3) appearance / ex parte (O.IX) → (4) written statement, set-off, counter-claim (O.VIII) → (5) examination of parties, ADR referral (O.X, S.89) → (6) discovery, admissions, documents (O.XI–XIII) → (7) framing of issues (O.XIV) → (8) witnesses summoned (O.XVI) → (9) hearing and evidence (O.XVIII) → (10) arguments → (11) judgment and decree (O.XX) → (12) appeal / execution.

Hearing (O.XVIII). r.1: the plaintiff has the right to begin, unless the defendant admits the facts and argues only law or new facts. r.3A: a party who wishes to testify appears before his other witnesses. r.4: examination-in-chief by affidavit; cross-examination before the court or a commissioner. r.2: oral arguments, and written arguments with leave. Affidavits (O.XIX): r.1 the court may order any fact proved by affidavit; r.3 affidavits state only what the deponent knows, except in interlocutory applications (belief with grounds).

Adjournments (O.XVII). r.1: granted only for sufficient cause, with costs, and not more than three times to a party during the hearing. r.2: if a party is absent on the adjourned date, the court proceeds under O.IX; where his evidence is largely recorded, it may decide on merits. r.3: if he fails to produce evidence, the court may decide forthwith. Costs: S.35 (costs follow the event), S.35A (compensatory costs up to ₹3,000 for false or vexatious claims), S.35B (costs for causing delay).

💡 Case story Salem Advocate Bar: lawyers in Salem challenged the 1999 and 2002 CPC amendments that limited adjournments and pushed ADR. The Supreme Court upheld them; in the 2005 follow-up it said the three-adjournment limit is the rule, to be crossed only for reasons beyond a party's control, with costs, and approved model rules for S.89 mediation.

Conclusion. Issues focus the fight; the hearing decides it; the limit on adjournments keeps it moving.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

The amendments are valid.

The limit of three adjournments is the rule. A further adjournment can be given only where circumstances are beyond the party's control, with costs, so that the limit is not made meaningless.

The Court approved model rules on ADR and case management for the High Courts to adopt.

Why it matters:

  • Courts must not grant adjournments mechanically; costs should be realistic.
  • Evidence by affidavit and recording of cross-examination by commissioners are valid ways to save time.
  • S.89 makes courts consider settlement through ADR in suitable cases.

  • Part AAdjournments: O.XVII r.1, three adjournments, + this case.
  • Part BStages of a suit / S.89: mention the amendments and this case. Headings: Issues → Materials (r.3) → Preliminary issue → Stages of a suit → Hearing and affidavits → Adjournments and costs. Memory trick: "Plaint → Summons → Statement → Issues → Evidence → Judgment".
  • Part CA party seeks a fourth adjournment: only for reasons beyond his control, with costs.

Part C workshop · 10 marks each

Solve the problem like a judge

Every Part C problem is a new story with the same law. Spot the issue, state the law, apply it to the facts, and conclude. Quote the matching case story to lift your answer.

Problem 1. Agent A sues his master M for arrears. M wants to set off damages for goods damaged by A's negligence. Can he?

Asked: Aug 2025

Issue
Can an unascertained claim for damages be set off against a money claim?
Law
O.VIII r.6: legal set-off needs an ascertained sum. Equitable set-off: allowed for an unascertained sum arising out of the same transaction. O.VIII r.6A: counter-claim for any claim, including damages.
Apply
M's damages are unascertained, so no legal set-off. But they arise from the same agency relationship as A's claim, so equitable set-off can be allowed; alternatively M can file a counter-claim.
Conclusion
Yes, as an equitable set-off (same transaction), or by a counter-claim under O.VIII r.6A; not as a legal set-off.

Problem 2. Ram and Shyam sell wheat (₹10,000) to Sohan and Mohan. Sohan sells cloth (₹12,000) to Shyam and sues him. Shyam claims set-off of the wheat price. Will he succeed?

Asked: May 2023

Issue
Can a debt owed jointly to two persons be set off in a suit by one of the debtors against one of the creditors?
Law
O.VIII r.6: the sum must be recoverable by the defendant from the plaintiff, and both must fill the same character. Illustrations (f) and (g): a debt due from or to one person alone cannot be set off against a joint claim, and vice versa.
Apply
The wheat price is owed by Sohan and Mohan jointly to Ram and Shyam jointly. The cloth price is owed by Shyam alone to Sohan alone. The parties do not fill the same character in the two claims.
Conclusion
No. Shyam cannot set off the wheat price; he must sue for it separately (with Ram, against Sohan and Mohan).

Problem 3. A sues B for a ₹50,000 debt. B files a counter-claim for ₹40,000 for A's breach of another contract. Can he?

Asked: Feb 2025

Issue
Can a counter-claim be based on a different transaction?
Law
O.VIII r.6A: the defendant may set up any right or claim against the plaintiff on a cause of action accruing before he files his defence, whether for damages or not, within pecuniary limits; it need not be connected with the plaintiff's claim.
Apply
B's claim is against the plaintiff A, arose before the defence, and is within limits. The court decides both together and passes a decree for the balance (r.6A(2), r.6F).
Conclusion
Yes. B can file the counter-claim in his written statement; it is treated as a cross-suit.

Problem 4. The defendant did not appear and did not file a written statement. What are the consequences? What if he had filed it and then stayed away?

Asked: 2013

Issue
Consequences of non-appearance, before and after a written statement.
Law
O.IX r.6: if summons duly served, the court proceeds ex parte. O.VIII r.10: failure to file a written statement: judgment may be pronounced. O.XVII r.2 and its Explanation: absence on an adjourned date after pleadings; where his evidence is largely recorded, the court may decide on merits.
Apply
Without a written statement, the plaint allegations stand unrebutted and an ex parte decree follows. With a written statement, the court still proceeds ex parte, but must consider the pleadings on record and decide on the evidence.
Conclusion
He faces an ex parte decree in both cases; his remedy is O.IX r.13 (within 30 days) or an appeal under S.96(2).

Problem 5. A suit was dismissed for default. What remedies does the plaintiff have?

Asked: 2017

Issue
How can a plaintiff revive a suit dismissed for his absence?
Law
O.IX r.8–9: if only the defendant appears, the suit is dismissed and a fresh suit is barred, but the plaintiff may apply to restore it for sufficient cause (Art. 122: 30 days). O.IX r.3–4: if both are absent, fresh suit (within limitation) or restoration. O.XLIII r.1(c): appeal against an order rejecting restoration.
Apply
He applies to restore under r.9 with reasons (illness, wrong date, advocate's mistake); delay beyond 30 days with an S.5 application.
Conclusion
Apply to restore under O.IX r.9 (or r.4); if refused, appeal under O.XLIII r.1(c). A fresh suit is barred after a r.8 dismissal.

Problem 6. The defendant files his written statement on the 100th day after service, citing his advocate's illness. Must the court reject it?

Asked: (standard problem; Part A "Written statement": Nov 2022)

Issue
Is the 90-day limit in O.VIII r.1 an absolute bar?
Law
O.VIII r.1 proviso: 30 days, extendable up to 90 days for recorded reasons. The limit is directory in ordinary civil suits Kailash v. Nanhku; in commercial suits, 120 days is mandatory (Commercial Courts Act, 2015; SCG Contracts v. K.S. Chamankar, 2019).
Apply
In an ordinary suit, a genuine reason (advocate's illness, proved) can justify acceptance as an exception, with costs. In a commercial suit, the court has no power to accept it after 120 days.
Conclusion
In an ordinary suit the court may accept it, for recorded reasons and on costs; in a commercial suit it must refuse after 120 days.

📖 Story to quote: Kailash v. Nanhku

Problem 7. On the day fixed for evidence, the plaintiff asks for a fourth adjournment. What should the court do?

Asked: (Part A "Adjournments": Aug 2021 · Feb 2024)

Issue
Can a party get more than three adjournments?
Law
O.XVII r.1 proviso: no adjournment more than three times to a party during the hearing; r.2: proceed under O.IX if a party is absent; r.3: decide forthwith if he fails to produce evidence. S.35B: costs for delay Salem Advocate Bar.
Apply
A fourth adjournment is allowed only for reasons beyond the plaintiff's control (e.g. sudden hospitalisation), with costs; otherwise the court closes his evidence and proceeds.
Conclusion
Refuse it unless the cause is beyond his control; if granted, impose costs under S.35B; otherwise proceed under O.XVII r.2 or r.3.

📖 Story to quote: Salem Advocate Bar

Problem 8. After issues are framed, the defendant realises no issue was framed on limitation, which he had pleaded. Can he ask for it?

Asked: (Part A "Framing of issues": 2015; B: Aug 2025)

Issue
Can issues be added after they are framed?
Law
O.XIV r.5: the court may amend or frame additional issues at any time before passing a decree. r.2(2): an issue of law on a legal bar (such as limitation) may be tried first. S.3 Lim. Act: the court must examine limitation even if not raised.
Apply
Limitation was pleaded in the written statement, so it is a material proposition in dispute and should have been an issue.
Conclusion
Yes. The court can frame an additional issue under O.XIV r.5, and may try it first as a preliminary issue under r.2.
