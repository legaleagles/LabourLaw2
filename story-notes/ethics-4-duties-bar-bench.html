Duties of an Advocate and Bar-Bench Relations

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

An advocate stands at a crossroads of loyalties. He must fight fearlessly for his client, yet never mislead the court; he must be fair to his opponent and courteous to colleagues; and he owes society a duty to help those who cannot pay. When these pull in different directions, the duty to the court and to justice comes first.

The Bar Council of India has written these duties down as Standards of Professional Conduct and Etiquette (BCI Rules, Part VI, Chapter II, made under S.49(1)(c) of the Advocates Act). Breaking them is professional misconduct.

Chapter 1 · asked 13× 🔥🔥🔥 Must do

Duties of an advocate towards the court, client, opponent, colleagues and the public

📖 The story: Ex-Capt. Harish Uppal v. Union of India

(2003) 2 SCC 45 · decided 17 Dec 2002

Harish Uppal, a former Army officer, had a case that suffered because his lawyers would not appear during repeated strikes by the Bar.

He petitioned the Supreme Court asking it to declare that lawyers have no right to strike, since strikes deny litigants their right to speedy justice. A Constitution Bench heard the matter.

❓ Do lawyers have a right to go on strike or call for a boycott of courts?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Source. The Advocates Act, 1961 empowers the BCI to lay down standards of professional conduct (S.7(1)(b), S.49(1)(c)); these are the BCI Rules, Part VI, Chapter II. Breach is misconduct under S.35.

Duty to the court (Rules 1–10). Act with dignity and self-respect; show respect to the court; not communicate privately with a judge about a pending case or try to influence him; not mislead the court or use illegal or improper means; refuse to act for a client who insists on unfair means; wear the prescribed dress in court; not appear before a judge who is a close relative; not appear in a matter in which he has a pecuniary interest; not stand surety for his client.

Duty to the client (Rules 11–33). Accept a brief in courts he practises in, at his usual fee (r.11); not withdraw without sufficient cause and reasonable notice; disclose any interest or connection; uphold the client's interest fearlessly by fair means (r.15); a prosecutor must not suppress evidence that may prove innocence; keep confidences (r.17; S.132 Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam); not foment litigation; no contingency fee (r.20); not buy any interest in the litigation; keep accounts of client's money (r.25–32); not act for the opposite party in the same matter (r.33).

💡 Example: a client tells his lawyer in confidence that he signed the disputed document. The lawyer must not disclose it, but also must not tell the court that the signature is forged. He can test the other side's evidence and argue the law, but cannot assert what he knows to be false.

Duty to the opponent (Rules 34–35). Not to negotiate directly with a party who is represented by an advocate, except through that advocate; to carry out legitimate promises made to the opposite side.

Duty to colleagues. No soliciting work or advertising, directly or through touts (r.36; a website with basic factual information is permitted); not let his name be used for unauthorised practice; not accept a fee below the minimum taxable fee; not appear in a case where another advocate has filed a vakalat without his consent.

Duty to society (Rule 46). Every advocate must bear in mind that anyone genuinely in need of a lawyer is entitled to legal assistance even if he cannot pay; the advocate should render legal aid within his means. He should also support law reform and uphold the rule of law.

Other restrictions . Not to carry on any business while practising (r.47); not to be a full-time salaried employee of any person or firm while practising (r.49); not to strike or boycott courts Ex-Capt. Harish Uppal.

🏛️ How the story ended

No. Lawyers have no right to strike or to call for a boycott, not even a token strike. Protest may be made by press statements, TV interviews, banners, or wearing arm bands outside court, but without stopping court work.

An advocate who has accepted a brief must attend court; refusal can be professional misconduct, and he may be liable for the client's loss. Only in the rarest cases (e.g. the dignity or independence of the judiciary is at stake) may the Bar abstain, and even then the court decides.

Why it matters:

  • Strikes violate the litigant's right to speedy justice (Art. 21).
  • Courts need not adjourn cases because of a strike.
  • Bar associations should set up grievance mechanisms instead.

Chapter 2 · asked 7× 🔥🔥 Important

Bar-Bench relations

🔁 Flashback: Ex-Capt. Harish Uppal v. Union of India (told earlier on this page). A former Army captain whose case was delayed by repeated lawyers' strikes asked the Supreme Court to declare such strikes illegal. Held: Advocates have no right to go on strike or boycott courts, not even a token strike; at most they may protest peacefully without stopping court work. Abstaining from work can be professional misconduct. 🪝 Lawyers protest, courts work

⚖️ The law behind the story

The law, step by step:

Meaning. The Bar (advocates) and the Bench (judges) are partners in the administration of justice, "two wheels of the same chariot". Judges come largely from the Bar; both need each other's respect for justice to be done quickly and fairly.

Duties of the Bar. Respect the court, accept its rulings (challenge them only by appeal), be punctual and prepared, not abuse or threaten judges, not use the Bar association to pressure courts, avoid strikes and boycotts Ex-Capt. Harish Uppal.

Duties of the Bench. Be patient, courteous and impartial; hear lawyers fully; avoid harsh or humiliating remarks in open court; decide promptly; maintain the dignity of the court without arrogance. Judges are bound by the Restatement of Values of Judicial Life (1997).

Causes of friction. Delays and adjournments, rude conduct on either side, strikes, contempt proceedings, perceived bias, and heavy workloads.

💡 Example: when a judge refused an adjournment, an advocate shouted at him and the local Bar association called a strike. The right path was to seek the remedy in a higher court or through the Bar Council, not to stop the work of all courts.

Remedies. Joint Bar-Bench committees and grievance-redressal committees in courts; Bar Council discipline; judicial training; contempt power used sparingly; the Supreme Court's directions in Harish Uppal to resolve grievances without strikes.
