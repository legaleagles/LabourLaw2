The Legal Profession: Development, Right to Practise and Senior Advocates

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

In 1726 the East India Company's Mayor's Courts were run by merchants, and anyone could plead. Two centuries later, India had barristers, vakils, pleaders and mukhtars, each with different rights in different courts. The Advocates Act, 1961 swept this away and created one class of lawyers: advocates, enrolled with a State Bar Council and entitled to practise across India.

The profession is called noble because it is meant to serve justice, not profit: an advocate cannot advertise, cannot share in the result of a case, and cannot run a business. That is why the Supreme Court has said lawyers are not "service providers" under consumer law, and why foreign law firms cannot simply open shop in India.

Chapter 1 · asked 14× 🔥🔥🔥 Must do

Legal profession: importance, nobility, and its development in India; solicitors' firms

📖 The story: Bar of Indian Lawyers v. D.K. Gandhi PS National Institute of Communicable Diseases

2024 INSC 393 · decided 14 May 2024

A client was unhappy with the way his advocate had handled his case and filed a consumer complaint for deficiency in service.

The National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission held that advocates are service providers and can be sued under consumer law. Bar associations appealed to the Supreme Court.

❓ Can an advocate be sued as a "service provider" for deficiency in service under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Importance. Lawyers make the rule of law real: they protect rights and liberty, help courts reach the truth, give legal advice, draft laws and documents, and give access to justice to the weak. Many leaders of the freedom struggle (Gandhi, Nehru, Patel, Ambedkar) were lawyers.

A noble profession, not a trade. The aim is service of justice, not profit. Hence the restrictions: no advertising or soliciting (BCI Rule 36), no contingency fees (Rule 20), no business while practising (Rule 47), duty to render legal aid (Rule 46). Lawyers' services are not treated as consumer "services" D.K. Gandhi.

Company period. The Mayor's Courts (1726) had no trained lawyers. The Regulating Act, 1773 set up the Supreme Court at Calcutta (1774), which enrolled English barristers (advocates) and attorneys. Bengal Regulation VII of 1793 first created a regular profession of vakils (pleaders) in the Company's courts.

Legal Practitioners Acts. The Act of 1846 opened the profession to all, regardless of nationality or religion. After the High Courts (1862), the Legal Practitioners Act, 1879 recognised several grades: advocates, vakils, pleaders, mukhtars and revenue agents, each with different rights.

Indian Bar Councils Act, 1926. On the Chamier Committee's report, Bar Councils were created for each High Court, giving the Bar some self-government, but they were only advisory and the grades remained.

Advocates Act, 1961. On the All India Bar Committee (1951) and the 14th Report of the Law Commission (1958), Parliament created a unified, autonomous All-India Bar: one class of advocates (S.29), State Bar Councils and the Bar Council of India, a common roll, disciplinary powers, and the right to practise in all courts (S.30).

Solicitors' firms. On the original side of the Bombay and Calcutta High Courts, a dual system continued: attorneys (solicitors) took instructions from clients and briefed counsel who argued. Solicitors' firms are partnerships of such attorneys, many over a century old, doing litigation and corporate work. Today they are all advocates under the Act; firms of advocates may be partnerships but cannot advertise or practise with non-lawyers.

After 1961. BCI Rules on conduct (1975); five-year integrated LL.B. and National Law Schools (from 1987); the All India Bar Examination (introduced in 2010, upheld in 2023); S.30 brought into force (2011); rules for foreign lawyers (notified 2023) after A.K. Balaji; growth of corporate law firms, legal aid and online courts.

🏛️ How the story ended

No. The legal profession is sui generis: its work depends on skill and judgment, and much of it is beyond the lawyer's control (the court decides).

The relationship between advocate and client involves direct and substantial control by the client, making it a contract of personal service, which is excluded from the definition of 'service'. Clients can still sue for negligence in civil courts or complain to the Bar Council.

Why it matters:

  • Supports the view that law is a profession, not a business.
  • Professional misconduct remains punishable under S.35 Advocates Act.
  • The Court also suggested the medical-profession precedent (V.P. Shantha) be reconsidered by a larger bench.

📚 More stories on this topic

Bar Council of India v. A.K. Balaji: Foreign law firms were advising clients in India and taking part in arbitrations without being enrolled under the Advocates Act. Held: "Practice of law" covers litigation and non-litigation work; foreign lawyers cannot practise Indian law in India. They may advise on foreign law on a casual "fly in and fly out" basis, and appear in international commercial arbitrations, subject to rules. 🪝 Indian law is for Indian advocates

🪝 "A lawyer is not a shopkeeper."

Right to practise: a right or a privilege? Art. 19(1)(g), S.29, S.30, S.33

🔁 Flashback: Bar of Indian Lawyers v. D.K. Gandhi PS National Institute of Communicable Diseases (told earlier on this page). A client filed a consumer complaint against his lawyer for deficiency in service, and the National Commission held advocates liable under consumer law. Held: The legal profession is sui generis; an advocate's service is a contract of personal service and is outside the Consumer Protection Act, 2019. Remedies lie in civil courts and Bar Council proceedings. 🪝 A lawyer is not a shopkeeper

⚖️ The law behind the story

The law, step by step:

Constitutional base. Art. 19(1)(g) guarantees every citizen the freedom to practise any profession, subject to reasonable restrictions and prescribed qualifications under Art. 19(6). So the State may require a law degree, enrolment, and good conduct.

Statutory right. S.29: advocates are the only recognised class entitled to practise law. S.30: every advocate on the State roll is entitled as of right to practise in all courts including the Supreme Court, tribunals and before any authority (in force from 15 June 2011). The right is subject to court rules (e.g. Advocate-on-Record system in the Supreme Court) and to S.34 (High Court rules).

Only advocates (S.33, S.45). No person may practise in any court or before any authority unless enrolled as an advocate. Illegal practice is punishable with imprisonment up to six months (S.45). A court may still permit a party in person, or a non-advocate in special cases (S.32).

Right or privilege. For an enrolled advocate, practice is a statutory right (S.30), but it is also called a privilege because it is earned by qualification and kept only by good conduct: Bar Councils can suspend or remove an advocate (S.35), and courts control appearance before them. With the right come duties to the court, client and society.

💡 Example: a law graduate who has not enrolled argues a bail application before a Magistrate on behalf of a friend. That is illegal practice (S.33, S.45). Once enrolled, he may appear in any court in India, from a Munsif court to the Supreme Court (subject to its Advocate-on-Record rules for filing).

Not a business or industry. Practice of law is a profession, not a trade: advocates cannot advertise, cannot share fees with non-lawyers, and are not "service providers" under consumer law D.K. Gandhi. Foreign firms cannot practise Indian law A.K. Balaji.

Chapter 3 · asked 3× 🔥 Good to know

Senior advocates: designation and restrictions

⚖️ The law behind the story

The law, step by step:

Designation (S.16). Advocates are of two classes: senior advocates and other advocates. An advocate may, with his consent, be designated a senior advocate by the Supreme Court or a High Court if it is of opinion that, by virtue of his ability, standing at the Bar, or special knowledge or experience in law, he deserves the distinction.

Procedure. The Supreme Court laid down a transparent process in Indira Jaising v. Supreme Court of India (2017): a Permanent Committee and assessment of years of practice, judgments, publications and reputation, with the final decision by the Full Court; the process has since been revised by the Court.

Restrictions (BCI Rules, Part VI, Chapter I). A senior advocate shall not file a vakalatnama or act in any court; shall not appear without an advocate-on-record or junior; shall not draft pleadings or affidavits or do similar drafting work (he may settle them); and shall not accept instructions directly from a client; he may give advice on evidence and argue.

💡 Example: a client wants a famous senior advocate to argue her appeal. She must first engage an ordinary advocate (or AOR in the Supreme Court), who files the vakalat and briefs the senior; the senior then argues.
