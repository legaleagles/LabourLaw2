Juvenile Justice

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

When a 15-year-old breaks the law, the question is not "how do we punish him?" but "how do we bring him back?". The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 treats a child as a child: no police lock-up, no jail, no death sentence, no life sentence without release, and a fresh start with no record that follows him.

The Act has two doors. A child in conflict with law goes to the Juvenile Justice Board (JJB). A child in need of care and protection (orphan, street child, abused child) goes to the Child Welfare Committee (CWC). Only for a heinous offence by a 16–18-year-old may the child, after a preliminary assessment, be tried as an adult by the Children's Court.

Chapter 1 · asked 7× 🔥🔥 Important

Salient features of the JJ Act, 2015 · Definitions · Principles · Role of judiciary

⚡ 30-second version · Shilpa Mittal

📍A 17-year-old driving his father's Mercedes at high speed killed a young man; the offence charged had a 10-year maximum but no minimum.
❓Is an offence with a maximum sentence above 7 years, but no minimum sentence, a "heinous offence" under the JJ Act?
🏛️Not a heinous offence (which needs a minimum of 7 years), so he could not be tried as an adult; to be treated as a serious offence.

🪝 Heinous needs a minimum

📖 Read the full story 3 short paragraphs

Shilpa Mittal v. State (NCT of Delhi) · (2020) 2 SCC 787 · decided Jan 2020

In Delhi, a boy aged between 16 and 18, driving his father's Mercedes at excessive speed, ran over and killed a young man. The car already had traffic violations on record.

He was charged with culpable homicide not amounting to murder, an offence with a maximum of 10 years but no minimum sentence. The Juvenile Justice Board treated it as heinous and ordered him to be tried as an adult; the Children's Court agreed.

The Delhi High Court held it was not heinous. The victim's sister appealed to the Supreme Court.

⚖️ The law you must know Part A lines

Background

The 2015 Act replaced the JJ Act, 2000. After the 2012 Delhi gang-rape case, in which one accused was a juvenile, Parliament allowed children of 16–18 accused of heinous offences to be tried as adults in some cases. It also consolidated the law on adoption. It was amended in 2021 (adoption orders now by the District Magistrate; wider "serious offences").

Key definitions (S.2)

Child: below 18. Child in conflict with law: alleged or found to have committed an offence while under 18. Child in need of care and protection: homeless, begging, abused, orphaned, at risk of child marriage, etc. Offences: petty (maximum up to 3 years), serious (3 to 7 years; since 2021 also offences with a maximum above 7 years but no minimum or a minimum below 7), heinous (minimum 7 years or more).

General principles (S.3)

Sixteen principles, including: presumption of innocence, dignity, participation, best interest of the child, family responsibility, safety, non-stigmatising semantics, privacy, institutionalisation as a last resort, repatriation, fresh start, diversion and natural justice.

Two bodies

The Juvenile Justice Board (S.4) deals with children in conflict with law; the Child Welfare Committee (S.27) deals with children in need of care and protection. Both must sit in child-friendly premises, not in a regular courtroom.

Children of 16–18 and heinous offences

The Board makes a preliminary assessment of the child's mental and physical capacity, ability to understand consequences, and the circumstances (S.15). If it finds trial as an adult necessary, it transfers the case to the Children's Court (S.18(3), S.19). Even then: no death sentence or life without release (S.21), and he stays in a place of safety till 21.

➕ More for Part B and examples 1 more point
Safeguards and role of the judiciary

S.10: no lock-up or jail; S.12: bail as a rule; S.23: no joint trial with adults; S.24: no disqualification from the finding; S.74: identity must not be disclosed in the media; S.75: cruelty to a child is an offence. Courts decide age (S.94), hear appeals (S.101), and supervise Boards; a juvenility claim can be raised before any court, at any stage, even after the case ends (S.9(2)).

🏛️ Why the court decided so

No. A heinous offence is one with a minimum sentence of 7 years or more; an offence without such a minimum cannot be heinous.

Using Article 142, the Court directed that such offences be treated as "serious offences" until Parliament acted.

  • Courts cannot rewrite a definition the legislature has drawn; the gap had to be filled by Parliament.
  • Parliament amended the Act in 2021: offences with a maximum above 7 years and no minimum (or a minimum below 7) are "serious offences".
  • Only heinous offences by 16–18-year-olds can lead to trial as an adult.

Chapter 2 · asked 3× 🔥 Good to know

Juvenile Justice Board: composition, powers and functions (S.4–9) · Child Welfare Committee (S.27–30)

⚡ 30-second version · Who sits on the Board?

📍A district sets up its Juvenile Justice Board.
⚖️S.4: a Judicial Magistrate first class with 3 years' experience as Principal Magistrate, plus two social workers, at least one a woman.
✅Three members: one judicial officer and two child-welfare experts, one of them a woman.

⚖️ The law you must know Part A lines

Composition (S.4)

A Metropolitan Magistrate or Judicial Magistrate of the first class (not the Chief Magistrate) with at least 3 years' experience, as the Principal Magistrate, and two social workers, at least one a woman, with 7 years' work in child welfare or a professional degree in child psychology, psychiatry, sociology or law.

Powers (S.8)

The Board has the powers of a Magistrate under the BNSS, and exclusive power to deal with children in conflict with law in its area. A Magistrate who is not a Board must forward the child to the Board (S.9(1)).

Functions (S.8(3))

Ensure the informed participation of the child and parents; respect the child's rights; provide legal aid; provide an interpreter; direct the Probation Officer to prepare a social investigation report within 15 days; decide bail (S.12); conduct the inquiry (S.14) and pass orders (S.18); inspect observation homes and special homes regularly.

➕ More for Part B and examples 3 more points
Child Welfare Committee (S.27)

A Chairperson and four members, at least one a woman and one an expert on children. It is the final authority on care, protection, treatment, development and rehabilitation of children in need; it can place a child in a children's home, with a fit person, or in foster care, and declare a child legally free for adoption. A child must be produced before it within 24 hours (S.31).

Age determination (S.94)

The Board or Committee goes by: (i) the school or matriculation date-of-birth certificate; failing that (ii) a birth certificate from a corporation, municipality or panchayat; only in their absence (iii) an ossification or other medical test, completed within 15 days.

💡 ExampleExample: a 15-year-old is caught for a mobile phone theft. Police produce him before the Board within 24 hours; he is released on bail to his mother (S.12); the Probation Officer reports on his family and school; the Board advises him and orders 2 months' community service (S.18). No criminal record follows him (S.24).

Chapter 3 · asked 7× 🔥🔥 Important

Rehabilitation and social reintegration of children (S.18, S.39–55)

⚡ 30-second version · Pratap Singh

📍An accused in a murder case was under 18 on the date of the offence but over 18 when produced before the court.
❓For deciding whether an accused is a juvenile, which date matters: the date of the offence or the date he is produced in court?
🏛️The relevant date for deciding juvenility is the date of the offence, not the date of production or trial.

🪝 The clock stops on the day of the crime

📖 Read the full story 2 short paragraphs

Pratap Singh v. State of Jharkhand · (2005) 3 SCC 551 · decided 2 Feb 2005

Pratap Singh was accused of murder. He claimed that he was a juvenile on the date of the offence, though he had crossed the juvenile age by the time he was produced before the court.

Earlier decisions of the Supreme Court had differed on which date mattered: the date of the offence, or the date the accused is first produced before the court. The question went to a Constitution Bench, along with whether the new JJ Act, 2000 applied to pending cases.

⚖️ The law you must know Part A lines

Aim (S.39)

Rehabilitation and social reintegration, primarily through family-based care (restoration to the family, adoption, foster care, sponsorship); institutional care is the last resort.

Orders by the JJB (S.18)

(a) send home after advice or admonition; (b) group counselling; (c) community service; (d) fine, payable by the child or parents (subject to labour laws); (e) release on probation of good conduct for up to 3 years under a parent or fit person; (f) probation under a fit facility; (g) a special home for up to 3 years for reformative services, education and skills.

Institutional care

Observation homes (S.47) for children during the inquiry; special homes (S.48) for children found to have committed an offence; a place of safety (S.49) for those aged 16–18 accused or found guilty of heinous offences; children's homes (S.50) and specialised adoption agencies for children in need of care.

➕ More for Part B and examples 2 more points
Non-institutional care

Adoption (S.56–73; adoption orders now by the District Magistrate), foster care (S.44), sponsorship (S.45: financial support to families for education and health), and after-care.

After-care (S.46)

A child leaving an institution at 18 may be given financial support and help up to 21 to rejoin society: education, skills, a place to stay.

🏛️ Why the court decided so

The date of commission of the offence is the reckoning date.

The JJ Act, 2000 also applied to proceedings pending when it came into force, where the person had not completed 18 years on that date.

  • A child's protection cannot depend on how quickly the police catch him.
  • The JJ Act, 2015 now says this expressly: a child in conflict with law is one below 18 on the date of the offence (S.2(13)), and a person caught after 18 is still dealt with as a child (S.6).
  • A claim of juvenility can be raised at any stage, even after disposal of the case (S.9(2)).

Part C workshop · 10 marks each

Solve the problem like a judge

Every Part C problem is a new story with the same law. Spot the issue, state the law, apply it to the facts, and conclude. Quote the matching case story to lift your answer.

Problem 1. A boy of 17 commits a serious offence; he is caught at 19 (or 21) and tried and convicted by an ordinary criminal court. Is it legal? What is the correct procedure?

Asked: 2018 · Aug 2025

🤔 Try it in your head, then tap to see the answer
Issue
Is a person who was a child on the date of the offence entitled to be dealt with under the JJ Act though now an adult?
Law
S.2(13): a child in conflict with law is one under 18 on the date of the offence Pratap Singh. S.6: a person apprehended after 18 for an offence committed under 18 is dealt with as a child. S.9: any court must forward such a person to the JJB; the claim can be raised at any stage. S.94: age determination.
Apply
He was 17 on the date of the offence, so the ordinary criminal court had no power to try him. The Board must inquire; if the offence is serious, the JJB orders under S.18; if heinous and he was 16–18, preliminary assessment (S.15) decides whether the Children's Court may try him.
Conclusion
The conviction by the ordinary court is illegal; the case must go to the JJB, which deals with him under the JJ Act (special home or other orders; no death or life without release).

📖 Story to quote: Pratap Singh

Problem 2. A boy aged 16 years 6 months is involved in kidnapping and murder of a woman. Is he punishable? By which court? What punishment?

Asked: 2016

🤔 Try it in your head, then tap to see the answer
Issue
How is a 16–18-year-old accused of a heinous offence dealt with?
Law
Murder (BNS 103) is heinous (minimum life). S.15: preliminary assessment by the JJB within 3 months. S.18(3), S.19: transfer to the Children's Court, which may try him as an adult. S.21: no death or life imprisonment without possibility of release.
Apply
If the JJB finds he had the mental and physical capacity and understood the consequences, the Children's Court tries him as an adult; else the JJB inquires and may send him to a special home for up to 3 years.
Conclusion
He may be tried as an adult by the Children's Court after preliminary assessment, but he cannot get death or life without release; he stays in a place of safety till 21.

Problem 3. Two boys aged 4 and 5 break open shop locks to steal; on the way back they injure a man who dies a week later. Are they punishable?

Asked: 2020 (also 2013: 12-year-old steals from his father's pocket)

🤔 Try it in your head, then tap to see the answer
Issue
Are very young children criminally responsible?
Law
BNS 20: nothing is an offence which is done by a child under 7 years. BNS 21: nothing is an offence by a child of 7 to 12 who has not attained sufficient maturity of understanding to judge the nature and consequences of his conduct.
Apply
Both boys are under 7, so whatever they did is not an offence. They are children in need of care and protection, to be produced before the CWC. (A 12-year-old: if he had sufficient maturity, the JJB deals with him for a petty offence.)
Conclusion
Not punishable (BNS 20); they go to the Child Welfare Committee for care and protection, not to any court.

Problem 4. X (14) and Z (19) were jointly tried by a Sessions Court for murdering a constable and acquitted. Was the trial proper?

Asked: 2019

🤔 Try it in your head, then tap to see the answer
Issue
Can a child and an adult be tried together?
Law
S.23: no joint proceedings of a child in conflict with law with a person who is not a child. S.8: the JJB has exclusive jurisdiction over the child. Murder by a 14-year-old cannot be tried as an adult (S.15 applies only to 16–18).
Apply
X's trial by the Sessions Court was without jurisdiction; Z (adult) could be tried by the Sessions Court separately.
Conclusion
The joint trial was improper as to X: his case had to be inquired into by the JJB; Z alone could be tried by the Sessions Court.

Problem 5. A boy of 13 committed chain-snatching, theft of a laptop and a purse within one year. How are "charges" framed, which forum, what punishment?

Asked: Feb 2024

🤔 Try it in your head, then tap to see the answer
Issue
How does the law deal with repeated offences by a 13-year-old?
Law
Snatching (BNS 304) and theft (BNS 303(2)) are punishable up to 3 years: petty offences. The JJB conducts an inquiry, not a trial; there is no charge-sheet "conviction" (S.14). Orders under S.18.
Apply
The Board holds one inquiry covering all three incidents, calls for a social investigation report, and decides the most suitable order.
Conclusion
The JJB (not a criminal court) inquires; it may order counselling, community service, probation, or at most a special home for up to 3 years; no "punishment" as for adults.
