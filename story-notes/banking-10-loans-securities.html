Loans, Advances and Securities

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

A bank lends other people's money. Every loan carries a silent question: "Will it come back?" So a good banker asks five things before lending: Who is the borrower (character)? Can he repay (capacity)? How much of his own money is in it (capital)? What can we fall back on (collateral)? What is happening in his business and the economy (conditions)?

The fallback is the security, and the law gives a menu: hand over gold or goods (pledge), keep the goods but give the bank a charge (hypothecation), give an interest in land or a house (mortgage), or transfer a right such as an insurance policy (assignment). Each comes with its own rules, and getting them wrong can leave the bank with nothing.

Chapter 1 · asked 2× 🔥 Good to know

Principles of sound lending and precautions while granting loans and advances

📖 The story: Will the money come back?

A first-time borrower asks for ₹10 lakh to open a restaurant. He has a poor credit report and no security.

❓ What does the law say?

⚖️ The law behind the story

Here is the law that decides it:

Safety. The first rule: the money must come back. The bank checks the borrower's honesty, repaying capacity and security.

Liquidity. Deposits are repayable on demand, so loans must be recoverable in time; a bank should not lock too much money in long, illiquid loans.

Profitability. The interest must cover the cost of deposits, expenses and risk; but profit must never override safety.

Purpose. The loan should be for a productive, lawful purpose that itself generates repayment; the bank must watch the end use.

Diversification. "Don't put all eggs in one basket": spread loans across borrowers, sectors and regions, within RBI's exposure limits, and meet priority-sector targets.

Precautions (the 5 Cs). Character (KYC, credit bureau report, past record); capacity (income, cash flows, project viability); capital (borrower's own stake, margin); collateral (title search, valuation, proper documentation and registration, insurance); conditions (industry and economy). After sanction: monitor end use, inspect stocks, review accounts, and act early on SMA signals. RBI bars loans to the bank's own directors (S.20 BR Act).

💡 Example: before lending ₹30 lakh for a house, the bank checks the buyer's salary slips and credit score (character, capacity), insists on a 20% down payment (capital), gets a 30-year title search and registers the mortgage (collateral), and checks that the builder's project is approved (conditions).

When lending goes wrong. Poor appraisal, over-optimism about big projects, weak governance and "evergreening" (fresh loans to repay old ones) turned many large loans into NPAs; after RBI's Asset Quality Review (2015) banks had to recognise them. The burden falls on everyone: banks charge higher fees and spreads, depositors earn less, and taxpayers fund recapitalisation. Remedies: better appraisal and early-warning systems, independent boards, RBI's prompt corrective action, the IBC, and wilful-defaulter rules.

🏛️ How the story ends

Safety is the first rule: the bank checks honesty, repaying capacity and security.

The bank will refuse or reduce the loan until safety is shown.

Chapter 2 · asked 5× 🔥🔥 Important

Modes of creating a charge: lien, pledge, hypothecation, mortgage and assignment

📖 The story: Lallan Prasad v. Rahmat Ali

AIR 1967 SC 1322 · decided 13 Dec 1966

Rahmat Ali borrowed ₹20,000 from Lallan Prasad and pledged a large quantity of aero scrap as security; he also signed a promissory note.

Rahmat Ali did not repay. Lallan Prasad sued to recover the loan, but he was not in a position to return the pledged scrap to Rahmat Ali.

❓ Can a pawnee sue for the debt without being able to return the pledged goods?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

Lien. Right to retain the debtor's goods until paid; the banker's general lien (S.171 Contract Act) is an implied pledge (see Cluster 7).

Pledge (S.172 Contract Act). Bailment of goods as security for a debt; the goods are delivered (actually or by handing over documents of title) to the pawnee. Pawnee's rights: retain for the debt, interest and expenses (S.173, S.174); on default, sue for the debt (keeping the goods as collateral) or sell after reasonable notice (S.176). Pawnor's right: redeem before sale (S.177). A pawnee who sues must be able to return the goods Lallan Prasad.

Hypothecation. A charge on movable property without delivery (SARFAESI S.2(1)(n)): the borrower keeps and uses the goods (a car, a truck, shop stock) and the bank has a charge, with the right to take possession on default. It is registered with CERSAI (and the Registrar of Companies for companies).

Mortgage (S.58 TPA). Transfer of an interest in specific immovable property to secure a loan. Kinds: simple; by conditional sale; usufructuary; English; by deposit of title deeds (equitable mortgage, common in bank lending, in notified towns: S.58(f)); anomalous. The mortgagor may redeem (S.60); the mortgagee may sue for sale or foreclosure (S.67), in some cases sell without court (S.69), and banks can use SARFAESI.

💡 Example: a gold loan is a pledge (gold kept by the bank); a car loan is a hypothecation (car with the owner, bank's charge noted in the RC); a home loan is a mortgage (title deeds deposited with the bank).

Assignment. Transfer of an actionable claim to the bank (S.130 TPA): book debts, receivables, and life insurance policies (assigned under S.38 Insurance Act and registered with the insurer).

Secured creditor (S.2(1)(zd) SARFAESI). A bank, financial institution, ARC, debenture trustee and the like, in whose favour a security interest (mortgage, charge, hypothecation, assignment) is created. Only a secured creditor can enforce under S.13.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

A pawnee has two remedies on default: sue for the debt keeping the goods as collateral, or sell the goods after reasonable notice (S.176).

If he sues for the debt, he must be ready and able to return the goods on being paid. As Lallan Prasad could not restore the scrap, he could not recover the loan.

Why it matters:

  • Pledge is a bailment: the pawnee holds the goods in trust for return on payment.
  • Sale without notice, or loss of the goods, can defeat the pawnee's claim.
  • Applies to banks: gold loans, pledged goods, pledged shares.

Chapter 3 · asked 3× 🔥 Good to know

Advances against goods, documents of title, shares, life policies and land

📖 The story: Loan against rice stock

A rice mill borrows against its stock in a godown.

❓ What does the law say?

⚖️ The law behind the story

Here is the law that decides it:

Goods. By pledge or hypothecation. Check ownership, quality and marketability; keep a margin for price falls; insure them; inspect godowns; avoid perishable goods.

Documents of title to goods. Bill of lading, warehouse receipt, railway receipt, delivery order (S.2(4) Sale of Goods Act). Delivering the document is constructive delivery of the goods, so the bank becomes pawnee. Check genuineness and that the goods are insured.

Shares. Today almost all shares are in demat form and are pledged through the depository. Merits: easily valued and sold. Risks: price falls, partly-paid shares, company's own lien. Precautions: lend only on listed, fully paid shares, keep an adequate margin, follow RBI and SEBI limits.

Preference shares and their redemption (S.55 Companies Act, 2013). A company can issue only redeemable preference shares, redeemable within 20 years (longer for infrastructure projects). They may be redeemed only if fully paid, and only out of profits available for dividend or the proceeds of a fresh issue; when redeemed out of profits, an equal sum goes to the capital redemption reserve.

Life policies. The bank lends up to the surrender value; the policy is assigned to the bank (S.38 Insurance Act) and notice given to the insurer; premiums must be kept paid.

Land and buildings. By mortgage. Precautions: title search for many years, encumbrance certificate, valuation, approved plans, registration of the mortgage, insurance of the building. Agricultural land is outside SARFAESI.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ends

Advances against goods by pledge or hypothecation: check ownership and quality, keep a margin for price falls, insure, inspect godowns, avoid perishables.

The bank lends only part of the stock's value and inspects the godown regularly.

Part C workshop · 10 marks each

Solve the problem like a judge

Every Part C problem is a new story with the same law. Spot the issue, state the law, apply it to the facts, and conclude. Quote the matching case story to lift your answer.

Problem 1. A house stands in Malati's name. After marriage, her husband, without her knowledge, mortgaged it to a bank. Later she sold the house to someone and registered the sale. Advise the bank on recovering the debt.

Asked: May 2023

Issue
Is the mortgage valid against Malati and her purchaser, and what can the bank do?
Law
S.58 TPA: only a person entitled to the property (or authorised by him) can mortgage it; S.7 TPA: competence to transfer. "Nemo dat": no one can give a better title than he has. S.41 TPA protects a transferee from an ostensible owner only where the real owner consented. S.43 TPA: if the husband later acquires the property, the mortgage may attach to it. Bank's duty: title search before lending.
Apply
Malati is the owner and did not consent; the husband had no title. The mortgage does not bind her or the person who bought from her, and the bank was careless in not checking title.
Conclusion
The bank cannot enforce the mortgage against the house or the purchaser. Its remedy is against the husband: sue him for the debt (DRT if ₹20 lakh or more), proceed against his other assets, and lodge a criminal complaint for cheating and forgery (BNS 318, 336). If Malati had consented or signed, the position would differ.

Problem 2. Udit takes a personal loan from Bank of Paka, pays two EMIs and stops paying. He ignores notices. How can the bank recover? Can it complain against him in a consumer court?

Asked: Aug 2025

Issue
What are a bank's remedies on an unsecured loan, and is the consumer forum available to it?
Law
No security interest, so SARFAESI does not apply. Remedies: set-off against Udit's deposits; civil suit (summary suit under O.XXXVII CPC on a written contract); DRT if ₹20 lakh or more; pre-litigation Lok Adalat (award is final, Legal Services Authorities Act S.21); arbitration if agreed; S.138 NI Act or S.25 Payment and Settlement Systems Act if EMI cheques or e-mandates bounce; reporting to credit bureaus. RBI rules forbid harassment by recovery agents. Consumer Protection Act, 2019: a "consumer" is one who buys goods or avails services; the bank is the service provider.
Apply
The bank must use lawful recovery routes, not force. It is not a consumer in relation to its own loan; consumer commissions decide complaints against service providers, not recovery claims.
Conclusion
The bank can recall the loan, set off his deposits, sue (or use Lok Adalat, arbitration or the DRT if the amount qualifies) and prosecute for bounced EMI cheques or mandates. It cannot go to a consumer court; Udit, not the bank, is the consumer.

Problem 3. A bank sold gold ornaments pledged for a gold loan two weeks after default, without informing the borrower. Advise the borrower.

Asked: Practice (pledge: Nov 2022, May 2023, Aug 2023)

Issue
Can a pawnee sell pledged goods without notice?
Law
S.176 Contract Act: on default the pawnee may sell the goods after giving the pawnor reasonable notice of the sale; S.177: the pawnor may redeem before the actual sale. RBI's gold loan rules also require prior notice before auction Lallan Prasad.
Apply
No notice was given, so the borrower lost his right to redeem. The sale is wrongful as against him.
Conclusion
The borrower can claim damages from the bank (the market value of the gold minus the dues) and complain to the RBI Ombudsman; the bank cannot recover any shortfall without accounting for the gold's true value.
