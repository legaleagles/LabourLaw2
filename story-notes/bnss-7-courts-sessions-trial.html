Criminal Courts and Sessions Trial

Every topic told as a story: the case first, then the law it turned on, then how it ended, then how to write it for marks.

Criminal courts are a staircase. At the bottom, the Judicial Magistrate of the second class (up to 1 year); above him, the first class Magistrate (up to 3 years); then the Chief Judicial Magistrate (up to 7 years); and at the top of the district, the Court of Session, which can pass any sentence, even death (but death needs the High Court's confirmation). The First Schedule tells you which step each offence climbs to.

A Sessions trial is like a play in acts: the Magistrate commits the case, the Public Prosecutor opens it, the judge frames the charge (or discharges), the prosecution and defence lead evidence, both argue, and the judge delivers judgment within 30 days (extendable to 45).

Chapter 1 · asked 6× 🔥🔥 Important

Classes of criminal courts and their powers: Court of Session, Sessions Judge, Chief Judicial Magistrate (S.6–23)

📖 The story: Which court is which?

A law student visits a district court complex and sees a Sessions Court, a Judicial Magistrate's court and the Collector's office.

❓ What does the law say?

⚖️ The law behind the story

Here is the law that decides it:

Classes (S.6). Besides the High Courts and courts under other laws, there are: (1) Courts of Session; (2) Judicial Magistrates of the first class; (3) Judicial Magistrates of the second class; (4) Executive Magistrates. (The BNSS has dropped the separate category of Metropolitan Magistrates.)

Court of Session (S.8). One for every sessions division, presided over by a Sessions Judge appointed by the High Court; Additional Sessions Judges may also be appointed. It tries the most serious offences, mostly on committal by a Magistrate (S.213).

Magistrates. In every district, a Chief Judicial Magistrate heads the Judicial Magistrates; Judicial Magistrates of the first and second class are appointed by the High Court in consultation with the State. Special Judicial Magistrates may be conferred powers for particular cases.

Sentencing powers (S.22–23). The High Court may pass any sentence authorised by law. The Sessions Judge may pass any sentence authorised by law, but a death sentence is subject to confirmation by the High Court (S.407). The CJM: any sentence except death, life, or imprisonment above 7 years. JMFC: up to 3 years, fine up to ₹50,000, or both, or community service. JMSC: up to 1 year, fine up to ₹10,000, or both, or community service. For several offences at one trial, sentences may run consecutively within limits (S.25).

Executive Magistrates. The District Magistrate, Additional DM, Sub-Divisional Magistrates and others appointed by the State. They do not try offences; they keep the public peace (e.g. dispersal of unlawful assemblies, S.148–151; orders in urgent cases of nuisance, S.163), and hold inquests in certain cases.

💡 Example: a theft (BNS 303(2), up to 3 years) is triable by any Magistrate; cheating (BNS 318(4), up to 7 years) by a JMFC; murder (BNS 103) and rape (BNS 64) only by the Court of Session. Check the sixth column of the First Schedule.

🏛️ How the story ends

S.6: Courts of Session, Judicial Magistrates of the first and second class, and Executive Magistrates (the BNSS drops separate Metropolitan Magistrates).

The Collector sits as an Executive Magistrate; the other two are judicial courts.

Chapter 2 · asked 11× 🔥🔥🔥 Must do

Inquiry, investigation and trial · Venue and place of trial (S.197–209)

📖 The story: Police collecting evidence

After a burglary FIR, police lift fingerprints, record statements and recover stolen items.

❓ What does the law say?

⚖️ The law behind the story

Here is the law that decides it:

Investigation. Collection of evidence by the police (or a person authorised by a Magistrate); it ends with the police report (S.193).

Inquiry (S.2). Every inquiry, other than a trial, conducted by a Magistrate or Court: e.g. inquiry before issuing process on a complaint (S.225), inquiry into maintenance (S.144), into security for keeping the peace, or the committal stage.

Trial. Not defined; it is the judicial examination of the guilt or innocence of the accused, which begins with framing of the charge (or in summons cases, stating the accusation) and ends in acquittal or conviction. Kinds: Sessions trial, warrant-case trial, summons-case trial, summary trial.

Ordinary place (S.197). Every offence is ordinarily inquired into and tried by a court within whose local jurisdiction it was committed.

Special rules. Where it is uncertain in which area the offence was committed, or it was committed partly in one area and partly in another, or is a continuing offence, it may be tried in any of those areas (S.198). Where an act is done in one area and its consequence follows in another, in either (S.199). Kidnapping or abduction: where the person was kidnapped, conveyed, concealed or detained; theft: also where the stolen property was possessed (S.201). Offences by letters or electronic communication: where sent or received (S.202). Offences outside India by an Indian citizen: as if committed where he is found, with Central Government sanction (S.208).

💡 Example (asked Nov 2020): a girl is kidnapped in Warangal, taken to Hyderabad, raped and murdered there. Courts at Warangal (kidnapping, S.201) and Hyderabad (rape and murder, S.197) both have jurisdiction, and all offences, being one transaction, can be tried together (S.243) by the Court of Session.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ends

Investigation is evidence collection by police, ending with the police report (S.193).

This is investigation; the Magistrate is not involved yet.

Chapter 3 · asked 7× 🔥🔥 Important

Trial before a Court of Session (S.248–260)

📖 The story: Hardeep Singh v. State of Punjab

(2014) 3 SCC 92 · decided 10 Jan 2014

In a murder case in Punjab, the police filed a charge sheet against some accused but not others. During the trial, a witness's evidence implicated persons not named in the charge sheet.

The trial court used its power to summon additional accused. Different High Courts and benches of the Supreme Court had given different answers on when and how this power can be used, so the questions were referred to a Constitution Bench.

❓ When can a criminal court summon a person, not already an accused, to be tried along with the accused?

⚖️ The law behind the story

To answer that question, the court applied this law. Read it with the story in mind:

1. Committal (S.232). When an offence is triable exclusively by the Court of Session, the Magistrate commits the case after supplying documents to the accused, within 90 days of cognizance (extendable to 180).

2. Opening (S.248–249). The trial is conducted by the Public Prosecutor, who opens the case by describing the charge and the evidence he will lead.

3. Discharge or charge (S.250–251). The accused may apply for discharge within 60 days of committal; if the judge finds no sufficient ground after hearing both sides, he discharges him with reasons. If there is ground to presume the offence, he frames the charge in writing within 60 days of the first hearing on charge, and reads and explains it.

4. Plea (S.252). If the accused pleads guilty, the judge records it and may convict; otherwise the trial proceeds.

5. Prosecution evidence (S.253–255). The judge fixes dates and takes all prosecution evidence (S.254), including by audio-video means; the accused cross-examines. If there is no evidence that the accused committed the offence, the judge acquits him (S.255).

6. Defence (S.256). Otherwise the accused is called on to enter upon his defence and lead evidence; he may also file a written statement. Process to compel witnesses is issued unless the request is vexatious.

7. Arguments and judgment (S.257–258). The prosecutor sums up, the defence replies. The judge delivers judgment within 30 days of the arguments, extendable to 45 days for reasons recorded. If convicted, the judge hears the accused on sentence before passing it (S.258(2)).

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ended

The power can be used at any stage after the inquiry or trial begins, on the basis of evidence recorded in court, including only the examination-in-chief.

The test is more than a prima facie case: the evidence must be such that, if unrebutted, it would lead to conviction.

Persons not charge-sheeted, or even discharged earlier, can be summoned if the evidence so shows.

Why it matters:

  • The real culprit should not escape just because the police did not send him for trial.
  • The power is discretionary and extraordinary, to be used sparingly.
  • BNSS S.358 continues this power (formerly S.319).

Chapter 4 · asked 7× 🔥🔥 Important

Public Prosecutor, withdrawal from prosecution, defence counsel and the police (S.18–20, S.360)

📖 The story: The victim's own lawyer

A murdered man's family worries the prosecution will be weak and wants their own advocate.

❓ What does the law say?

⚖️ The law behind the story

Here is the law that decides it:

Public Prosecutor (S.18–20). Appointed for the High Court and every district, from advocates with at least 7 years' practice; Assistant Public Prosecutors conduct cases before Magistrates (S.19); a Directorate of Prosecution supervises them (S.20). A victim may engage an advocate to assist the prosecution.

Role. He is an officer of the court, not a mere agent of the police: his duty is to help the court find the truth, place all evidence fairly (even evidence favouring the accused), and not to secure a conviction at any cost.

Withdrawal (S.360). The Public Prosecutor may, with the consent of the court, withdraw from the prosecution at any time before judgment: before charge, the accused is discharged; after charge, acquitted. The BNSS adds that no withdrawal is allowed without hearing the victim. The court must be satisfied that he applied his mind independently and in public interest, not for extraneous reasons (Sheonandan Paswan v. State of Bihar, 1987).

Defence counsel. Every accused has a right to be defended by a lawyer of his choice (Art. 22(1)); legal aid at State expense if he has no means (S.341). The defence lawyer must defend fearlessly, but must not mislead the court.

Police. They prevent and detect crime, register FIRs, investigate, arrest, search and seize, and file the police report; a police officer is not a prosecutor, and a confession to him is inadmissible.

Agold-edged lines are your Part A answer.

🏛️ How the story ends

Public Prosecutors are appointed from advocates with 7 years' practice; a victim may engage an advocate to assist the prosecution.

The family's advocate assists the Public Prosecutor, who still leads the case.

Part C workshop · 10 marks each

Solve the problem like a judge

Every Part C problem is a new story with the same law. Spot the issue, state the law, apply it to the facts, and conclude. Quote the matching case story to lift your answer.

Problem 1. Within one year a person committed theft, cheating and criminal trespass. Which court tries him, and what punishment?

Asked: 2015 · Feb 2024

Issue
Which court has jurisdiction over several different offences, and what sentence can it pass?
Law
Theft BNS 303(2): up to 3 years, fine, or both. Cheating BNS 318(4): up to 7 years and fine. Criminal trespass BNS 329(3): up to 3 months or fine up to ₹5,000, or both. First Schedule: theft by any Magistrate; cheating by JMFC. S.241: a separate charge for each distinct offence; S.242 (same kind) does not apply as they are different kinds; S.243 only if one transaction. S.23 and S.25: JMFC sentencing limits and consecutive sentences.
Apply
All three are triable by a JMFC, separately charged; he can be tried at one trial if they form one series, otherwise in separate cases.
Conclusion
A Judicial Magistrate of the first class can try all three, framing separate charges; punishment within the BNS limits for each and the JMFC's powers (3 years / ₹50,000 per offence, sentences can run consecutively under S.25).

Problem 2. Two persons took away a box of gold chains from a shop; at home they found it empty. Have they committed any offence? Which court? Punishment?

Asked: 2015 · Feb 2024

Issue
Is it theft, or attempt to commit theft, when the box turns out to be empty?
Law
BNS 303: theft (dishonestly moving movable property out of a person's possession). BNS 62: attempt to commit an offence punishable with imprisonment, up to half of the longest term. BNS 3(5): common intention. Classic illustration: breaking open a box to steal jewels and finding it empty is an attempt to commit theft.
Apply
They dishonestly took the box itself, which is theft of the box; and as to the gold chains, they did everything towards stealing them, so it is an attempt to commit theft. Both acted with common intention.
Conclusion
Yes: theft of the box and attempt to commit theft of the chains (BNS 303(2), 62 with 3(5)); triable by any Magistrate; attempt punishable up to half the term for theft (up to 1½ years), or fine, or both.

Problem 3. At 2 a.m., A committed robbery, extortion and murder (or set fire to a house for robbery, burning three people to death). How to file the case, which court, what punishment?

Asked: Sep 2021 · Aug 2023 (also 2020: murder and burning of body)

Issue
Procedure and forum for grave offences in one transaction.
Law
FIR under S.173 (cognizable). Murder BNS 103: death or life and fine. Robbery BNS 309(4): rigorous imprisonment up to 10 years (up to 14 years if on a highway between sunset and sunrise). Extortion BNS 308(2): up to 7 years. Mischief by fire to destroy a house BNS 326(g). Causing disappearance of evidence BNS 238. S.243: offences in one transaction tried together. S.232: committal to Sessions.
Apply
The family or any informant lodges an FIR; police investigate and file the charge sheet; the Magistrate commits the case; the Court of Session tries all offences together.
Conclusion
FIR at the police station → investigation → committal → trial by the Court of Session; punishment up to death or life imprisonment for murder, with sentences for the other offences.

Problem 4. A subordinate makes a defamatory statement against a senior police officer. How to proceed, and which court?

Asked: Sep 2021

Issue
Who can prosecute for defamation, and before which court?
Law
Defamation BNS 356: simple imprisonment up to 2 years, or fine, or both, or community service. S.222 BNSS: no court takes cognizance of defamation except on a complaint by the person aggrieved; where the defamed person is a public servant and it relates to his public functions, the Public Prosecutor may file a complaint in the Court of Session with the Government's sanction.
Apply
The officer may file a private complaint before the Magistrate; if it concerns his official conduct, the State may prosecute through the Public Prosecutor before the Sessions Court. Departmental action is a separate route.
Conclusion
Only on a complaint: by the officer before a Magistrate, or, for defamation about his official duties, by the Public Prosecutor (with sanction) directly before the Court of Session.
