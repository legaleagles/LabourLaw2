★ Golden rules

  • ADR is "appropriate" dispute resolution: choose the method that suits the dispute; courts remain for what needs adjudication.
  • From negotiation to arbitration, party control falls and third-party power rises.
  • Only arbitration ends in a decision imposed on the parties; the others end in an agreement.
  • Article 39A (equal justice) and Article 21 (speedy justice) give ADR its constitutional base.
  • Not every dispute can go to ADR: crimes, matrimonial status, guardianship, insolvency and similar matters stay with courts.

⚖️ Cases

  • Jalour Singh “No consent, no award” A Lok Adalat has no power to adjudicate; it can only record a settlement agreed by the parties. An award made without consent is invalid and can be challenged by a writ petition.
  • Afcons Infrastructure “Consider ADR always; force arbitration never” Considering ADR under S.89 is mandatory, but arbitration and conciliation need consent; "judicial settlement" and "mediation" must be read interchanged; the Court listed cases fit and unfit for ADR.

★ Golden rules

  • A Lok Adalat can only record a settlement; it cannot decide a case when the parties do not agree.
  • A Lok Adalat award is a decree, final and binding, with no appeal (S.21).
  • Free legal aid is a fundamental right of an accused who cannot afford a lawyer (Art. 21, 39A).
  • Family Courts must first try to settle the dispute (S.9) and lawyers appear only with leave (S.13).
  • Under S.89, courts must consider ADR in every suit; arbitration and conciliation need the parties' consent.

⚖️ Cases

  • Haresh Dayaram Thakur “No signature, no settlement” Conciliation must follow Part III strictly: only a settlement agreement drawn up and signed by the parties (and authenticated by the conciliator) binds. The order was set aside.
  • K. Srinivas Rao “Mediate before you litigate” The marriage was dissolved, and the Court directed Family Courts and criminal courts to refer suitable matrimonial disputes to mediation, with pre-litigation desks at mediation centres.

★ Golden rules

  • Conciliation begins only when the other party accepts the invitation in writing (S.62).
  • The conciliator may propose terms but can never impose them; only a settlement signed by the parties binds.
  • A signed settlement agreement has the status of an arbitral award (S.74) and is enforced like a decree.
  • Everything said in conciliation is confidential and cannot be used later in court or arbitration (S.75, S.81).
  • Courts should send matrimonial disputes to mediation early, even before litigation.

★ Golden rules

  • No written arbitration agreement, no arbitration (S.7); "may refer, if parties agree" is not enough.
  • The arbitration clause survives even if the main contract is said to be void (S.16: separability).
  • A civil court must refer the parties to arbitration if a valid agreement exists (S.8).
  • Most civil disputes are arbitrable; rights in rem, criminal, matrimonial, insolvency and some public-law matters are not.
  • A party with an interest cannot unilaterally appoint the arbitrator; equal treatment applies from day one.

⚖️ Cases

  • Ssangyong “An arbitrator cannot rewrite the contract” Courts cannot review awards on merits, but an award that rewrites the contract and binds a party to terms it never agreed to shocks the conscience; the majority award was set aside.
  • Gayatri Balasamy “Correct, do not rewrite” Courts have a limited power to modify: sever invalid parts, correct clerical or computational errors apparent on the record, and adjust post-award interest; the Supreme Court may use Art. 142 rarely. No rewriting on merits.

★ Golden rules

  • An award must be in writing, signed, reasoned, dated and state its place (S.31).
  • Courts do not sit in appeal over awards: no re-appreciation of evidence or correction of mere errors of law.
  • Since 2015, "public policy" is narrow: fraud or corruption, the fundamental policy of Indian law, or the most basic notions of morality and justice.
  • Courts can now modify an award only in narrow cases: severing invalid parts, clerical errors, post-award interest.
  • Filing S.34 does not automatically stay the award; the court may stay it on conditions such as a deposit (S.36(3)).

⚖️ Cases

  • Renusagar Power “Public policy is not every law” A foreign award can be refused as contrary to public policy only if it offends the fundamental policy of Indian law, the interests of India, or justice or morality; the award was enforced.
  • BALCO “The seat decides the court” Part I (including S.34) applies only to arbitrations seated in India; awards made abroad can only be resisted at the enforcement stage under Part II. Applies to agreements made after 6 Sep 2012.

★ Golden rules

  • A foreign award must come from a New York Convention country notified by India and arise from a commercial relationship (S.44): India's two reservations.
  • An Indian court cannot review the merits of a foreign award; refusal is only on the S.48 grounds.
  • Public policy for foreign awards is narrow: fundamental policy of Indian law, interests of India, justice or morality.
  • If the seat is abroad, Indian courts cannot set aside the award under S.34; they only decide enforcement.
  • The 1996 Act is modelled on the UNCITRAL Model Law (1985).

⚖️ Cases

  • L. Chandra Kumar “No tribunal is above the High Court” The power of judicial review of the High Courts (Art. 226/227) and the Supreme Court (Art. 32) is part of the basic structure; clauses excluding it are invalid. Tribunal decisions can be challenged before a High Court Division Bench.

★ Golden rules

  • Tribunals were added by the 42nd Amendment (1976): Art. 323A (service matters) and Art. 323B (other matters).
  • Tribunals cannot shut out the High Court and Supreme Court: judicial review is a basic feature.
  • Lokpal and Lokayukta are ombudsmen against corruption, inspired by the Scandinavian ombudsman.
  • The Lokpal can inquire even into the Prime Minister, with strict safeguards.
  • ODR rests on the IT Act, 2000 and electronic arbitration agreements (S.7) and online mediation (Mediation Act, 2023).
