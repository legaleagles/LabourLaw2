⚖️ Cases

  • Mohori Bibee “A minor cannot sign away” A minor's agreement is void ab initio; the mortgage was set aside and the minor did not have to repay.
  • Bir Singh “Your signature speaks for you” A signed cheque voluntarily handed over attracts the presumption of a debt even if someone else filled in the details; the drawer must disprove the debt.

🧩 Part C routes

  • "When B delivers the goods"S.4 (a) Not a promissory note; it is an ordinary contract to pay on delivery. (b) A mere acknowledgment (I.O.U.) is not a note, but it is evidence of the debt and an acknowledgment under S.18 Limitation Act; if it contains a promise to pay, it is a note.
  • Note for a time-barred debtS.25(3)S.118(a)S.9 The note is valid; Jayant's defence fails. Pratap can sue Jayant (maker) and, if needed, Umang (indorser).
  • Minor's note renewed at majorityS.11S.26 No. The fresh note is void for want of consideration and the suit is not maintainable. (If fresh money had been lent on majority, the new note would be valid to that extent.)
  • MD signs without his titleS.27S.28 The MD is personally liable on the note; the company is not liable on the instrument. A may still sue the company on the loan itself if he proves the money was borrowed for and used by the company.
  • Maker dies: claim on the heirs?S.29S.50S.52 Yes. Z can recover from X's legal heirs, limited to the assets of X that came into their hands.
  • Maturity of a one-month noteS.23S.22S.25 The first note matures on 3 September 2018; the second on 4 April 2018. If that day is a public holiday under S.25, it is payable on the preceding business day.
  • A missing bill of exchangeS.5S.45AS.58 B can obtain a duplicate bill from A under S.45A on giving security, and warn the drawee not to pay anyone else; if A refuses, B can sue on the original debt.

★ Golden rules

  • Unconditional is the soul of a note or bill. "When B delivers the goods" or "after my marriage" kills it (S.4, S.5).
  • A holder in due course takes free from the defects of earlier holders: he must give value, take it before maturity, and in good faith (S.9).
  • A minor can make or indorse an instrument and bind everyone except himself (S.26).
  • A signed blank or incomplete stamped paper is an inchoate instrument; the holder may complete it up to the stamp amount (S.20).
  • Every instrument is presumed to be for consideration, and every holder a holder in due course (S.118).

⚖️ Cases

  • R.C. Cooper “Even the State must pay fairly” The 1969 Act was struck down: the compensation principles were unfair and the named banks were wrongly barred from doing any banking in future. A new Act followed in 1970.
  • IAMAI v. RBI “Power, yes; overkill, no” RBI has the power to regulate such activity, but this total ban was disproportionate and was set aside.

🧩 Part C routes

  • "Sunrise Bank Finance" takes depositsS.7S.22S.46 Its business is illegal; RBI and the police can act, the directors face penalties, and depositors can claim their money back.
  • Bank board wants to trade riceS.8 The decision is illegal under S.8. Only if the bank sells goods pledged by a defaulting borrower (realisation of security) is it permitted.
  • ₹40,000 unauthorised card debit The bank must refund the ₹40,000. If it refuses, he can complain to the bank, and after 30 days, to the RBI Ombudsman, who can award the loss plus compensation for harassment.

★ Golden rules

  • Banking = accepting public deposits for lending or investment, repayable on demand or otherwise, withdrawable by cheque, draft or order (S.5(b)).
  • No company can use the word "bank" in its name unless it is a banking company (S.7), and none can do banking without an RBI licence (S.22).
  • A bank cannot trade in goods, except to realise its security (S.8).
  • Nationalisation must still respect fundamental rights: the 1969 Act fell, the 1970 Act replaced it.
  • RBI can regulate new technology, but its measures must be proportionate.

⚖️ Cases

  • Canara Bank v. Canara Sales “Forged signature, bank pays” A forged cheque is no mandate; the bank must bear the loss. The customer's failure to check its passbook is not enough to stop it from suing.
  • Macmillan and Arthur “Leave no gaps” The customer owes the bank a duty to draw cheques so as not to invite fraud; having left the gaps, the firm bore the loss.
  • Curtice “A stop order must be seen” A countermand takes effect only when it actually comes to the bank's attention; there is no constructive countermand.
  • Lloyds Bank v. Savory “Know who pays your customer” The bank was negligent: it had not found out who the customers' employers were, so it lost the statutory protection.

🧩 Part C routes

  • Thief forges the payee's indorsementS.85(1)S.10S.58 R cannot recover from the paying bank. He can sue the thief, and the person who received the money (and, if the cheque was collected through a careless collecting bank, that bank for conversion).
  • Office boy adds a zeroS.87S.89S.18 The bank is liable if the alteration was visible or the words did not match; Uday bears the loss if his careless drawing made a clean alteration possible. Either way, the office boy is liable for forgery and cheating (BNS 318, 336).
  • Paid before the stop order arrivedS.31 The banker is not liable; the payment is valid and can be debited to B.
  • False credits in the passbook The bank can correct its error for the future, but it is liable for wrongful dishonour of A's cheque and cannot recover sums A spent in good faith relying on the entries.
  • ₹1.1 lakh cheque, ₹1 lakh balanceS.31S.30S.138 Z cannot sue the bank. He should send a demand notice to the drawer within 30 days of the return memo, and if unpaid within 15 days, file a complaint under S.138; he can also sue for the amount.
  • Post-dated cheque presented lateS.6 The cheque is valid and must be paid if funds are sufficient; if dishonoured for want of funds, Y can proceed under S.138.
  • Draft credited, then reversed?S.85AS.131S.131A The loss falls on AB Bank, not YZ Bank. AB can recover from Z, who is also liable for cheating and forgery.

★ Golden rules

  • The paying banker must honour a properly drawn cheque when the drawer has sufficient funds; otherwise it must compensate the drawer (S.31).
  • A forged drawer's signature is no mandate at all: the bank bears the loss, even if the customer did not check his statements.
  • A customer must draw cheques carefully; if he leaves room for alteration, he bears the loss.
  • A stop-payment order works only when it actually reaches the bank.
  • A collecting banker who ignores warning signs (employer's cheques into an employee's account) loses S.131 protection.

⚖️ Cases

  • Rangappa “Admit the signature, explain the debt” S.139 presumes that a legally enforceable debt existed; the drawer can rebut it by probable evidence, but a bare story is not enough.
  • Meters and Instruments “Pay up, case closed” S.138 is primarily a civil wrong with a criminal sanction; if the complainant is duly compensated, the court may close the case even without consent, and trials should be quick and summary.
  • Laxmi Dyechem “A bounce is a bounce” Dishonour for such reasons can also be an offence under S.138 if the drawer caused it to avoid payment; "insufficient funds" is the genus, these are its species.

🧩 Part C routes

  • "Pay C if he returns from London"S.52 No. Until C returns from London, he cannot claim on the indorsement or hold the indorser liable. (A paying banker that pays in due course is still discharged; but C has no enforceable right.)
  • "Pay B ₹500" on a ₹1,000 noteS.56 The partial indorsement is invalid for negotiation: B does not become a holder and cannot sue on the note. The note itself remains valid in the original holder's hands. If ₹500 had been paid, he could note it and indorse the balance.
  • Steps of a blank indorsementS.15S.16S.46 A blank indorsement is just the holder's signature plus delivery; it turns the bill into a bearer instrument, and the indorser is liable to every later holder.
  • Place of drawing alteredS.87 B is not liable on the bill. The drawer and the indorsee, who agreed to the change, remain liable.
  • Accommodation bill passed onS.43 A, B and C have no claims. D can recover from C, B, A and X. E can recover from C, B, A and X, but not from D.
  • Signed "A, Agent"S.28S.27 Yes, A is personally liable. He should have signed "for and on behalf of P, A, Agent".
  • School-fee cheque: account closedS.138S.142S.143A The school should send Amit a written demand within 30 days of the return memo; if he does not pay within 15 days of receiving it, file a complaint under S.138 within the next month, ask for interim compensation, and may also file a summary civil suit.
  • Garnishee order after the chequeO.XXI r.46S.31S.30 The bank was right to dishonour; B cannot sue the bank. B can recover from A by a civil suit (summary suit), and can send a S.138 notice; if A does not pay within 15 days, B may file a complaint, which will turn on whether A had arranged funds.

★ Golden rules

  • Indorsement + delivery transfers an order instrument; a blank indorsement makes it payable to bearer (S.16, S.49).
  • An indorsement for part of the amount is not valid for negotiation (S.56).
  • Once a cheque is admitted to be signed, the court presumes a legally enforceable debt; the drawer must disprove it.
  • "Account closed", "stop payment" and "signature differs" can also lead to S.138 liability.
  • S.138 is mainly about compensating the payee; courts encourage quick settlement.

  • Foley v. Hill “Deposit = loan to the bank” The bankers were his debtors, not trustees or agents: money deposited becomes the bank's money. His remedy was an ordinary claim for debt.

★ Golden rules

  • A deposit is a loan to the bank; the money becomes the bank's, and it need not account for how it uses it.
  • The bank's debt is unusual: it is payable only on demand by the customer, at the branch.
  • Secrecy is a legal duty with four exceptions: law, public duty, bank's interest, customer's consent.
  • Customers' bank records are part of their privacy; officials cannot rummage through them without safeguards.
  • A customer is a person with an account; how long or how often he deals does not matter.

  • Brandao v. Barnett “Lien, unless the purpose says no” Bankers have a general lien on securities deposited with them as bankers, as part of the law merchant; but there was no lien here, because the bills were held for a special purpose (custody).

★ Golden rules

  • A banker's lien is a general lien (for the whole balance) and an implied pledge, so the bank may sell after reasonable notice.
  • No lien on articles in safe custody or on property given for a specific purpose.
  • A banker's lien extends to fixed deposits and securities that come to it in the course of banking.
  • Set-off needs mutual debts, due, between the same parties in the same right.
  • In a running account, first in, first out: early payments wipe out early debts.

  • A bank guarantee is an independent promise; a dispute in the main contract does not stop it.
  • Courts stop payment only for egregious fraud known to the bank or irretrievable injustice.
  • Under UCP 600, every credit is irrevocable even if it does not say so.
