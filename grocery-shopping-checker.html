🔍 FSSAI-Backed Quality Checks
Why these tests matter
Food adulteration isn't just about value for money — some adulterants (synthetic dyes in turmeric, detergent in milk) carry real health risks. FSSAI's own DART (Detect Adulteration with Rapid Test) programme teaches these exact household checks, using items already in your kitchen. None of them require special equipment.

Milk — The Slip Test (water dilution)
▾
Water added to milk is one of the most common forms of dilution, reducing nutritional value without an obvious visual change.
How to check: Place a drop of milk on a polished, slanted surface (a steel plate works well). Pure milk flows slowly and leaves a distinct white trail. Milk diluted with water flows quickly and leaves little to no trail.
🧪
Milk — The Iodine Test (starch)
▾
Starch is sometimes added to thicken diluted milk and mask the dilution.
How to check: Add a few drops of iodine solution to a small milk sample. A blue colour appearing indicates added starch. Regular milk does not turn blue.
🫧
Milk — The Detergent Test
▾
Detergent is occasionally added to make watered-down milk foam like fresh milk when shaken, imitating a sign people associate with quality.
How to check: Mix equal parts milk and water in a clear bottle, shake vigorously. If excessive, persistent foam forms (well beyond a normal light froth), detergent contamination is possible.
🟡
Turmeric — The Water Test
▾
Cheap synthetic dyes, including lead chromate, are sometimes added to turmeric to make its colour look richer and more uniform — these dyes can be genuinely harmful with repeated exposure.
How to check: Add a pinch of turmeric powder to a glass of water. If the water turns orange-red almost instantly and intensely, that's a warning sign of added colour — pure turmeric colours water more gradually and mildly.
📅
Best Before vs Expiry — Know the Difference
▾
"Best Before" is about peak quality, not safety — the product is often still fine to consume shortly after, just possibly less fresh in taste or texture. "Expiry" or "Use By" is a harder safety line, especially on perishables and infant food.
How to check: Don't treat every "Best Before" date as a hard cutoff — but always treat "Expiry"/"Use By" dates on perishables, dairy, and infant products as firm. Clearance bins mixing both types under one "discount" banner deserve a closer look.
🪪
The FSSAI Licence Number
▾
Every food business in India — from a big supermarket to a small snack stall — is legally required to display a 14-digit FSSAI licence or registration number.
How to check: Look for the number on packaging or a displayed certificate. You can verify a licence number is genuine and active on the FoSCoS portal (foscos.fssai.gov.in). No number, or an unverifiable one, is worth raising with the seller.
⚖️
Your Right to a Correct Weighment
▾
Under the Legal Metrology Act, you're entitled to weights and measures that are accurate and independently verifiable — a shop's scale isn't the final word if you have reason to doubt it.
How to check: Ask that the container/bag be tared (zeroed out) before your loose items are weighed, so you aren't billed for the packaging itself. Most towns have a government-verified weighing facility if a dispute is serious enough to escalate.

Shrinkflation — Same Price, Less Inside
The packaging looks identical, the price hasn't moved — but the net weight printed in small text has quietly dropped from, say, 200g to 170g. Nobody notices a 15% cut when the box looks the same.
Beat it: Check the net weight printed on the pack, not just the price, especially on packaged snacks and staples you buy often. Compare price-per-100g/kg rather than price-per-pack when deciding if it's actually good value.

"MRP Plus Local Taxes"
A shop adds a few extra rupees at billing, calling it "local tax" or "service charge" on top of the printed MRP.
Beat it: MRP is legally the maximum final price, inclusive of all taxes. Nothing can legally be added on top of it. Point to the printed MRP and decline to pay anything extra.

Weighment Shortfall — Tare Weight Not Deducted
Loose items like grains, dal, or vegetables are weighed in a bag or tray — and that bag/tray's own weight is sometimes billed as if it were product.
Beat it: Ask for the empty container to be zeroed ("tared") on the scale before your item goes in. It's a simple, fair request any legitimate shop will honour.

Hidden Delivery & Packing Charges
Quick-commerce apps sometimes reveal a "small cart fee," "packing charge," or "surge fee" only at the final payment screen, after you've already spent time filling your cart.
Beat it: Check the full breakdown before entering payment, not just the item total. If fees feel excessive, it's easy to abandon a cart before paying — the sunk time isn't a reason to accept an unfair fee.

Bill Rounding Always Goes Up

"Opened, No Return" Sprung After Purchase
A packaged food item turns out to be spoiled or defective only once opened at home — and the "no return on opened items" policy is then used to refuse any refund, even though you couldn't have discovered the defect any other way.

A packaged water bottle or snack gets sold above its printed MRP with a "chilled" or "convenience" justification — a well-known but still illegal practice under the Legal Metrology Act.

Your Legal Backing
Legal Metrology Act, 2009
MRP is the final, tax-inclusive price — nothing can legally be added on top. Weighing and measuring equipment must be accurate and government-verifiable.
FSSAI — Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006
Every food business needs a valid licence/registration number, verifiable on FoSCoS. Adulteration is a punishable offence, with DART household tests published for consumer awareness.
Consumer Protection Act, 2019
Short weight, hidden charges, and misleading "fresh"/"discount" claims are unfair trade practices — you can complain and claim redress.
