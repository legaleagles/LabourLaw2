Why food detectives matter

The Mystery of the Squeaky Paneer
A starch mystery — 4 min read
▾
{{NAME}} loved paneer butter masala more than anything — but one Sunday, the paneer at home felt strange. It didn't have that soft, slightly squeaky bite paneer usually has. It felt... rubbery. {{NAME}}'s grandmother, who'd been making paneer at home for forty years, picked up a small piece and rubbed it between her fingers.
"Some shops," she said, "mix starch into paneer to make it weigh more and cost them less to make. Let's find out." She boiled a tiny piece in water, let it cool, and added two drops of iodine tincture from the first-aid box.
🔍 The Clue: If the paneer turns blue where the iodine touches it, there's starch hiding inside — real paneer, made only from milk, won't turn blue at all.
The spot turned a deep, unmistakable blue. Grandmother nodded — the paneer had been mixed with starch to bulk it up. It wasn't dangerous in a big way, but it also wasn't the paneer they'd paid full price for.
⚠️ Why it matters: Starch-mixed paneer has far less real protein than pure paneer — you're paying paneer prices for mostly starch, and for growing kids especially, that protein gap matters more than it seems.
The next week, {{NAME}}'s family found a trusted local dairy that made paneer fresh every morning. {{NAME}} did the iodine test herself just to check — no blue at all. Case closed.

▾
{{NAME}} couldn't wait for mango season. But the mangoes her father brought home looked odd — bright yellow on the outside, but with strange green patches near the stem, and almost no sweet mango smell at all, even up close.
"These were probably ripened too fast," her father said. Some sellers use a chemical called calcium carbide to force fruit to ripen in a day or two instead of naturally over a week — it's cheaper and quicker for them, but it's actually banned by law in India.
🔍 The Clues: Naturally ripened mangoes have an even color all over, a strong sweet smell before you even cut them, and the color inside matches the outside. Carbide-ripened fruit often has patchy color, weak or no smell, and sometimes a faint chemical odor.

The Sparkly Sweet With a Secret

▾

⚠️ Why it matters: Eating aluminium foil regularly, dressed up as vark, isn't something your body is designed to process as food — it's simply not meant to go inside you the way real silver is.
{{NAME}}'s family switched to a sweet shop that could show them where their vark came from. {{NAME}} still does the crumble test every Diwali now — it's become a little family tradition of its own.

The Oil That Made a Village Sick
A true, serious story — 5 min read
▾
This isn't a made-up mystery, {{NAME}} — it really happened. In 1998, in Delhi and nearby areas, thousands of people suddenly fell ill with swollen legs, breathing trouble, and serious heart problems. Doctors were confused at first — until they traced it back to something everyone had been cooking with: mustard oil.
The mustard oil had been secretly mixed with oil from a weed called argemone, which looks a little like mustard when you're not looking closely. Argemone oil is toxic — it doesn't belong anywhere near food.
🔍 The Real Lesson: Unlike paneer or honey, there's no simple kitchen test for argemone oil in mustard oil — this is exactly why it's so dangerous, and exactly why buying from a sealed, branded, FSSAI-marked bottle matters so much more than loose oil from an unknown source.
Thousands of people were hospitalised, and dozens died before doctors figured out the cause and the government banned the sale of the contaminated oil.
⚠️ Why it matters: Argemone oil causes a condition called "epidemic dropsy" — severe swelling, breathing difficulty, and heart failure in serious cases. It's one of the clearest examples in Indian history of why food safety rules exist at all.
Today, mustard oil sold in India must be pure, with strict testing to prevent this happening again. It's a serious story, but an important one, {{NAME}} — some food safety rules exist because of real people who got hurt, and knowing why helps us take the rules seriously.

📋 For Parents — The Real Detection Guide
Tap any card to open it — every test here is from FSSAI's own household-testing manual
🧀
Paneer, Khoya & Milk — Starch and Detergent
▾
Starch is added to bulk up paneer, khoya, and milk cheaply, and detergent is sometimes added to milk to fake the foam of a good churn.
Starch test: Boil a small piece with a little water, cool, add 2-3 drops of iodine tincture. Blue colour means starch is present.
Detergent test: Shake a sample of milk vigorously. Thick, dense foam suggests detergent; pure milk forms only a thin layer.
Health impact: Starch-heavy paneer has far less real protein than the price suggests. Detergent residue in milk can cause stomach upset, especially in young children.
🧈
Ghee & Butter — Mashed Potato/Starch
▾
Mashed potatoes, sweet potatoes, or other starches are sometimes mixed into ghee or butter to increase quantity cheaply.
How to check: Take half a teaspoon of the ghee/butter in a clear bowl, add 2-3 drops of iodine tincture. A blue colour confirms starch adulteration.
Health impact: Mainly a value issue rather than a serious health risk on its own — but it means you're paying ghee prices for potato starch.
🍯
Honey — Why Home Tests Aren't Reliable Anymore
▾
A major 2020 investigation by India's Centre for Science and Environment found that several big honey brands passed India's official purity tests and traditional home tests, yet failed advanced laboratory testing (NMR) — meaning sophisticated sugar-syrup adulteration is specifically engineered to beat the old checks.
What actually helps: Since the burn test and water-drop test can be fooled, your strongest protection is buying from a known local beekeeper, a brand that publishes third-party lab results, or a certified source — trust the seller, not the kitchen trick.
Health impact: Sugar-syrup-diluted honey isn't typically dangerous, but it means you're paying honey prices for flavoured sugar water with none of honey's real nutritional properties.
🫒
Mustard Oil — Argemone Oil
▾
Argemone oil, from a toxic weed, has historically been mixed into mustard oil. There is no reliable simple home test for this specific adulterant — it requires laboratory analysis to detect confidently.
What actually helps: Buy only sealed, branded mustard oil with a valid FSSAI mark, from a reputable retailer — never loose, unbranded oil of unclear origin, especially given how serious this specific risk is.
Health impact: Argemone oil causes "epidemic dropsy" — severe swelling, breathing difficulty, and in serious cases, heart failure or death. A real 1998 outbreak in Delhi affected thousands and caused dozens of deaths.
🥭
Fruits — Calcium Carbide Ripening
▾
Calcium carbide ("masala") is banned but still sometimes used to force mangoes, bananas, and papayas to ripen overnight instead of naturally. It releases acetylene gas with traces of arsenic and phosphorus.
Visual signs: Uneven or patchy colouring (green patches near the stem on an otherwise yellow fruit), little to no natural fruity smell, sometimes a faint chemical odour, and colour that doesn't match ripeness inside when cut.
Health impact: Linked to dizziness, throat and mouth irritation, difficulty swallowing, vomiting, and skin ulcers — serious enough that FSSAI runs dedicated enforcement drives every mango season.
🍬
Silver Vark on Sweets — Aluminium Substitution
▾
The decorative silver layer on Indian sweets is sometimes actually thin aluminium foil, which isn't approved as a food-safe material the way genuine silver vark is.
How to check: Rub a small flake between your fingers — real silver crumbles into powder, while aluminium tears into shreds. For confirmation, a small piece burned by an adult: real silver leaves a tiny shiny ball; aluminium turns to grey ash.
Health impact: Aluminium isn't meant to be consumed as food — regular ingestion dressed up as a tradition isn't something the body is designed to process safely.
🍬
Sugar & Jaggery — Chalk Powder
▾
Chalk powder is sometimes mixed into sugar, "pithi" sugar, or jaggery to add cheap bulk weight.
How to check: Dissolve about 10g of the sample in a glass of water. Real sugar/jaggery dissolves fully; chalk powder settles as a visible layer at the bottom.
Health impact: Mainly a value and quality issue — chalk isn't typically acutely toxic in small amounts, but it's not food, and it means you're not getting what you paid for.
🍵
Tea, Coffee & Pepper — Common Substitutions
▾
Used tea leaves get re-coloured and re-dried for resale, iron filings are sometimes added to tea to increase weight, tamarind seed powder gets mixed into ground coffee, and dried papaya seeds — which look similar — are used to stretch whole black pepper.
How to check: For tea, rub a few leaves on white paper — excessive colour transfer suggests artificial dye. For pepper, look closely for seeds that are slightly smoother or a different shade than genuine peppercorns.
Health impact: Mostly a value and quality concern rather than acute danger, though re-used tea leaves can carry mould or bacteria from improper storage.
🍜
Instant Noodles — Sodium & Refined Flour
▾
Not an adulteration issue, but a nutrition-awareness one: instant noodles are typically made from refined maida, cooked in or with palm oil, and the flavour sachet is often high in sodium — none of this is hidden illegally, but it's easy to miss on a busy day.
How to check: Since 2024, FSSAI requires sugar, salt, and saturated fat to be printed in bold, larger font on the nutrition panel — check that number against how many packets are eaten in a week, not just a single serving.
Health impact: A single packet can contain close to half a child's recommended daily salt intake. Occasional consumption is generally fine; frequent reliance on it as a main meal displaces more nutritious food over time.
🥤
Sugary Drinks — The Hidden Sugar Load
▾
A single bottle of a sugary carbonated drink can contain sugar equivalent to 8-10 teaspoons — often close to or exceeding a child's entire recommended daily added-sugar intake in one serving.
How to check: Check the bold sugar figure (grams) on the nutrition panel and mentally divide by 4 to estimate teaspoons — a number that's often surprising compared to how "not that sweet" the drink tastes.
Health impact: Regular high intake is linked to tooth decay (from both sugar and acidity), excess calorie intake contributing to weight gain, and long-term risk factors for type 2 diabetes. India's Supreme Court has been pushing FSSAI to finalise mandatory front-of-pack warning labels for exactly this category.
🍦
Ice Cream vs "Frozen Dessert" — The Legal Difference
▾
Under FSSAI Regulation 2.1.7, a product can only be labelled "Ice Cream" if made primarily from milk fat (minimum 10%). Products using vegetable oils/fats instead — commonly palm oil — must legally be labelled "Frozen Dessert" or "Frozen Confection," with the vegetable fat percentage declared.
How to check: Read the actual product name printed on the tub or wrapper — not the picture, colour scheme, or price — since both categories are often styled to look nearly identical on shelf.
Health impact: Not inherently harmful — this is a labelling-honesty issue, not a safety one — but frozen desserts are often higher in added sugar and lower in real dairy protein, so it's worth knowing which one you're buying.
📺
Decoding Advertising Aimed at Kids
▾
Repetition, bright colours, mascots, jingles, and free toy tie-ins are well-documented persuasion techniques used specifically in advertising aimed at children — designed to build brand familiarity and "pester power" independent of a product's actual nutritional merit. "Health-washing" — claims like "made with real fruit" on an otherwise sugar-first ingredient list — is a related, common tactic.
What actually helps: Watch an ad together and name the techniques out loud with your child — the mascot, the jingle, the toy offer — turning it into a recognition game rather than a lecture. Then check the actual ingredients list together against the claim made in the ad.
Why it matters: This is significant enough as a public health concern that India's food regulator and courts have been actively developing stronger front-of-pack warning labels and nutrition-disclosure rules through 2025-2026, specifically citing marketing's role in rising diet-related illness in children.
