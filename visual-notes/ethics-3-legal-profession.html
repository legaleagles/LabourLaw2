🎓The Legal Profession: Development, Right to Practise and Senior Advocates

From merchants pleading in Mayor's Courts to one All-India Bar of advocates: a noble profession that serves justice, not profit.

1The big picture

In 1726 the East India Company's Mayor's Courts were run by merchants, and anyone could plead. Two centuries later, India had barristers, vakils, pleaders and mukhtars, each with different rights in different courts. The Advocates Act, 1961 swept this away and created one class of lawyers: advocates, enrolled with a State Bar Council and entitled to practise across India.

The profession is called noble because it is meant to serve justice, not profit: an advocate cannot advertise, cannot share in the result of a case, and cannot run a business. That is why the Supreme Court has said lawyers are not "service providers" under consumer law, and why foreign law firms cannot simply open shop in India.

1

Importance

Lawyers make the rule of law real: they protect rights and liberty, help courts reach the truth, give legal advice, draft laws and documents, and give access to justice to the weak. Many leaders of the freedom struggle (Gandhi, Nehru, Patel, Ambedkar) were lawyers.

🧠 Memory trick

Rights · Truth · Advice · Drafting · Access to justice.

🌍 Real life

Gandhi, Nehru and Patel were all lawyers before they led the freedom struggle.

2

Noble profession

The aim is service of justice, not profit. Hence the restrictions: no advertising or soliciting (BCI Rule 36), no contingency fees (Rule 20), no business while practising (Rule 47), duty to render legal aid (Rule 46). Lawyers' services are not treated as consumer "services" D.K. Gandhi.

💬 In simple words
  • Legal aid: free legal help for those who cannot afford a lawyer.
🧠 Memory trick

No adverts (R.36) · no contingency fee (R.20) · no business (R.47) · legal aid (R.46).

3

Company period

The Mayor's Courts (1726) had no trained lawyers. The Regulating Act, 1773 set up the Supreme Court at Calcutta (1774), which enrolled English barristers (advocates) and attorneys. Bengal Regulation VII of 1793 first created a regular profession of vakils (pleaders) in the Company's courts.

💬 In simple words
  • Enrolment: being entered on the State Bar Council's roll, which makes you an advocate.
🔢 Number trick

1726 Mayor's Courts (no trained lawyers) → 1773–74 Regulating Act and the Supreme Court at Calcutta → 1793 Bengal Regulation VII.

4

Legal Practitioners Acts

The Act of 1846 opened the profession to all, regardless of nationality or religion. After the High Courts (1862), the Legal Practitioners Act, 1879 recognised several grades: advocates, vakils, pleaders, mukhtars and revenue agents, each with different rights.

🔢 Number trick

1846: open to all, regardless of nationality or religion. 1879: grades of advocates, vakils, pleaders, mukhtars.

5

Bar Councils Act 1926

On the Chamier Committee's report, Bar Councils were created for each High Court, giving the Bar some self-government, but they were only advisory and the grades remained.

💬 In simple words
  • Bar Council: the elected body that regulates advocates in a State or in India.
🔢 Number trick

1926: Bar Councils for each High Court (Chamier Committee), but only advisory.

6

Advocates Act 1961

S.29S.30

On the All India Bar Committee (1951) and the 14th Report of the Law Commission (1958), Parliament created a unified, autonomous All-India Bar: one class of advocates (S.29), State Bar Councils and the Bar Council of India, a common roll, disciplinary powers, and the right to practise in all courts (S.30).

🔢 Number trick

1951 All India Bar Committee + 1958 14th Law Commission Report → 1961: one class of advocates (S.29).

7

Solicitors' firms

On the original side of the Bombay and Calcutta High Courts, a dual system continued: attorneys (solicitors) took instructions from clients and briefed counsel who argued. Solicitors' firms are partnerships of such attorneys, many over a century old, doing litigation and corporate work. Today they are all advocates under the Act; firms of advocates may be partnerships but cannot advertise or practise with non-lawyers.

🎨 Picture it

A two-step dance on the Bombay and Calcutta original sides: the solicitor takes instructions, the counsel argues.

8

After 1961

S.30

BCI Rules on conduct (1975); five-year integrated LL.B. and National Law Schools (from 1987); the All India Bar Examination (introduced in 2010, upheld in 2023); S.30 brought into force (2011); rules for foreign lawyers (notified 2023) after A.K. Balaji; growth of corporate law firms, legal aid and online courts.

🔢 Number trick

BCI Rules 1975 · National Law Schools from 1987 · AIBE 2010 (upheld 2023) · S.30 in force 2011 · foreign-lawyer rules 2023.

🔁 Recall: Travel through five stations: Mayor's Court (1726) → Calcutta Supreme Court (1774) → Legal Practitioners Acts (1846/1879) → Bar Councils Act (1926) → Advocates Act (1961).

1

Constitutional base

Art. 19

Art. 19(1)(g) guarantees every citizen the freedom to practise any profession, subject to reasonable restrictions and prescribed qualifications under Art. 19(6). So the State may require a law degree, enrolment, and good conduct.

🧠 Memory trick

Art. 19(1)(g) gives the freedom; Art. 19(6) lets the State demand a degree, enrolment and good conduct.

2

Statutory right

S.29S.30S.34

S.29: advocates are the only recognised class entitled to practise law. S.30: every advocate on the State roll is entitled as of right to practise in all courts including the Supreme Court, tribunals and before any authority (in force from 15 June 2011). The right is subject to court rules (e.g. Advocate-on-Record system in the Supreme Court) and to S.34 (High Court rules).

🧠 Memory trick

S.29: one class. S.30: every court, every tribunal, every authority in India (in force 15 June 2011).

3

Only advocates

S.33S.45S.32

No person may practise in any court or before any authority unless enrolled as an advocate. Illegal practice is punishable with imprisonment up to six months (S.45). A court may still permit a party in person, or a non-advocate in special cases (S.32).

🔢 Number trick

Practising without enrolment: up to 6 months' imprisonment (S.45).

🌍 Real life

A law graduate who has not enrolled argues a friend's bail application: illegal practice. Once enrolled, he may appear anywhere from a Munsif court to the Supreme Court.

4

Right or privilege

S.30S.35S.33S.45

For an enrolled advocate, practice is a statutory right (S.30), but it is also called a privilege because it is earned by qualification and kept only by good conduct: Bar Councils can suspend or remove an advocate (S.35), and courts control appearance before them. With the right come duties to the court, client and society.

Example: a law graduate who has not enrolled argues a bail application before a Magistrate on behalf of a friend. That is illegal practice (S.33, S.45). Once enrolled, he may appear in any court in India, from a Munsif court to the Supreme Court (subject to its Advocate-on-Record rules for filing).
🧠 Memory trick

Degree + enrolment = right; good conduct = keep the right. A right (S.30) and a privilege (S.35).

5

Not a business

Practice of law is a profession, not a trade: advocates cannot advertise, cannot share fees with non-lawyers, and are not "service providers" under consumer law D.K. Gandhi. Foreign firms cannot practise Indian law A.K. Balaji.

🎬 Case link

D.K. Gandhi: the profession is sui generis; lawyers are outside consumer law.

🎬 Case link

A.K. Balaji: foreign lawyers cannot practise Indian law; "fly in, fly out" advice on foreign law only.

📖 Word origin

Sui generis = "of its own kind".

🔁 Recall: "Degree + enrolment = right; good conduct = keep the right." That is why it is both a right (S.30) and a privilege (S.35).

4Senior advocates 🔥 Good to know

📝 How it is asked“Senior advocates: designation and restrictions”

  • short answer (Part A): Sep 2021 · Aug 2023 (restrictions) · Feb 2024 (senior advocate)

3 times in past papers · Asked occasionally: know the meaning, the section and one case.

🧠 Say it: “Dosa, Puri, Rava”

  1. DDosaDesignation (S.16)
  2. PPuriProcedure
  3. RRavaRestrictions
1

Designation (S.16)

S.16

Advocates are of two classes: senior advocates and other advocates. An advocate may, with his consent, be designated a senior advocate by the Supreme Court or a High Court if it is of opinion that, by virtue of his ability, standing at the Bar, or special knowledge or experience in law, he deserves the distinction.

💬 In simple words
  • Senior advocate: an advocate given special designation for ability and standing at the Bar.
🧠 Memory trick

Designated for ability · standing at the Bar · special knowledge or experience, with his consent (S.16).

2

Procedure

The Supreme Court laid down a transparent process in Indira Jaising v. Supreme Court of India (2017): a Permanent Committee and assessment of years of practice, judgments, publications and reputation, with the final decision by the Full Court; the process has since been revised by the Court.

🧠 Memory trick

Indira Jaising (2017): a Permanent Committee, points for years of practice, judgments, publications and reputation, then the Full Court.

3

Restrictions

A senior advocate shall not file a vakalatnama or act in any court; shall not appear without an advocate-on-record or junior; shall not draft pleadings or affidavits or do similar drafting work (he may settle them); and shall not accept instructions directly from a client; he may give advice on evidence and argue.

Example: a client wants a famous senior advocate to argue her appeal. She must first engage an ordinary advocate (or AOR in the Supreme Court), who files the vakalat and briefs the senior; the senior then argues.
🎨 Picture it

A specialist surgeon: he operates (argues) only after another doctor (a junior or AOR) admits the patient and hands over the file.

🌍 Real life

To get a famous senior to argue her appeal, a client first engages an ordinary advocate or AOR, who files the vakalat and briefs the senior.

🔁 Recall: A senior advocate is a specialist surgeon: he operates (argues) but only when another doctor (junior/AOR) admits the patient and hands over the file.

  • S.16senior and other advocates (designation by Supreme Court or High Court)

  • S.33 · S.45only enrolled advocates may practise · illegal practice: up to 6 months' imprisonment
  • Art. 19(1)(g) · 19(6)freedom of profession · reasonable restrictions and qualifications

6Case stories

Bar Council of India v. A.K. Balaji

(2018) 5 SCC 379 · decided 13 Mar 2018

📖 What happenedForeign law firms were advising clients in India and taking part in arbitrations without being enrolled under the Advocates Act.
❓ The questionCan foreign lawyers and law firms practise law in India, in litigation or advisory work?
⚖️ Held"Practice of law" covers litigation and non-litigation work; foreign lawyers cannot practise Indian law in India. They may advise on foreign law on a casual "fly in and fly out" basis, and appear in international commercial arbitrations, subject to rules.

Bar of Indian Lawyers v. D.K. Gandhi PS National Institute of Communicable Diseases

📖 What happenedA client filed a consumer complaint against his lawyer for deficiency in service, and the National Commission held advocates liable under consumer law.
❓ The questionCan an advocate be sued as a "service provider" for deficiency in service under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019?
⚖️ HeldThe legal profession is sui generis; an advocate's service is a contract of personal service and is outside the Consumer Protection Act, 2019. Remedies lie in civil courts and Bar Council proceedings.

7Golden rules

  • ★ 1The Advocates Act, 1961 created an All-India Bar with a single class of advocates (S.29).
  • ★ 2Practising law is a right of an enrolled advocate under the Act, subject to reasonable restrictions on the freedom of profession (Art. 19(1)(g), 19(6)).
  • ★ 3Foreign lawyers and firms cannot practise Indian law in India; only advising on foreign law on a casual basis is allowed. A.K. Balaji
  • ★ 4Lawyers' services are not covered by consumer law: the legal profession is sui generis. D.K. Gandhi
  • ★ 5A senior advocate is designated for ability and standing; he cannot file a vakalatnama or take instructions directly from a client.
