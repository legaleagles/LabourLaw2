1The big picture

A bank lends other people's money. Every loan carries a silent question: "Will it come back?" So a good banker asks five things before lending: Who is the borrower (character)? Can he repay (capacity)? How much of his own money is in it (capital)? What can we fall back on (collateral)? What is happening in his business and the economy (conditions)?

The fallback is the security, and the law gives a menu: hand over gold or goods (pledge), keep the goods but give the bank a charge (hypothecation), give an interest in land or a house (mortgage), or transfer a right such as an insurance policy (assignment). Each comes with its own rules, and getting them wrong can leave the bank with nothing.

1

Safety

The first rule: the money must come back. The bank checks the borrower's honesty, repaying capacity and security.

🧠 Memory trick

The first rule: the money must come back.

🏘️Real-life exampleWill the money come back?
  1. 📍 What happened

    A first-time borrower asks for ₹10 lakh to open a restaurant. He has a poor credit report and no security.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Safety is the first rule: the bank checks honesty, repaying capacity and security.

  3. ✅ Result

    The bank will refuse or reduce the loan until safety is shown.

2

Liquidity

Deposits are repayable on demand, so loans must be recoverable in time; a bank should not lock too much money in long, illiquid loans.

🎨 Picture it

Deposits are repayable on demand, so loans must not be locked away too long.

🏘️Real-life exampleAll the money in a 20-year loan
  1. 📍 What happened

    A bank wants to put most of its short-term deposits into 20-year infrastructure loans.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Liquidity: deposits are repayable on demand, so loans must be recoverable in time.

  3. ✅ Result

    Too much long, illiquid lending risks the bank not paying its depositors.

3

Profitability

The interest must cover the cost of deposits, expenses and risk; but profit must never override safety.

🧠 Memory trick

Profit must cover cost and risk, but never overrides safety.

🏘️Real-life exampleHigh interest, high risk
  1. 📍 What happened

    A borrower offers to pay a very high interest rate for a risky speculative venture.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Profitability: interest must cover deposit costs, expenses and risk, but profit must never override safety.

  3. ✅ Result

    A high rate does not make a bad loan good.

4

Purpose

The loan should be for a productive, lawful purpose that itself generates repayment; the bank must watch the end use.

🎨 Picture it

Where is the money going? A productive, lawful purpose that itself repays the loan; watch the end use.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe tractor loan used for a wedding
  1. 📍 What happened

    A farmer takes a tractor loan but spends it on a family wedding.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    The loan should be for a productive, lawful purpose that generates repayment; the bank must watch the end use.

  3. ✅ Result

    With no tractor earning income, repayment is at risk; the bank should have checked the end use.

5

Diversification

"Don't put all eggs in one basket": spread loans across borrowers, sectors and regions, within RBI's exposure limits, and meet priority-sector targets.

🧠 Memory trick

Don't put all your eggs in one basket: spread across borrowers, sectors and regions.

🏘️Real-life exampleAll eggs in one basket
  1. 📍 What happened

    A small bank lends 60% of its money to builders in one city just before a property slump.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Diversification: spread loans across borrowers, sectors and regions, within RBI exposure limits.

  3. ✅ Result

    The slump hits the bank hard; spreading the loans would have limited the damage.

6

Precautions (5 Cs)

S.20

Character (KYC, credit bureau report, past record); capacity (income, cash flows, project viability); capital (borrower's own stake, margin); collateral (title search, valuation, proper documentation and registration, insurance); conditions (industry and economy). After sanction: monitor end use, inspect stocks, review accounts, and act early on SMA signals. RBI bars loans to the bank's own directors (S.20 BR Act).

Example: before lending ₹30 lakh for a house, the bank checks the buyer's salary slips and credit score (character, capacity), insists on a 20% down payment (capital), gets a 30-year title search and registers the mortgage (collateral), and checks that the builder's project is approved (conditions).
💬 In simple words
  • Mortgage: giving a lender a right over land or a house as security for a loan.
🧠 Memory trick

The 5 Cs: Character · Capacity · Capital · Collateral · Conditions.

🌍 Real life

A ₹30 lakh home loan: salary slips and credit score (character, capacity), a 20% down payment (capital), a title search and registered mortgage (collateral).

🏘️Real-life exampleThe 5 Cs at the loan desk
  1. 📍 What happened

    A manager appraises a ₹50 lakh loan for a printing press.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    The 5 Cs: character (KYC, credit report), capacity (cash flows), capital (own stake), collateral (title, valuation, registration), conditions (industry). Then monitor end use.

  3. ✅ Result

    If all five check out, the loan is sanctioned and then monitored.

7

When lending goes wrong

Poor appraisal, over-optimism about big projects, weak governance and "evergreening" (fresh loans to repay old ones) turned many large loans into NPAs; after RBI's Asset Quality Review (2015) banks had to recognise them. The burden falls on everyone: banks charge higher fees and spreads, depositors earn less, and taxpayers fund recapitalisation. Remedies: better appraisal and early-warning systems, independent boards, RBI's prompt corrective action, the IBC, and wilful-defaulter rules.

📖 Word origin

Evergreening = fresh loans to repay old ones, hiding bad loans; RBI's Asset Quality Review (2015) forced banks to recognise them.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe loan to repay a loan
  1. 📍 What happened

    A struggling company gets a fresh loan from its bank just to pay the interest on its old loan.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    "Evergreening" hides bad loans; after RBI's Asset Quality Review (2015) banks had to recognise them as NPAs.

  3. ✅ Result

    It only delays the problem; the account should be recognised as stressed.

🔁 Recall: Lending is like lending your bicycle: will he return it (safety), when I need it (liquidity), will he pay rent (profit), where is he going (purpose), and have I lent all my bicycles to one person (diversification)?

1

Lien

S.171

Right to retain the debtor's goods until paid; the banker's general lien (S.171 Contract Act) is an implied pledge (see Cluster 7).

💬 In simple words
  • Banker's lien: the bank's right to hold a customer's securities until his dues are paid.
  • Pledge: handing over goods (like gold) to the lender as security.
🎨 Picture it

Lien is the bank's grip (see Cluster 7): retain until paid.

2

Pledge

S.172S.173S.174S.176S.177

Bailment of goods as security for a debt; the goods are delivered (actually or by handing over documents of title) to the pawnee. Pawnee's rights: retain for the debt, interest and expenses (S.173, S.174); on default, sue for the debt (keeping the goods as collateral) or sell after reasonable notice (S.176). Pawnor's right: redeem before sale (S.177). A pawnee who sues must be able to return the goods Lallan Prasad.

🎨 Picture it

The bank holds the goods.

🎬 Case link

Lallan Prasad: the pawnee may sue for the debt only if he can return the goods on payment.

🗣️ Jargon buster

Sell only after reasonable notice to the pawnor (S.176); he may redeem before that (S.177).

🏘️Real-life exampleGold handed over
  1. 📍 What happened

    A woman gives her gold bangles to a bank for a gold loan, and then defaults.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Pledge (S.172): goods are delivered as security. On default, the pawnee may sue for the debt or sell after reasonable notice (S.176).

  3. ✅ Result

    After notice, the bank can auction the bangles, and must return any surplus.

3

Hypothecation

S.2(1)(n)

A charge on movable property without delivery (SARFAESI S.2(1)(n)): the borrower keeps and uses the goods (a car, a truck, shop stock) and the bank has a charge, with the right to take possession on default. It is registered with CERSAI (and the Registrar of Companies for companies).

🎨 Picture it

The borrower keeps the goods; the bank has a charge and can take possession on default.

📖 Word origin

Hypothecation, from Greek hypotheke, "a pledge put under": security without handing over the thing.

🏘️Real-life exampleDriving the truck the bank has a charge on
  1. 📍 What happened

    A transporter buys a truck on a bank loan and drives it every day.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Hypothecation: a charge on movables without delivery; the borrower keeps and uses the goods; the bank can take possession on default. Registered with CERSAI.

  3. ✅ Result

    He drives the truck; if he defaults, the bank can take possession of it.

4

Mortgage

S.58S.58(f)S.60S.67S.69

Transfer of an interest in specific immovable property to secure a loan. Kinds: simple; by conditional sale; usufructuary; English; by deposit of title deeds (equitable mortgage, common in bank lending, in notified towns: S.58(f)); anomalous. The mortgagor may redeem (S.60); the mortgagee may sue for sale or foreclosure (S.67), in some cases sell without court (S.69), and banks can use SARFAESI.

Example: a gold loan is a pledge (gold kept by the bank); a car loan is a hypothecation (car with the owner, bank's charge noted in the RC); a home loan is a mortgage (title deeds deposited with the bank).
💬 In simple words
  • Hypothecation: a charge on goods (like a car) that stay with the borrower.
🎨 Picture it

The bank gets an interest in land or a house.

🌍 Real life

Gold loan = pledge (bank keeps the gold) · car loan = hypothecation (car with you, bank's charge in the RC) · home loan = mortgage (title deeds with the bank).

🏘️Real-life exampleTitle deeds with the bank
  1. 📍 What happened

    A businessman in a notified town deposits the original title deeds of his house with the bank for a loan, without a registered mortgage deed.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.58(f) TPA: a mortgage by deposit of title deeds (equitable mortgage) in notified towns, common in bank lending. The mortgagor can redeem (S.60).

  3. ✅ Result

    A valid equitable mortgage is created; he gets the deeds back on repayment.

5

Assignment

S.130S.38

Transfer of an actionable claim to the bank (S.130 TPA): book debts, receivables, and life insurance policies (assigned under S.38 Insurance Act and registered with the insurer).

🎨 Picture it

The bank holds a right: book debts, receivables, or a life insurance policy (S.130 TPA, S.38 Insurance Act).

🏘️Real-life exampleAssigning the insurance policy
  1. 📍 What happened

    A borrower assigns his life insurance policy to the bank as extra security.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Assignment of actionable claims (S.130 TPA); life policies are assigned under S.38 Insurance Act and registered with the insurer.

  3. ✅ Result

    If he defaults or dies, the bank can claim the policy money up to its dues.

6

Secured creditor

S.2(1)(zd)S.13

A bank, financial institution, ARC, debenture trustee and the like, in whose favour a security interest (mortgage, charge, hypothecation, assignment) is created. Only a secured creditor can enforce under S.13.

🧠 Memory trick

Only a secured creditor can use SARFAESI S.13. An unsecured personal loan means suing, the DRT, Lok Adalat or arbitration.

🏘️Real-life exampleOnly a secured creditor can use S.13
  1. 📍 What happened

    An unsecured supplier who is owed money tries to use SARFAESI to seize the debtor's factory.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.2(1)(zd): only a secured creditor (bank, ARC, debenture trustee) holding a security interest can enforce under S.13.

  3. ✅ Result

    The supplier cannot use SARFAESI.

🔁 Recall: Ask "who holds what?": bank holds goods = pledge; borrower holds goods = hypothecation; bank holds an interest in land = mortgage; bank holds a right (policy, debts) = assignment.

1

Goods

By pledge or hypothecation. Check ownership, quality and marketability; keep a margin for price falls; insure them; inspect godowns; avoid perishable goods.

🧠 Memory trick

Own it · Quality · Sell easily · Margin · Insure · Inspect; avoid perishables.

🏘️Real-life exampleLoan against rice stock
  1. 📍 What happened

    A rice mill borrows against its stock in a godown.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Advances against goods by pledge or hypothecation: check ownership and quality, keep a margin for price falls, insure, inspect godowns, avoid perishables.

  3. ✅ Result

    The bank lends only part of the stock's value and inspects the godown regularly.

2

Documents of title

S.2(4)

Bill of lading, warehouse receipt, railway receipt, delivery order (S.2(4) Sale of Goods Act). Delivering the document is constructive delivery of the goods, so the bank becomes pawnee. Check genuineness and that the goods are insured.

🎨 Picture it

Handing over a bill of lading or warehouse receipt is handing over the goods themselves (constructive delivery).

🏘️Real-life exampleThe warehouse receipt
  1. 📍 What happened

    A trader stores cotton in a warehouse and hands the warehouse receipt to the bank for a loan.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Delivering a document of title (warehouse receipt, bill of lading) is constructive delivery of the goods, so the bank becomes pawnee.

  3. ✅ Result

    The bank effectively holds the cotton as pledge.

3

Shares

Today almost all shares are in demat form and are pledged through the depository. Merits: easily valued and sold. Risks: price falls, partly-paid shares, company's own lien. Precautions: lend only on listed, fully paid shares, keep an adequate margin, follow RBI and SEBI limits.

🧠 Memory trick

Listed · Fully paid · Margin, pledged through the depository.

🏘️Real-life exampleLoan against demat shares
  1. 📍 What happened

    An investor pledges her listed shares through her depository for a loan.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Shares are pledged through the depository; lend only on listed, fully paid shares, with an adequate margin for price falls.

  3. ✅ Result

    If prices fall, the bank asks her to top up the margin.

4

Preference shares

S.55

A company can issue only redeemable preference shares, redeemable within 20 years (longer for infrastructure projects). They may be redeemed only if fully paid, and only out of profits available for dividend or the proceeds of a fresh issue; when redeemed out of profits, an equal sum goes to the capital redemption reserve.

🔢 Number trick

Only redeemable preference shares, within 20 years, fully paid, out of profits or a fresh issue (S.55).

🏘️Real-life exampleRedeeming preference shares
  1. 📍 What happened

    A company wants to redeem its preference shares out of borrowed money.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.55 Companies Act: only redeemable preference shares (within 20 years), redeemed only if fully paid, out of profits available for dividend or a fresh issue.

  3. ✅ Result

    Borrowed money is not a permitted source.

5

Life policies

S.38

The bank lends up to the surrender value; the policy is assigned to the bank (S.38 Insurance Act) and notice given to the insurer; premiums must be kept paid.

🧠 Memory trick

Lend up to the surrender value; assign the policy; tell the insurer; keep premiums paid.

🏘️Real-life exampleLoan against an LIC policy
  1. 📍 What happened

    A teacher with an LIC policy needs ₹1 lakh for her daughter's fees.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Banks lend up to the surrender value; the policy is assigned (S.38 Insurance Act) with notice to the insurer, and premiums must be kept paid.

  3. ✅ Result

    She can borrow up to the surrender value.

6

Land and buildings

By mortgage. Precautions: title search for many years, encumbrance certificate, valuation, approved plans, registration of the mortgage, insurance of the building. Agricultural land is outside SARFAESI.

🧠 Memory trick

Title search · Encumbrance certificate · Valuation · Approved plan · Registration · Insurance.

🗣️ Jargon buster

Nobody can mortgage what he does not own: a husband's mortgage of his wife's house without her consent is void against her.

🏘️Real-life exampleLending on a plot
  1. 📍 What happened

    A bank is asked for a loan against a plot in a new layout.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Precautions: title search, encumbrance certificate, valuation, approved plans, registration and insurance; agricultural land is outside SARFAESI.

  3. ✅ Result

    The bank lends only after a clean title and EC.

🔁 Recall: Walk through a borrower's house: goods in the shop, receipts in the drawer, shares in the demat account, a policy in the cupboard, and the house itself. Each can be security, each with its own precaution.

7Golden rules

  • ★ 1Pledge = possession with the bank; hypothecation = possession with the borrower; mortgage = interest in immovable property.
  • ★ 2A pawnee who sues for the debt must be able to return the pledged goods on payment. Lallan Prasad
  • ★ 3A pawnee may sell the pledged goods only after reasonable notice to the pawnor (S.176 Contract Act).
  • ★ 4Nobody can mortgage what he does not own: a mortgage by a non-owner is void against the true owner.
  • ★ 5An unsecured loan cannot be enforced under SARFAESI; the bank must sue, go to the DRT, or use Lok Adalat or arbitration.

8Part C: problem routes

Every problem takes the same four stops: issue → law → apply → conclusion.

1. A house stands in Malati's name. After marriage, her husband, without her knowledge, mortgaged it to a bank. Later she sold the house to someone and registered the sale. Advise the bank on recovering the debt.

  1. 🎯 IssueIs the mortgage valid against Malati and her purchaser, and what can the bank do?
  2. 📜 LawS.58 TPA: only a person entitled to the property (or authorised by him) can mortgage it; S.7 TPA: competence to transfer. "Nemo dat": no one can give a better title than he has. S.41 TPA protects a transferee from an ostensible owner only where the real owner consented. S.43 TPA: if the husband later acquires the property, the mortgage may attach to it. Bank's duty: title search before lending.
  3. 🔍 ApplyMalati is the owner and did not consent; the husband had no title. The mortgage does not bind her or the person who bought from her, and the bank was careless in not checking title.
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe bank cannot enforce the mortgage against the house or the purchaser. Its remedy is against the husband: sue him for the debt (DRT if ₹20 lakh or more), proceed against his other assets, and lodge a criminal complaint for cheating and forgery (BNS 318, 336). If Malati had consented or signed, the position would differ.

2. Udit takes a personal loan from Bank of Paka, pays two EMIs and stops paying. He ignores notices. How can the bank recover? Can it complain against him in a consumer court?

  1. 🎯 IssueWhat are a bank's remedies on an unsecured loan, and is the consumer forum available to it?
  2. 📜 LawNo security interest, so SARFAESI does not apply. Remedies: set-off against Udit's deposits; civil suit (summary suit under O.XXXVII CPC on a written contract); DRT if ₹20 lakh or more; pre-litigation Lok Adalat (award is final, Legal Services Authorities Act S.21); arbitration if agreed; S.138 NI Act or S.25 Payment and Settlement Systems Act if EMI cheques or e-mandates bounce; reporting to credit bureaus. RBI rules forbid harassment by recovery agents. Consumer Protection Act, 2019: a "consumer" is one who buys goods or avails services; the bank is the service provider.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe bank must use lawful recovery routes, not force. It is not a consumer in relation to its own loan; consumer commissions decide complaints against service providers, not recovery claims.
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe bank can recall the loan, set off his deposits, sue (or use Lok Adalat, arbitration or the DRT if the amount qualifies) and prosecute for bounced EMI cheques or mandates. It cannot go to a consumer court; Udit, not the bank, is the consumer.

3. A bank sold gold ornaments pledged for a gold loan two weeks after default, without informing the borrower. Advise the borrower.

  1. 🎯 IssueCan a pawnee sell pledged goods without notice?
  2. 📜 LawS.176 Contract Act: on default the pawnee may sell the goods after giving the pawnor reasonable notice of the sale; S.177: the pawnor may redeem before the actual sale. RBI's gold loan rules also require prior notice before auction Lallan Prasad.
  3. 🔍 ApplyNo notice was given, so the borrower lost his right to redeem. The sale is wrongful as against him.
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe borrower can claim damages from the bank (the market value of the gold minus the dues) and complain to the RBI Ombudsman; the bank cannot recover any shortfall without accounting for the gold's true value.
