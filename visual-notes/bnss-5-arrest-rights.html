1The big picture

Arrest takes away the most precious thing a person has: liberty. So the law treats arrest like a surgery: done only when necessary, by the right person, in the right way, with a written record, and with the patient (the arrested person) told what is happening and allowed to call his family and lawyer.

Joginder Kumar, a young advocate, was picked up and held for five days without reason; D.K. Basu wrote to the Supreme Court about deaths in police custody; Arnesh Kumar showed how husbands and their families were arrested routinely on dowry complaints. Each case added a safeguard, and the BNSS now writes most of them into the law.

1

Meaning

S.43

Arrest is the taking of a person into custody by authority of law, by actually touching or confining his body unless he submits by word or action (S.43). Ways of bringing a person before the court: summons, warrant, arrest without warrant, proclamation.

💬 In simple words
  • Warrant: a court's written order to the police to arrest someone.
  • Summons: a court's written order calling someone to appear.
📖 Word origin

Arrest comes from Old French arester, "to stop": the law stops a person's liberty.

🎨 Picture it

Arrest = touch or confine the body (S.43), unless he submits by word or action. If he says "I'll come with you", no touching is needed.

🧠 Memory trick

Four ways to bring a person to court: S-W-A-P → Summons · Warrant · Arrest without warrant · Proclamation.

🏘️Real-life exampleArrested without a hand being laid
  1. 📍 What happened

    Police tell a suspect at his shop, "You are under arrest, come with us." He quietly gets into the jeep.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.43: arrest is by touching or confining the body, unless he submits by word or action.

  3. ✅ Result

    By walking to the jeep he submitted: he is under arrest, though no one touched him.

2

Without warrant (S.35)

S.35(1)S.35(2)

A police officer may arrest without warrant a person who: (a) commits a cognizable offence in his presence; (b) is reasonably suspected of a cognizable offence punishable up to 7 years, only if he has reason to believe it and arrest is necessary (to prevent another offence, for proper investigation, to stop tampering or threats, or to secure attendance), recording reasons in writing; (c) is credibly accused of an offence punishable with more than 7 years or death; (d) is a proclaimed offender; (e) has stolen property; (f) obstructs a police officer or escapes custody; and others listed. For non-cognizable offences: no arrest without warrant or a Magistrate's order (S.35(2)).

💬 In simple words
  • Cognizable offence: a serious offence where police can arrest without a warrant and investigate on their own.
  • Proclaimed offender: an absconding accused officially declared by the court.
🧠 Memory trick

Three questions before handcuffs: Cognizable? Necessary? Reasons written?

🔢 Number trick

7 years is the dividing line: up to 7 years, arrest only if necessary and with written reasons; above 7 years or death, arrest on a credible accusation.

🌍 Real life

A man breaks into a parked car right in front of a constable: a cognizable offence in his presence, so he can be arrested without warrant.

🏘️Real-life exampleCaught in the act, or only suspected?
  1. 📍 What happened

    Police see a man snatching a purse. Separately, a man is suspected of cheating punishable with 5 years.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.35(1): arrest without warrant for a cognizable offence committed in the officer's presence; for one punishable up to 7 years only if he has reason to believe and arrest is necessary.

  3. ✅ Result

    The snatcher can be arrested on the spot. For the cheating suspect, arrest needs recorded reasons of necessity.

3

Notice of appearance

S.35(3)S.35(7)

Where arrest is not required, the officer issues a notice to appear. A person who complies shall not be arrested unless the officer records reasons; if he fails to comply, he may be arrested. Infirm persons or those above 60 cannot be arrested for offences below 3 years without a DSP's permission (S.35(7)).

Case story Arnesh Kumar: a husband feared arrest on a dowry-cruelty complaint. The Supreme Court noted that police were arresting husbands and their old parents mechanically. It directed that for offences up to 7 years the officer must satisfy himself that arrest is necessary, fill a checklist, and send it to the Magistrate; otherwise he must issue a notice to appear. Officers who disobey face departmental action and contempt.
💬 In simple words
  • Complaint: a direct allegation of a crime made to a Magistrate, not to the police.
🎨 Picture it

A "please come" letter before handcuffs: the notice of appearance (S.35(3)). Comply, and you are not arrested unless reasons are recorded.

🎬 Case link

Arnesh Kumar: no mechanical arrests of husbands and old parents on dowry-cruelty complaints; record why arrest is necessary.

🔢 Number trick

60+ or infirm, offence below 3 years: arrest only with a DSP's permission (S.35(7)).

🏘️Real-life exampleNotice instead of arrest
  1. 📍 What happened

    A man accused of an offence punishable with 3 years is cooperating and has a fixed address. In another case the accused is 68 years old.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.35(3)–(6): where arrest is not required, a notice to appear is issued; one who complies is not arrested without recorded reasons. S.35(7): those above 60 or infirm are not arrested for offences below 3 years without a DSP's permission (Arnesh Kumar).

  3. ✅ Result

    The first gets a notice, not handcuffs. The 68-year-old needs a DSP's permission if the offence is below 3 years.

4

With warrant

A warrant is a written order of a court, signed and sealed, directing a police officer to arrest a named person and bring him before the court. It stays in force until cancelled or executed. It is used where the court has issued summons and the person does not appear, or for offences where the court decides to compel appearance.

🎨 Picture it

A warrant is the court's arrest ticket with a name on it: written, signed, sealed, valid till cancelled or executed.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe witness who never came
  1. 📍 What happened

    A summoned accused ignores three court dates in a criminal case.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    A warrant is a written, signed and sealed court order to arrest a named person and bring him to court; it stays in force until cancelled or executed.

  3. ✅ Result

    The court issues a warrant, and police bring him before it.

5

Procedure of arrest

S.36S.37S.43(3)S.43

S.36: the officer wears a clear identification and prepares an arrest memo attested by a family member or local witness and countersigned by the arrested person. S.37: a designated police officer in each district and station displays names of those arrested. S.43(3): handcuffs may be used only for habitual or repeat offenders, escapees and listed grave offences. A woman is arrested only by a woman officer and, except in exceptional cases with a Magistrate's permission, not after sunset and before sunrise (S.43).

🧠 Memory trick

Badge · Memo · Board · Rarely cuffs · Woman by woman: ID worn, arrest memo (S.36), names displayed (S.37), handcuffs only for listed cases (S.43(3)), a woman arrested by a woman officer (S.43).

🎨 Picture it

The arrest memo is like a delivery receipt: attested by a family member or local witness, countersigned by the arrested person.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe arrest memo
  1. 📍 What happened

    A man is arrested at night. His wife wants to know who arrested him and where he has been taken.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.36: the officer wears clear identification and prepares an arrest memo attested by a family member or local witness. S.37: names of those arrested are displayed. S.43(3): handcuffs only for habitual offenders and listed grave cases.

  3. ✅ Result

    The wife can see the memo and the displayed list. Handcuffing an ordinary first-time accused would be illegal.

6

Special persons

Members of the armed forces (prior Government consent for official acts); judges: arrest of a judicial officer follows the Supreme Court's guidelines Delhi Judicial Service Assn., and no criminal case is registered against a High Court or Supreme Court judge without consulting the Chief Justice of India (K. Veeraswami v. Union of India, 1991).

🎬 Case link

Delhi Judicial Service Assn.: a judicial officer's arrest needs immediate intimation to the District Judge or High Court, and no handcuffs.

🗣️ Jargon buster

K. Veeraswami (1991): no criminal case against a High Court or Supreme Court judge without consulting the Chief Justice of India.

🏘️Real-life exampleArresting a judge
  1. 📍 What happened

    Police want to register a corruption case against a sitting High Court judge.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    No criminal case against a High Court or Supreme Court judge without consulting the Chief Justice of India (K. Veeraswami).

  3. ✅ Result

    The police must first consult the CJI.

🔁 Recall: Ask the officer three questions: "Is it cognizable? Is arrest necessary? Have you written your reasons?" If any answer is "no", issue a notice, not handcuffs.

Right to know grounds

S.47(1)Art. 22S.47(2)

The arrested person must be told the full particulars of the offence or grounds of arrest at once (S.47(1), Art. 22(1)); for a bailable offence, also that he is entitled to bail (S.47(2)).

💬 In simple words
  • Bail: release from custody on a promise to come back to court.
  • Bailable offence: a less serious offence where bail is a right, not a favour.
🎨 Picture it

"Why me?" must be answered at once: the grounds of arrest (S.47(1), Art. 22(1)), and for a bailable offence, that he is entitled to bail (S.47(2)).

🏘️Real-life example"Why am I being taken?"
  1. 📍 What happened

    A man is picked up and asks what he is accused of. The officer says, "You will know at the station."

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.47(1), Art. 22(1): the arrested person must be told the grounds of arrest at once; for a bailable offence, also that he is entitled to bail.

  3. ✅ Result

    Refusing to tell him is illegal; the arrest can be challenged.

2

Inform family

S.48

The officer must inform a relative or friend nominated by him, and the designated police officer, of the arrest and the place of detention (S.48), and enter it in a register.

🎨 Picture it

One phone call: a relative or friend he names, and the designated police officer, are told of the arrest and the place of detention (S.48).

🏘️Real-life exampleHis mother doesn't know
  1. 📍 What happened

    A college student is arrested at 11 pm. He names his mother to be informed.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.48: the officer must inform a relative or friend nominated by him of the arrest and place of detention, and record it.

  3. ✅ Result

    The police must call his mother and tell her where he is.

3

Lawyer and legal aid

Art. 22S.38S.341Art. 39A

Right to consult a legal practitioner of his choice (Art. 22(1)); right to meet an advocate during interrogation, though not throughout (S.38). An accused who cannot afford a lawyer gets legal aid at State expense (S.341, Art. 39A); a trial without it, where it was needed, is vitiated.

🧠 Memory trick

Can't afford a lawyer? The State pays (S.341, Art. 39A). A trial without needed legal aid is vitiated.

🔢 Number trick

S.38: a lawyer during interrogation, though not throughout.

🏘️Real-life exampleNo money for a lawyer
  1. 📍 What happened

    A poor labourer is tried for a serious offence and cannot afford an advocate.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Right to a lawyer of choice (Art. 22(1)); to meet an advocate during interrogation (S.38); and legal aid at State expense (S.341, Art. 39A). A trial without needed legal aid is vitiated.

  3. ✅ Result

    The court must assign him a legal-aid lawyer; otherwise any conviction can be set aside.

4

Medical examination

S.53S.51

By a medical officer soon after arrest; a woman by a female medical officer (S.53); at his own request to show torture (S.53, and S.51 at the police's request).

🎨 Picture it

The doctor's check is a shield against torture: at his own request he can be examined to show torture (S.53). A woman is examined by a female medical officer.

🏘️Real-life exampleBruises after custody
  1. 📍 What happened

    An arrested man says he was beaten in the police station.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.53: medical examination soon after arrest, a woman by a female doctor; at his own request to show torture.

  3. ✅ Result

    He asks the Magistrate for a medical examination, which records the injuries.

5

24-hour production

S.58Art. 22S.187

Produced before a Magistrate within 24 hours, excluding journey time (S.58, Art. 22(2)); further custody only by a Magistrate's order (S.187).

🔢 Number trick

24 hours, journey time excluded (S.58, Art. 22(2)). After that, custody only by a Magistrate's order (S.187).

🧠 Memory trick

The sun may rise only once before he sees a Magistrate.

🏘️Real-life exampleArrested Friday, held till Monday
  1. 📍 What happened

    A man is arrested on Friday evening and the police plan to produce him "after the weekend".

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.58, Art. 22(2): produce before a Magistrate within 24 hours, excluding journey time; further custody only by a Magistrate's order (S.187).

  3. ✅ Result

    Holding him till Monday is illegal detention; he must be produced by Saturday evening (a duty Magistrate is available).

6

Silence

Art. 20

No person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself (Art. 20(3)); in police questioning he may refuse to answer incriminating questions (Nandini Satpathy v. P.L. Dani, 1978). Narco-analysis, polygraph and brain-mapping without consent violate Art. 20(3) and 21 (Selvi v. State of Karnataka, 2010).

📖 Word origin

Self-incrimination = being made a witness against yourself. Art. 20(3) forbids compelling it.

🧠 Memory trick

No narco, no polygraph, no brain-mapping without consent (Selvi, 2010). He may refuse to answer incriminating police questions (Nandini Satpathy, 1978).

🏘️Real-life example"Take the lie-detector test"
  1. 📍 What happened

    Police insist that an accused undergo a polygraph and narco-analysis.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Art. 20(3): no compulsion to be a witness against oneself; such tests without consent violate Art. 20(3) and 21 (Selvi).

  3. ✅ Result

    He can refuse; a forced test is unconstitutional.

7

Confessions

S.183S.36S.58

A confession is admissible only if recorded by a Judicial Magistrate (not a police officer), after warning the accused that he is not bound to confess and that it may be used against him, and after giving him time to reflect, free from police presence; the Magistrate certifies it was voluntary. Statements of witnesses may also be recorded (compulsory for some offences, and by video where possible).

Case story D.K. Basu: a legal aid activist wrote to the Chief Justice about deaths in police lock-ups in West Bengal. Treating the letter as a petition, the Supreme Court laid down 11 requirements (identification of officers, arrest memo, informing relatives, medical checks, access to a lawyer, informing the control room) and held that compensation is a remedy for custodial violence. The BNSS now writes most of them into S.36–S.58.
🎨 Picture it

A confession room with the police outside the door: a Judicial Magistrate warns him, gives time to reflect, records it and certifies it was voluntary.

🧠 Memory trick

Confession to the police = zero value.

🎬 Case link

D.K. Basu: eleven requirements for every arrest; custodial torture violates Art. 21, and the State pays compensation.

🏘️Real-life exampleConfession in front of the police
  1. 📍 What happened

    After 2 days in custody, an accused signs a confession written by the police. Later, a Magistrate records his confession.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.183: a confession is admissible only if recorded by a Judicial Magistrate, after warning him he need not confess, giving time to reflect, free from police presence.

  3. ✅ Result

    The police-written confession is inadmissible. The Magistrate's one counts only if these safeguards were followed.

🔁 Recall: Count on one hand plus two: Grounds, Family, Lawyer, Doctor, 24 hours (S.47, 48, 38, 53, 58), then Silence (Art. 20(3)) and Confession only before a Magistrate (S.183).

6Golden rules

  • ★ 1Arrest is not a must for offences up to 7 years: the officer must record why arrest is necessary, else issue a notice to appear (S.35(3)). Arnesh Kumar
  • ★ 224 hours: every arrested person is produced before a Magistrate, excluding journey time (S.58, Art. 22(2)).
  • ★ 3Custodial violence is never allowed; illegal detention gives a right to compensation. D.K. Basu
  • ★ 4A judicial officer is arrested only with immediate intimation to the District Judge / High Court; no handcuffs. Delhi Judicial Service Assn.
  • ★ 5A confession to the police is inadmissible; only one recorded by a Judicial Magistrate under S.183 can be used.

7Part C: problem routes

Every problem takes the same four stops: issue → law → apply → conclusion.

1. A was arrested and kept in illegal custody for 7 days. What remedies does he have against the police?

  1. 🎯 IssueWhat remedies lie for illegal detention beyond 24 hours?
  2. 📜 LawS.58 BNSS and Art. 22(2): production within 24 hours. Art. 21. Remedies: habeas corpus (Art. 32 / 226); compensation for violation of Art. 21 (Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar, 1983; D.K. Basu); criminal complaint for wrongful confinement (BNS 127); departmental action; S.399 BNSS compensation for groundless arrest.
  3. 🔍 ApplySeven days without production is plainly illegal. He can seek release, compensation from the State, and prosecution of the officers.
  4. ✅ ConclusionHabeas corpus for release, compensation under Art. 32 / 226, a criminal complaint for wrongful confinement and departmental action against the officers.

2. A judge is involved in a murder (or corruption) case. Can the police arrest him? How?

  1. 🎯 IssueHow is a judicial officer arrested?
  2. 📜 LawNo absolute immunity. Guidelines Delhi Judicial Service Assn.: immediate intimation to the District Judge or High Court; formal arrest only if immediate arrest is necessary; no handcuffs; no statements recorded without his lawyer and the District Judge's presence; medical examination; no police custody without the District Judge's permission. For High Court / Supreme Court judges: no case registered without consulting the Chief Justice of India (K. Veeraswami).
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe police may arrest a subordinate judge in a murder case, following the guidelines; the trial is by the Court of Session (murder, BNS 103).
  4. ✅ ConclusionYes, the police can arrest a subordinate judge, but only in line with the Supreme Court's guidelines and with immediate intimation to the District Judge / High Court; for superior court judges, the CJI must be consulted first.

3. Mr. X is involved in a conspiracy. Can a police officer arrest him without warrant?

  1. 🎯 IssueIs criminal conspiracy an arrestable (cognizable) offence?
  2. 📜 LawBNS 61: criminal conspiracy; its punishment follows the offence conspired. S.35(1): arrest without warrant for cognizable offences, subject to necessity for offences up to 7 years. First Schedule: conspiracy to commit a cognizable offence is cognizable.
  3. 🔍 ApplyIf the conspiracy is to commit a serious (cognizable) offence, the police may arrest without warrant, recording reasons where the punishment is up to 7 years; if the object is non-cognizable, a warrant is needed.
  4. ✅ ConclusionYes, if the conspiracy is to commit a cognizable offence (subject to the necessity test of S.35); otherwise only with a warrant.

4. An accused is denied a lawyer during his trial. He says this violates his right to a fair trial. Decide.

  1. 🎯 IssueIs the right to counsel part of a fair trial?
  2. 📜 LawArt. 22(1): right to consult and be defended by a lawyer of choice. Art. 21 and 39A: free legal aid. S.341 BNSS: legal aid at State expense where the accused has no means. (Hussainara Khatoon, 1979; Khatri v. State of Bihar, 1981.)
  3. 🔍 ApplyA trial without a defence lawyer, where the accused wanted one or could not afford one, is not a fair trial.
  4. ✅ ConclusionHis claim is right; the trial is vitiated, and a retrial must be held with a lawyer (legal aid if needed).

5. Z was arrested for dowry harassment (cruelty). Later his wife dies due to the harassment. Who can file a case, and is he punishable for her death?

  1. 🎯 IssueWhat offences arise, and who may set the law in motion?
  2. 📜 LawCruelty by husband: BNS 85 (cognizable, non-bailable). Dowry death: BNS 80 (death within 7 years of marriage, otherwise than normal, with cruelty soon before death; minimum 7 years, up to life). Abetment of suicide where applicable. Presumption of dowry death under the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam. Inquest by the Executive Magistrate (S.194).
  3. 🔍 ApplyAnyone (her parents, relatives, the police on information) can give the FIR, since the offences are cognizable. If she died within 7 years of marriage after harassment for dowry, it is dowry death.
  4. ✅ ConclusionHer relatives or anyone may lodge an FIR; Z can be tried for dowry death (BNS 80) besides cruelty (BNS 85), by the Court of Session.
