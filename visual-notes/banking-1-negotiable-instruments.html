1The big picture

A negotiable instrument is money on paper that can travel. Ravi buys goods from Sita and gives her a written promise to pay ₹50,000 in three months. Sita needs cash today, so she signs the back and hands it to Gopal, who pays her ₹49,000. Gopal can now collect the full ₹50,000 from Ravi. The paper moved from hand to hand like a currency note, and each honest buyer got a clean title.

Three papers do this job under S.13 of the NI Act: the promissory note (a promise to pay: two parties), the bill of exchange (an order to pay: three parties) and the cheque (an order on a banker, payable on demand). The person who buys such paper honestly and for value, the holder in due course, is the hero of this Act.

1

Meaning

S.13

S.13 NI Act, 1881: a negotiable instrument means a promissory note, bill of exchange or cheque payable either to order or to bearer. "Negotiable" means transferable by delivery or by indorsement and delivery, so that the transferee gets the right to sue in his own name.

💬 In simple words
  • Negotiable instrument: a paper like a cheque or promissory note that can be passed on, giving the new holder the right to the money.
  • Promissory note: a signed written promise to pay a fixed sum.
🎨 Picture it

Money on paper that can travel: Ravi's ₹50,000 promise passes from Sita to Gopal like a currency note.

📖 Word origin

Negotiable comes from Latin negotiari, "to do business": paper that can be traded.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe cheque passed on to a supplier
  1. 📍 What happened

    A customer pays a garment shop owner with a cheque "Pay Anil or order". Anil signs the back and hands it to his cloth supplier.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.13: a promissory note, bill or cheque payable to order or to bearer is negotiable: transferable by indorsement and delivery, giving the transferee the right to sue in his own name.

  3. ✅ Result

    The supplier now owns the cheque and can sue in his own name if it bounces.

2

Features

S.47S.48S.9S.53S.118

(1) Easy transfer: bearer paper by mere delivery, order paper by indorsement and delivery (S.47, S.48). (2) Better title: a holder in due course gets a title free from defects of earlier parties (S.9, S.53). (3) Right to sue in his own name; no notice of transfer to the debtor is needed. (4) Presumptions in favour of the holder (S.118). (5) Always for a sum of money only, in writing and signed.

💬 In simple words
  • Holder in due course: someone who honestly took the instrument for value before it was due, and gets a clean right to it.
  • Indorsement: signing the back of a cheque or bill to transfer it to someone else.
🧠 Memory trick

Easy transfer · Better title · Sue in own name · Presumptions · Money only.

🏘️Real-life exampleBetter title than the seller had
  1. 📍 What happened

    A trader honestly buys a bearer cheque for value. Later it turns out an earlier holder had obtained it by fraud.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    A holder in due course gets a title free from defects of earlier parties (S.9, S.53); no notice of transfer is needed; S.118 presumptions help him.

  3. ✅ Result

    The honest trader can still enforce the cheque despite the earlier fraud.

3

Kinds under the Act

S.4S.5S.6

Only three are named: promissory note (S.4), bill of exchange (S.5) and cheque (S.6). A cheque is a special bill drawn on a banker and payable on demand.

Example: a ₹10,000 cheque "pay Sita or order" can be indorsed by Sita to Gopal and by Gopal to Hari. Hari, if he took it honestly and for value, can sue every earlier signer if the cheque bounces.
💬 In simple words
  • Bill of exchange: a signed written order telling someone to pay a fixed sum.
🧠 Memory trick

Promise (note) · Order (bill) · Order on a bank, on demand (cheque).

🏘️Real-life exampleNote, bill or cheque?
  1. 📍 What happened

    A borrower signs "I promise to pay ₹50,000 to Ravi". A trader writes to his buyer "Pay ₹50,000 to my supplier in 60 days". A depositor writes on his bank's leaf "Pay ₹50,000 to Gopal".

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Only three are named by the Act: promissory note (S.4), bill of exchange (S.5) and cheque (S.6), a special bill drawn on a banker and payable on demand.

  3. ✅ Result

    The first is a note, the second a bill, the third a cheque.

4

Deemed negotiable

S.13

S.13 is not exhaustive. By mercantile usage, hundis, bank drafts, dividend warrants, share warrants to bearer and bearer debentures are treated as negotiable. Postal orders, money orders, deposit receipts and share certificates are not negotiable.

🧠 Memory trick

Negotiable by custom: hundis, bank drafts, dividend warrants, bearer debentures. Not: postal orders, money orders, deposit receipts, share certificates.

📖 Word origin

A hundi is the traditional Indian bill of exchange.

🏘️Real-life exampleIs a fixed deposit receipt negotiable?
  1. 📍 What happened

    A man tries to sell his bank fixed-deposit receipt and his postal order by simply handing them over.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    By mercantile custom, hundis, bank drafts and dividend warrants are negotiable; postal orders, deposit receipts and share certificates are not.

  3. ✅ Result

    Handing over the deposit receipt or postal order does not transfer the right to the money.

5

Inland and foreign

S.11S.12S.104

An instrument drawn or made in India and payable in India (or drawn on a person resident in India) is inland; any other is foreign. A foreign bill must be protested on dishonour where the law of the place where it was drawn requires it (S.104).

💬 In simple words
  • Dishonour: the bank or drawee refusing to pay or accept the instrument.
🧠 Memory trick

Born in India and paid in India → inland; anything else → foreign (S.11, S.12).

🏘️Real-life exampleA bill drawn in Dubai
  1. 📍 What happened

    A Hyderabad exporter receives a bill drawn in Dubai and payable in Hyderabad.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.11–12: drawn or made in India and payable in India is inland; anything else is foreign. A foreign bill may need protest on dishonour (S.104).

  3. ✅ Result

    It is a foreign bill; if dishonoured, protest may be needed.

6

Inchoate instrument

S.20

When a person signs and delivers a stamped paper blank or incomplete, he authorises the holder to complete it for any amount not exceeding what the stamp covers; he is liable to a holder in due course for that amount Bir Singh.

📖 Word origin

Inchoate = just begun, incomplete.

🎨 Picture it

A signed blank stamped paper is a blank ticket: the holder may fill it up to what the stamp covers.

🎬 Case link

Bir Singh: a signed cheque handed over voluntarily carries the presumption of debt, even if someone else filled it in.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe blank signed cheque
  1. 📍 What happened

    A man gives a friend a signed cheque with the amount left blank "to fill in later". The friend fills in ₹2 lakh.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.20: signing and delivering an incomplete instrument authorises the holder to complete it; the signer is liable (Bir Singh).

  3. ✅ Result

    The signer is liable for ₹2 lakh; he took the risk by signing blank.

7

Presumptions

S.118S.119

Until the contrary is proved, it is presumed: (a) the instrument was made for consideration; (b) it was made on the date it bears; (c) a bill was accepted within a reasonable time after its date and before maturity; (d) transfer was before maturity; (e) indorsements were made in the order they appear; (f) a lost instrument was duly stamped; (g) the holder is a holder in due course. S.119: on proof of protest, the court presumes dishonour.

🎨 Picture it

The guard who believes the passenger: consideration, date, timely acceptance, transfer before maturity and holder in due course are all presumed until disproved (S.118).

🏘️Real-life examplePresumed for value
  1. 📍 What happened

    In a cheque case, the drawer says, "I never got anything in return for this cheque," but offers no proof.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.118: until the contrary is proved, an instrument is presumed to be for consideration, made on its date, and indorsed in the order shown.

  3. ✅ Result

    The court presumes consideration; the drawer must prove otherwise.

🔁 Recall: Think of a travelling rupee note: who made it (kinds), how it travels (features), who else is allowed on the train (deemed), where it was born (inland/foreign), the blank ticket (inchoate) and the guard who believes the passenger (presumptions).

Promissory note (S.4)

S.4

An instrument in writing (not a bank or currency note) containing an unconditional undertaking, signed by the maker, to pay a certain sum of money only to, or to the order of, a certain person, or to the bearer.

🎨 Picture it

Thumb up = a promise: "I promise to pay B or order ₹500" is a note.

🏘️Real-life exampleA promise on paper
  1. 📍 What happened

    A farmer signs: "I promise to pay Lakshmi Traders or order ₹1,00,000 on 1 December 2025."

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.4: a promissory note is an unconditional undertaking in writing, signed by the maker, to pay a certain sum of money to a certain person or his order.

  3. ✅ Result

    This is a valid promissory note.

2

Essentials

S.4

(1) In writing; (2) an express promise, not a mere acknowledgment ("Mr B, I.O.U. ₹1,000" is not a note); (3) unconditional; (4) signed by the maker; (5) maker and payee certain; (6) sum certain; (7) money only (S.4 illustrations: "and deliver my black horse" fails); (8) properly stamped.

S.4 illustrations to quote: "I promise to pay B or order ₹500" is a note. "I acknowledge myself indebted to B in ₹1,000, to be paid on demand, for value received" is also a note. But "I promise to pay B ₹500 seven days after my marriage with C" is not: the marriage may never happen.
🧠 Memory trick

"Wise People Usually Sign Clear Contracts, Mostly": Writing · Promise · Unconditional · Signed · Certain parties · Certain sum · Money only.

🌍 Real life

"Mr B, I.O.U. ₹1,000" is only an acknowledgment, not a promise, so it is not a note. "And deliver my black horse" fails: money only.

🏘️Real-life example"IOU" and "if I win"
  1. 📍 What happened

    One slip says "Mr B, I.O.U. ₹1,000". Another says "I promise to pay ₹5,000 if I win the election". A third adds "and deliver my bullock".

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Essentials: an express promise (not a mere acknowledgment), unconditional, and for money only.

  3. ✅ Result

    None of the three is a promissory note: acknowledgment, condition, and goods.

3

Bill of exchange (S.5)

S.5S.7

An instrument in writing containing an unconditional order, signed by the maker, directing a certain person to pay a certain sum of money only to, or to the order of, a certain person or to the bearer. Parties: drawer (orders), drawee (ordered; becomes acceptor on signing) and payee (S.7).

💬 In simple words
  • Drawer and drawee: the drawer writes the cheque or bill; the drawee (the bank, for a cheque) is told to pay.
🎨 Picture it

Finger pointing = an order: the drawer orders, the drawee pays (and becomes the acceptor), the payee receives.

🏘️Real-life exampleAn order to pay
  1. 📍 What happened

    A seller of rice draws on his buyer: "Pay ₹2 lakh to my supplier or order, 90 days after date," and the buyer signs "Accepted".

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.5: a bill is an unconditional order to pay. Parties: drawer (seller), drawee who becomes acceptor on signing (buyer), and payee (supplier).

  3. ✅ Result

    The buyer is now the acceptor and primarily liable.

4

Note v. bill

S.30S.32S.31S.104

(1) Note = promise; bill = order. (2) Note has two parties; bill has three. (3) Maker of a note is primarily liable; drawer of a bill is liable only if the acceptor fails (S.30, S.32). (4) A bill needs acceptance (unless on demand); a note does not. (5) A note cannot be payable to bearer (RBI Act S.31 bars it); a bill on demand to bearer is also barred. (6) Protest is mainly a requirement for foreign bills (S.104).

🧠 Memory trick

Promise v. order · 2 v. 3 parties · a bill needs acceptance, a note does not.

🏘️Real-life exampleWho pays first?
  1. 📍 What happened

    A note and a bill both remain unpaid at maturity.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    The maker of a note is primarily liable; the drawer of a bill is liable only if the acceptor fails. A bill needs acceptance; a note does not.

  3. ✅ Result

    The note-holder goes after the maker; the bill-holder first after the acceptor.

5

Maturity and grace

S.21S.22S.23S.24S.25

A note or bill not payable on demand is at maturity on the third day after the day it is expressed to be payable (S.22: three days of grace). "So many months after date": the corresponding date of that month, or the last day if none (S.23). "So many days after date": exclude the day of the date (S.24). If maturity falls on a public holiday, it is payable on the preceding business day (S.25).

💬 In simple words
  • Days of grace: three extra days allowed after the due date of a bill or note.
🔢 Number trick

3 days of grace (S.22). A note dated 31 July payable "one month after date" is payable 31 August, and at maturity on 3 September.

🗣️ Jargon buster

Maturity on a public holiday? Payable on the preceding business day (S.25).

🏘️Real-life exampleThree extra days
  1. 📍 What happened

    A bill dated 1 January is payable "3 months after date".

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.22–23: three days of grace; "months after date" falls on the corresponding date.

  3. ✅ Result

    Due on 1 April; with grace, it matures on 4 April.

6

At sight, after sight

S.21

"At sight" and "on presentment" mean on demand (no grace days). "After sight" means after acceptance or protest for non-acceptance (bill), or after presentment for sight (note).

🧠 Memory trick

At sight = on demand: no days of grace.

🏘️Real-life examplePay at sight
  1. 📍 What happened

    A bill says "Pay at sight". Another says "Pay 30 days after sight".

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.21: "at sight" means on demand (no grace); "after sight" runs from acceptance (for a bill).

  3. ✅ Result

    The first is payable immediately; the second 30 days after acceptance, plus grace.

🔁 Recall: Hold up your hand: thumb = Promise (note), finger pointing = Order (bill). Count three fingers for the three parties and three more for the three days of grace.

1

Holder (S.8)

S.8

The person entitled in his own name to possession of the instrument and to receive or recover the amount due. A thief or a finder of an order instrument is not a holder.

🗣️ Jargon buster

A thief or a finder of an order instrument is not a holder.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe finder is not a holder
  1. 📍 What happened

    A man finds a lost cheque payable "to Sita or order" on a bus and tries to cash it.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.8: a holder is one entitled in his own name to possess and recover. A finder or thief of an order instrument is not a holder.

  3. ✅ Result

    He is not a holder and gets no right to the money.

2

Holder in due course (S.9)

S.9S.58

A person who, for consideration, became the possessor (bearer instrument) or the payee or indorsee (order instrument) before the amount became payable, and without sufficient cause to believe that any defect existed in the title of the person from whom he derived his title. Three tests: value, before maturity, good faith.

Example: A obtains B's acceptance to a bill by fraud and indorses it to C, who pays full value without knowing of the fraud. C is a holder in due course and can recover from B; the fraud is no defence against C (S.58 bars only the wrongdoer and those taking from him with notice).
🧠 Memory trick

V-B-G: Value paid · Before maturity · Good faith.

🌍 Real life

A gets B's acceptance by fraud and indorses the bill to C, who pays full value honestly: C recovers from B.

🏘️Real-life exampleValue, time and good faith
  1. 📍 What happened

    A trader takes a cheque for goods supplied, a month before its date, with no reason to suspect anything.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.9: a holder in due course takes for consideration, before maturity, and without cause to believe there was any defect.

  3. ✅ Result

    He is a holder in due course.

3

Privileges

S.9S.58S.36S.53S.120

(1) Gets a title free from defects of prior parties (S.9, S.58). (2) Every prior party is liable to him until the instrument is satisfied (S.36). (3) A later holder who takes from an HDC gets the same rights, even without value, unless he was party to a fraud (S.53). (4) Estoppel: the maker, drawer or acceptor cannot deny the original validity of the instrument against him (S.120); the maker or acceptor cannot deny the payee's capacity to indorse (S.121). (5) Even an inchoate instrument binds the signer to him (S.20).

🎨 Picture it

A clean title, like a fresh currency note: earlier defects do not follow him.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe fraud that cannot be raised
  1. 📍 What happened

    The drawer of that cheque says the person he first gave it to had cheated him.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    A holder in due course takes free from defects of prior parties; every prior party is liable to him (S.36).

  3. ✅ Result

    The drawer must pay the trader and pursue the cheat separately.

4

Payment in due course (S.10)

S.10S.85S.128

Payment according to the apparent tenor of the instrument, in good faith and without negligence, to any person in possession of it under circumstances which do not give reasonable ground to believe he is not entitled to payment. Such payment discharges the instrument and protects the payer (and the paying banker: S.85, S.128).

💬 In simple words
  • Paying banker: the drawer's bank, which pays the cheque.
🧠 Memory trick

The mirror of V-B-G: apparent tenor · good faith · no negligence.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe bank that paid honestly
  1. 📍 What happened

    A bank pays a cheque in good faith, without negligence, to a person holding it who looks entitled. It later turns out he was not.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.10: payment in due course, according to the apparent tenor, in good faith and without negligence, discharges the instrument and protects the payer.

  3. ✅ Result

    The bank is protected.

🔁 Recall: HDC is a V-B-G customer: Value paid, Before maturity, Good faith. Payment in due course is the mirror: apparent tenor, good faith, no negligence.

Presentment for acceptance

S.61S.62

A bill payable after sight must be presented to the drawee for acceptance within a reasonable time after it is drawn, on a business day and at a reasonable hour; in default, no party to the bill is liable to the holder who made the default. A note payable at a certain period after sight must be presented to the maker for sight (S.62).

🎨 Picture it

The courier knocks on the drawee's door for acceptance, within a reasonable time, on a business day.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe bill nobody showed the buyer
  1. 📍 What happened

    A holder of a bill payable "60 days after sight" keeps it in his drawer for a year without presenting it for acceptance.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.61: a bill payable after sight must be presented for acceptance within a reasonable time; otherwise no party is liable to that holder.

  3. ✅ Result

    By his delay the holder loses his rights against the parties.

2

Presentment for payment

S.64S.68S.65S.72S.84

Every instrument must be presented for payment to the maker, acceptor or drawee by or on behalf of the holder, at the proper place (S.68–70) and during business hours (S.65); a cheque within a reasonable time to charge the drawer (S.72, S.84). Without proper presentment, the other parties are not liable to the holder.

🧠 Memory trick

Right place, right time (business hours), right person.

🏘️Real-life examplePresent it at the right place
  1. 📍 What happened

    A note says it is payable at the maker's shop in Begum Bazar. The holder demands payment at the maker's home at midnight.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.64–75: presentment for payment at the proper place, during business hours; a cheque within a reasonable time to charge the drawer.

  3. ✅ Result

    That is not proper presentment; he must present at the shop during business hours.

3

When excused

S.76S.75A

If the maker or drawee intentionally prevents presentment; if the place of business is closed on the due date; if no person is there to pay; if the party has promised to pay notwithstanding non-presentment; or if, after diligent search, the drawee cannot be found. S.75A: delay is excused if caused by circumstances beyond the holder's control.

🧠 Memory trick

Excused if: prevented · shop shut · nobody there · promised to pay anyway · drawee untraceable.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe shop was shut
  1. 📍 What happened

    On the due date, the holder goes to the acceptor's shop and finds it permanently closed, with no one there.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.76: presentment is excused if the place of business is closed on the due date, no one is there to pay, or the drawee cannot be found after diligent search.

  3. ✅ Result

    Presentment is excused; he can proceed against the parties.

4

Noting and protest

S.99S.100S.104S.119

On dishonour, the holder may get a notary public to record the fact of dishonour on the instrument (noting, within a reasonable time); the notary's formal certificate of dishonour is the protest. Protest is required for foreign bills where the law of the place of drawing requires it (S.104) and is useful evidence for inland ones (S.119 presumption).

📖 Word origin

Noting = the notary's note of dishonour on the paper; protest = his formal certificate.

🏘️Real-life exampleGetting the dishonour certified
  1. 📍 What happened

    An exporter's foreign bill is dishonoured, and the law of the country where it was drawn requires protest.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.99–100: a notary public records the dishonour (noting) and issues a formal certificate (protest); required for foreign bills where that law requires it (S.104).

  3. ✅ Result

    He gets it noted and protested by a notary.

5

Acceptance for honour

S.108

When a bill has been noted or protested for non-acceptance, any person not already liable on it may, with the holder's consent, accept it for the honour of any party liable (the drawer or an indorser); he becomes liable to all parties after the party for whose honour he accepted. Payment for honour gives him the holder's rights against that party and prior parties.

🎨 Picture it

A friend steps in to save a party's honour: he accepts or pays the dishonoured bill.

🏘️Real-life exampleA friend steps in
  1. 📍 What happened

    A bill drawn by a well-known exporter is dishonoured by the drawee. A business friend of the exporter offers to accept it to save his name.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.108–116: after noting or protest, a person not liable may, with the holder's consent, accept for honour of a party; he becomes liable to all parties after that party.

  3. ✅ Result

    The friend accepts for the exporter's honour.

6

Bills in sets

S.132S.133

Foreign bills are often drawn in a set of two or three parts, each sent separately, so that one at least arrives. All parts together make one bill; payment of one discharges all. The holder of the part first acquired is entitled to the others and to payment (S.133).

🎨 Picture it

Three copies in the post so that one at least arrives; pay one, and all are discharged.

🏘️Real-life exampleThree copies of one bill
  1. 📍 What happened

    An exporter sends a foreign bill in a set of three by three different couriers.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.132–133: bills in sets: all parts make one bill; payment of one discharges all.

  3. ✅ Result

    Only one payment is made; the other copies become useless.

🔁 Recall: Imagine a courier carrying a bill: he knocks (acceptance), comes back to collect (payment), is excused if the shop is shut, calls the notary if refused (noting/protest), a friend steps in for honour, and he carries three copies in case one is lost (sets).

8Golden rules

  • ★ 1Unconditional is the soul of a note or bill. "When B delivers the goods" or "after my marriage" kills it (S.4, S.5).
  • ★ 2A holder in due course takes free from the defects of earlier holders: he must give value, take it before maturity, and in good faith (S.9).
  • ★ 3A minor can make or indorse an instrument and bind everyone except himself (S.26). Mohori Bibee
  • ★ 4A signed blank or incomplete stamped paper is an inchoate instrument; the holder may complete it up to the stamp amount (S.20). Bir Singh
  • ★ 5Every instrument is presumed to be for consideration, and every holder a holder in due course (S.118).

9Part C: problem routes

Every problem takes the same four stops: issue → law → apply → conclusion.

1. (a) "I promise to pay B ₹500 when B delivers the goods", signed by A. (b) A owes B ₹1,000 and writes a note acknowledging the debt. Is either a promissory note?

  1. 🎯 IssueDo these writings satisfy S.4 NI Act?
  2. 📜 LawS.4: unconditional undertaking, signed, to pay a certain sum of money to a certain person. Illustrations: "I acknowledge myself indebted to B in ₹1,000, to be paid on demand, for value received" is a note; "Mr B, I.O.U. ₹1,000" is not; a promise on an uncertain event (after my marriage) is not.
  3. 🔍 Apply(a) Payment depends on delivery of goods, an event that may or may not happen: the undertaking is conditional. (b) A bare acknowledgment ("I owe B ₹1,000") has no promise to pay; if the writing adds "to be paid on demand" or "I promise to pay", it becomes a note.
  4. ✅ Conclusion(a) Not a promissory note; it is an ordinary contract to pay on delivery. (b) A mere acknowledgment (I.O.U.) is not a note, but it is evidence of the debt and an acknowledgment under S.18 Limitation Act; if it contains a promise to pay, it is a note.

2. Umang lent money to Jayant in 2015. Later Jayant wrote a promissory note acknowledging the (time-barred) debt and promising to pay within a year. Umang indorsed it to Pratap for value. Jayant refuses: "no liability existed when I made the note". Decide.

  1. 🎯 IssueIs a promissory note given for a time-barred debt valid, and can the indorsee sue?
  2. 📜 LawContract Act S.25(3): a written, signed promise to pay a time-barred debt is valid without fresh consideration. NI Act S.118(a): presumption of consideration; S.9: holder in due course; S.36: prior parties liable to HDC.
  3. 🔍 ApplyBy the time the note was written, the 2015 loan was time-barred (3 years). But S.25(3) makes Jayant's written promise enforceable. Pratap took the note for value before maturity and in good faith, so he is a holder in due course.
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe note is valid; Jayant's defence fails. Pratap can sue Jayant (maker) and, if needed, Umang (indorser).

3. A minor borrows ₹50,000 on a promissory note. On attaining majority he executes a fresh note "in consideration of" the earlier one. Is a suit maintainable on the fresh note?

  1. 🎯 IssueCan a note made on majority to pay a loan taken during minority be enforced?
  2. 📜 LawContract Act S.11: a minor's agreement is void Mohori Bibee. NI Act S.26: a minor cannot bind himself. Consideration for the new promise must be real; a void earlier note is no consideration; ratification of a void agreement is not possible.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe first note was void from the start. The new note rests only on that void note (no new money), so it has no lawful consideration.
  4. ✅ ConclusionNo. The fresh note is void for want of consideration and the suit is not maintainable. (If fresh money had been lent on majority, the new note would be valid to that extent.)

4. The Managing Director of a company borrows ₹1 lakh from A on a promissory note, without indicating that he signs for the company. Is the company liable?

  1. 🎯 IssueWho is liable when an agent signs without showing he signs as agent?
  2. 📜 LawS.27: an agent can bind his principal only if he signs in that capacity. S.28: an agent who signs without indicating that he signs as agent, and that he does not intend to incur personal liability, is personally liable, except to those who induced him to sign believing only the principal would be liable.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe note shows no words like "for and on behalf of the company". On the face of the instrument, the MD alone is the maker.
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe MD is personally liable on the note; the company is not liable on the instrument. A may still sue the company on the loan itself if he proves the money was borrowed for and used by the company.

5. X executed a promissory note in favour of Z and then died. Can Z claim against X's legal heirs?

  1. 🎯 IssueDoes liability on a note survive the maker's death?
  2. 📜 LawThe maker's liability on the note passes to his estate. S.29 NI Act: a legal representative who himself signs an instrument is personally liable unless he limits it to the assets received. CPC S.50, S.52: a decree against legal representatives is executed only against the deceased's property in their hands.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe debt is a liability of X's estate. Z can sue the heirs as legal representatives, but they are not personally liable beyond what they inherited.
  4. ✅ ConclusionYes. Z can recover from X's legal heirs, limited to the assets of X that came into their hands.

6. A note dated 31.07.2018 is payable "one month after date". (Variant: a note dated 1.2.2018 payable two months after date.) When is it payable?

  1. 🎯 IssueHow is maturity computed?
  2. 📜 LawS.23: payable so many months after date, it falls due on the corresponding day of the month, or the last day if none. S.22: three days of grace. S.25: if the day is a public holiday, the preceding business day.
  3. 🔍 Apply31.07.2018 + one month = 31.08.2018 (August has 31 days) + 3 days of grace = 3 September 2018. Variant: 1.2.2018 + two months = 1.4.2018 + 3 days = 4 April 2018.
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe first note matures on 3 September 2018; the second on 4 April 2018. If that day is a public holiday under S.25, it is payable on the preceding business day.

7. A draws a bill of exchange in favour of B. Two days later the bill is missing. What is a bill, why does it matter to B, and what can B do?

  1. 🎯 IssueWhat are the rights of a holder whose bill is lost before maturity?
  2. 📜 LawS.5: bill of exchange. S.45A: a holder who loses a bill before it is overdue may ask the drawer for a duplicate of the same tenor, giving security (indemnity) if required. S.58: a finder gets no title.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe bill is B's right to receive money from the drawee; a finder cannot recover on it if it is an order bill. B should at once inform the drawee/acceptor not to pay, ask A for a duplicate under S.45A, and offer an indemnity.
  4. ✅ ConclusionB can obtain a duplicate bill from A under S.45A on giving security, and warn the drawee not to pay anyone else; if A refuses, B can sue on the original debt.
