💳Paying Banker and Collecting Banker

Every cheque has two banks: one pays, one collects. Do it honestly and carefully and the law protects you, but never against a forged drawer's signature.

🧠Mind map

1The big picture

Every cheque has two banks in its life. The paying banker is the drawer's bank, which pays out of the drawer's account. The collecting banker is the payee's bank, which collects the money and credits it to its customer. Both handle lakhs of cheques a day, and neither can check whether every signature on the back is genuine.

So the law makes a deal with them: pay or collect honestly and carefully, and you are protected even if a thief was somewhere in the chain. But a bank that is careless, or pays a cheque whose drawer's own signature is forged, must bear the loss itself.

2Paying banker: duty to pay

Exam question: Paying banker: duty to honour cheques, when the bank must or may refuse, and wrongful dishonour

1

Duty to honour (S.31)

S.31

The drawee of a cheque having sufficient funds of the drawer, properly applicable to its payment, must pay the cheque when duly required, and in default must compensate the drawer for any loss or damage. The duty is owed to the drawer (customer), not to the payee.

🧠 Memory trick

Pay or compensate (S.31).

🗣️ Jargon buster

The duty is owed to the drawer (the customer), not to the payee.

2

Conditions

(1) Sufficient, available funds (or an agreed overdraft); (2) cheque properly drawn and signed as per mandate; (3) presented at the branch where the account is kept (or through clearing), in business hours; (4) presented within three months of its date (otherwise stale); (5) no legal bar.

🧠 Memory trick

Funds · Form · Branch · Time: enough money, properly drawn and signed, at the right branch or clearing, within 3 months of its date.

3

Mandate ends

The bank must stop payment on: (1) the drawer's countermand (stop-payment order); (2) notice of the customer's death, insanity or insolvency; (3) a garnishee order or other attachment (court, income-tax); (4) notice of a defect in the holder's title (lost or stolen cheque); (5) winding-up of a company customer; (6) closure of the account.

🧠 Memory trick

The mandate ends on Stop · Death · Court · Defect: a stop order, notice of death, insanity or insolvency, a garnishee or attachment order, notice of a defect in title.

🎬 Case link

Curtice: a stop order works only when it actually reaches the bank.

4

Must refuse

S.18

Post-dated cheque before its date; stale cheque; material alteration not authenticated by the drawer; signature not matching; amount in words and figures differ (S.18: words prevail, but banks return such cheques); mutilated cheque; a crossed cheque presented at the counter for cash; insufficient funds (the bank cannot pay part).

🌍 Real life

Words say ₹10,000, figures say ₹1,000: in law the words prevail (S.18), but banks return such cheques.

🧠 Memory trick

Post-dated · Stale · Altered · Mismatch · Mutilated: the five usual "return" reasons.

5

Garnishee order

S.60O.XXI r.46

A creditor who has a decree against the customer asks the court to attach the customer's balance in the bank. The court first issues an order nisi (show cause); from service of it, the bank must stop payments out of the attached amount; then an order absolute directs payment to the court or creditor. It attaches the balance at the time of service, not future credits; a joint account is not attached for the debt of one holder alone; money held in trust is not attachable.

📖 Word origin

An order nisi is an order "unless": the bank freezes the money unless cause is shown.

6

Wrongful dishonour

If the bank wrongly returns a cheque, it is liable to the customer for damages to his credit and reputation; he need not prove actual loss (Marzetti v. Williams, 1830), and a trader gets substantial damages for injury to his credit. The customer may also go to the RBI Ombudsman or a consumer commission.

Example: Ravi has ₹1,00,000 and issues a cheque for ₹1,10,000. The bank must return it: it cannot pay ₹1,00,000 in part. But if Ravi had ₹1,20,000 and the bank returned the cheque by mistake, it would pay damages to Ravi for the insult to his credit.
🌍 Real life

Ravi has ₹1,20,000; the bank wrongly bounces his ₹1,10,000 cheque. It pays him damages for the insult to his credit.

🗣️ Jargon buster

No proof of actual loss is needed (Marzetti v. Williams, 1830); a trader gets substantial damages.

🔁 Recall: Imagine the bank cashier with a checklist: money there? signature right? date fine? any stop order, death, court order? If all clear, pay; if he refuses wrongly, he pays damages.

3Protection of the paying banker

Exam question: Statutory protection to the paying banker; payment in due course; forged cheques

🧠 Say it: “With Patience, Our Brave Dog Catches A Fox”

  2. PPatiencePayment in due course
  3. OOurOrder cheques S.85(1)
  4. BBraveBearer cheques S.85(2)
  5. DDogDrafts S.85A
  6. CCatchesCrossed cheques S.128
  7. AAAlteration S.89
  8. FFoxForged drawer signature
1

Why protection

A bank can verify its own customer's signature, but not the signatures of strangers who indorse a cheque. To keep cheques moving, the NI Act protects a bank that pays in due course.

🎨 Picture it

The bank knows its own customer's signature, not the signatures of strangers who indorse the cheque.

2

Payment in due course

S.10

Payment (1) according to the apparent tenor, (2) in good faith and without negligence, (3) to the person in possession, (4) where there is no reasonable ground to believe he is not entitled.

🧠 Memory trick

Apparent tenor · Good faith, no negligence · Person in possession · No reason to suspect.

3

Order cheques S.85(1)

S.85(1)

Where a cheque payable to order purports to be indorsed by or on behalf of the payee, the drawee is discharged by payment in due course. The bank need not prove the indorsement was genuine; it must only be regular on its face.

🧠 Memory trick

"Purports" to be indorsed: the bank need only see that it is regular on its face.

4

Bearer cheques S.85(2)

S.85(2)

A cheque originally payable to bearer can be paid to the bearer in due course notwithstanding any indorsement on it ("once a bearer, always a bearer").

🧠 Memory trick

Once a bearer, always a bearer.

5

Drafts S.85A

S.85A

A draft drawn by one office of a bank on another office, payable to order, gets the same protection when the indorsement purports to be by the payee.

🗣️ Jargon buster

A bank's own draft, drawn by one office on another, gets the same protection.

6

Crossed cheques S.128

S.128

A banker who pays a crossed cheque in due course to a banker (generally) or to the named banker (specially) is discharged, and the drawer is in the same position as if the true owner had been paid.

🎨 Picture it

A crossed cheque travels only through a bank; pay it to a banker in due course and you are discharged.

7

Alteration S.89

S.89

If a cheque has been materially altered but does not appear to be, payment according to its apparent tenor at the time is a valid discharge.

🧠 Memory trick

If the alteration cannot be seen, paying the cheque as it then appears is a valid discharge.

8

Forged drawer signature

A cheque with a forged drawer's signature is not the customer's order at all; the bank pays out of its own money. The customer is not estopped merely because he did not check his statements Canara Bank v. Canara Sales; he is liable only if he knew and kept quiet, or was negligent in drawing the cheque Macmillan and Arthur.

🎬 Case link

Canara Bank v. Canara Sales: a forged drawer's signature is no mandate; the bank bears the loss.

🎬 Case link

Macmillan and Arthur: but a customer who leaves gaps in a cheque bears the loss of the fraud.

🔁 Recall: Protection = a shield with five studs: S.85(1) order, S.85(2) bearer, S.85A drafts, S.89 alteration, S.128 crossed. The shield works only when you pay "in due course" and never against a forged drawer's signature.

4Collecting banker

Exam question: Collecting banker: meaning, duties and statutory protection (S.131)

🧠 Say it: “My Aunt Drinks Coffee, So Coffee Not”

  1. MMyMeaning
  2. AAuntAgent or holder for value
  3. DDrinksDuties
  4. CCoffeeConversion risk
  5. SSoS.131 protection
  6. CCoffeeConditions
  7. NNotNegligence
1

Meaning

A collecting banker is the bank that collects a cheque on behalf of its customer from the paying banker and credits the proceeds to the customer's account.

🎨 Picture it

The collecting banker is a postman for cheques.

2

Agent or holder for value

Usually it acts as the customer's agent. It becomes a holder for value if it pays the customer before collection (lets him draw against the uncleared cheque) or has a lien on it; it can then sue the parties in its own name.

🧠 Memory trick

Usually an agent; a holder for value if it lets the customer draw before collection, or has a lien.

3

Duties

Present the cheque promptly (delay making it liable for loss); give the customer prompt notice of dishonour; credit proceeds at once; follow the crossing (an "account payee" cheque only to the payee's account); follow KYC rules when opening accounts.

🧠 Memory trick

Present promptly · notify dishonour · credit at once · obey the crossing · KYC.

4

Conversion risk

If the customer had no title (stolen cheque, forged indorsement), the true owner can sue the collecting banker for conversion, since it handled his property.

📖 Word origin

Conversion = dealing with someone else's property as your own; the true owner can sue the bank.

5

S.131 protection

S.131S.131A

A banker who has, in good faith and without negligence, received payment for a customer of a cheque crossed generally or specially to itself incurs no liability to the true owner merely because the customer's title was defective. Crediting the account before receiving payment does not take away this protection (Explanation I); it covers electronic images of truncated cheques too. S.131A extends it to drafts.

🧠 Memory trick

Good faith + no negligence + for a customer + crossed cheque = no liability to the true owner.

6

Conditions

(1) Crossed cheque; (2) collected for a customer (a person with an account; duration does not matter); (3) as agent; (4) good faith; (5) without negligence. The bank must prove the absence of negligence.

🗣️ Jargon buster

Even a brand-new account holder is a "customer"; the duration of the account does not matter. The bank must prove it was not negligent.

7

Negligence

Opening an account without proper introduction or KYC; collecting an employer's or company's cheque into an employee's or director's personal account without enquiry Lloyds Bank v. Savory; collecting an account payee cheque for someone else; ignoring an irregular indorsement; amounts out of line with the customer's known means.

🎬 Case link

Lloyds Bank v. Savory: the bank never asked who its customers' employers were, so it lost the protection.

🌍 Real life

A company's cheque deposited into a director's or employee's personal account without enquiry: a red flag.

🔁 Recall: The collecting banker is a postman for cheques: he is safe if he delivers crossed letters, only for his own customers, honestly and carefully. If he ignores an obviously wrong address, he pays.

6Case stories

Canara Bank v. Canara Sales Corporation

(1987) 2 SCC 666 · AIR 1987 SC 1603

📖 What happenedA firm's accountant forged the managing partner's signature on many cheques over years; the bank paid them.
❓ The questionIs a bank liable for paying cheques bearing a forged drawer's signature, if the customer did not check its statements?
⚖️ HeldA forged cheque is no mandate; the bank must bear the loss. The customer's failure to check its passbook is not enough to stop it from suing.

London Joint Stock Bank Ltd v. Macmillan and Arthur

[1918] AC 777 · House of Lords

📖 What happenedA clerk got a cheque signed for £2 with blank spaces, then altered it to £120 and cashed it.
❓ The questionWho bears the loss when a cheque is fraudulently altered because the drawer left blank spaces?
⚖️ HeldThe customer owes the bank a duty to draw cheques so as not to invite fraud; having left the gaps, the firm bore the loss.

Curtice v. London City and Midland Bank Ltd

[1908] 1 KB 293 · Court of Appeal

📖 What happenedA customer stopped a cheque by telegram, but it was delivered after hours and lay unread; the bank paid the cheque next day.
❓ The questionIs a stop-payment order effective once it is delivered, even if the bank has not actually seen it?
⚖️ HeldA countermand takes effect only when it actually comes to the bank's attention; there is no constructive countermand.

Lloyds Bank Ltd v. E.B. Savory and Co.

[1933] AC 201 · House of Lords

📖 What happenedTwo clerks of a stockbroking firm stole its cheques and paid them into their own (and a wife's) accounts at the bank.
❓ The questionWas the collecting bank negligent, and so unprotected?
⚖️ HeldThe bank was negligent: it had not found out who the customers' employers were, so it lost the statutory protection.

7Golden rules

  • ★ 1The paying banker must honour a properly drawn cheque when the drawer has sufficient funds; otherwise it must compensate the drawer (S.31).
  • ★ 2A forged drawer's signature is no mandate at all: the bank bears the loss, even if the customer did not check his statements. Canara Bank v. Canara Sales
  • ★ 3A customer must draw cheques carefully; if he leaves room for alteration, he bears the loss. Macmillan and Arthur
  • ★ 4A stop-payment order works only when it actually reaches the bank. Curtice
  • ★ 5A collecting banker who ignores warning signs (employer's cheques into an employee's account) loses S.131 protection. Lloyds Bank v. Savory

8Part C: problem routes

Every problem takes the same four stops: issue → law → apply → conclusion.

1. A cheque payable to "R or order" is stolen; the thief forges R's indorsement and the bank pays it in due course. Can R recover from the bank?

  1. 🎯 IssueIs the paying banker liable to the true owner when it pays on a forged indorsement?
  2. 📜 LawS.85(1): an order cheque that purports to be indorsed by the payee, paid in due course, discharges the banker. S.10: payment in due course. S.58: the thief gets no title.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe indorsement purported to be R's and nothing looked suspicious. The bank paid in good faith and without negligence. A bank is not expected to know the signature of a payee who is not its customer.
  4. ✅ ConclusionR cannot recover from the paying bank. He can sue the thief, and the person who received the money (and, if the cheque was collected through a careless collecting bank, that bank for conversion).

2. Uday signs a bearer cheque for ₹20,000 and gives it to his office boy to deposit. The boy adds a zero and encashes ₹2,00,000. Is the bank liable?

  1. 🎯 IssueWho bears the loss of a fraudulent alteration of the amount?
  2. 📜 LawS.87: a material alteration voids the instrument. S.89: payment of an altered cheque whose alteration is not apparent, according to its apparent tenor, is protected. S.18: amount in words prevails. Customer's duty to draw carefully Macmillan and Arthur.
  3. 🔍 ApplyIf the words still read "twenty thousand", the alteration was apparent: paying ₹2 lakh was not according to the apparent tenor and was negligent, so the bank bears the loss. If Uday left gaps that let the boy alter both words and figures cleanly, he breached his duty of care and must bear the loss.
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe bank is liable if the alteration was visible or the words did not match; Uday bears the loss if his careless drawing made a clean alteration possible. Either way, the office boy is liable for forgery and cheating (BNS 318, 336).

3. Banker A pays customer B's cheque before B's stop-payment order reaches the bank. Is the banker liable?

  1. 🎯 IssueWhen does a countermand take effect?
  2. 📜 LawS.31 and the end of the mandate: a countermand must be clear, from the drawer, and must actually reach the branch where the account is kept. There is no "constructive" countermand Curtice.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe cheque was paid before the order reached the bank. At that time the bank still had B's mandate.
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe banker is not liable; the payment is valid and can be debited to B.

4. The bank made false (excess) credit entries in A's passbook. Relying on them, A issued a post-dated cheque, which the bank dishonoured. Position of the banker?

  1. 🎯 IssueIs the bank bound by wrong credit entries in the passbook?
  2. 📜 LawEntries in a passbook are the bank's statements of account. If the customer, in good faith, relies on a wrong credit and changes his position, the bank is estopped from reversing it to his prejudice (Skyring v. Greenwood, 1825); dishonouring a cheque drawn within the apparent balance is wrongful dishonour (Holland v. Manchester and Liverpool District Banking Co., 1909). A customer who knew of the error cannot take advantage of it.
  3. 🔍 ApplyA acted in good faith on the bank's own entries and issued a cheque within the balance shown.
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe bank can correct its error for the future, but it is liable for wrongful dishonour of A's cheque and cannot recover sums A spent in good faith relying on the entries.

5. A customer has ₹1,00,000 in his account and issues a cheque for ₹1,10,000 to Z. The bank refuses to pay. Advise Z.

  1. 🎯 IssueCan Z sue the bank, and what are his remedies?
  2. 📜 LawS.31: the duty to pay is owed to the drawer only when funds are sufficient; a bank cannot pay part. S.30: the drawer is liable to the holder on dishonour. S.138: dishonour for insufficient funds is an offence.
  3. 🔍 ApplyFunds were insufficient, so the refusal was correct. Z has no claim against the bank (no privity).
  4. ✅ ConclusionZ cannot sue the bank. He should send a demand notice to the drawer within 30 days of the return memo, and if unpaid within 15 days, file a complaint under S.138; he can also sue for the amount.

6. On 1 January 2025, X gives Y a post-dated cheque dated 26 January 2025. Y presents it on 20 April 2025. Explaining the essentials of a cheque, decide whether it is valid.

  1. 🎯 IssueIs a post-dated cheque valid, and is it stale on 20 April?
  2. 📜 LawS.6: a cheque is a bill of exchange drawn on a specified banker and payable only on demand (including electronic forms). Essentials: writing, unconditional order, drawn on a banker, signed, certain sum, certain payee, payable on demand. A post-dated cheque is a bill until its date and a cheque from that date Anil Kumar Sawhney. RBI: a cheque is valid for three months from its date.
  3. 🔍 ApplyIts date is 26 January 2025, so three months run from that date, not from 1 January. 20 April 2025 is within three months.
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe cheque is valid and must be paid if funds are sufficient; if dishonoured for want of funds, Y can proceed under S.138.

7. Z deposits a demand draft issued by YZ Bank into his account with AB Bank. AB credits him; YZ pays AB; Z withdraws. YZ later says the draft was issued to a different institution and blames AB for not verifying its customer. Decide the liability of each bank.

  1. 🎯 IssueWho bears the loss when a draft is collected for a person who was not its payee?
  2. 📜 LawS.85A: the issuing (paying) bank is protected if it paid a draft in due course on an indorsement that purports to be by the payee. S.131, S.131A: the collecting bank is protected only if it acted in good faith and without negligence for a customer. Negligence includes crediting an instrument payable to an institution into an individual's account without enquiry Lloyds Bank v. Savory.
  3. 🔍 ApplyYZ paid AB in due course, so YZ is protected. AB credited a draft payable to an institution into Z's personal account without asking why: that is negligence, so AB loses S.131A protection and is liable to the true owner for conversion.
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe loss falls on AB Bank, not YZ Bank. AB can recover from Z, who is also liable for cheating and forgery.
