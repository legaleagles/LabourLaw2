1The big picture

When a 15-year-old breaks the law, the question is not "how do we punish him?" but "how do we bring him back?". The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 treats a child as a child: no police lock-up, no jail, no death sentence, no life sentence without release, and a fresh start with no record that follows him.

The Act has two doors. A child in conflict with law goes to the Juvenile Justice Board (JJB). A child in need of care and protection (orphan, street child, abused child) goes to the Child Welfare Committee (CWC). Only for a heinous offence by a 16–18-year-old may the child, after a preliminary assessment, be tried as an adult by the Children's Court.

1

Background

The 2015 Act replaced the JJ Act, 2000. After the 2012 Delhi gang-rape case, in which one accused was a juvenile, Parliament allowed children of 16–18 accused of heinous offences to be tried as adults in some cases. It also consolidated the law on adoption. It was amended in 2021 (adoption orders now by the District Magistrate; wider "serious offences").

💬 In simple words
  • Juvenile: a person under 18 accused of an offence.
🔢 Number trick

2000 → 2015 → 2021: the old Act, the new Act, then the amendment (adoption orders by the District Magistrate; wider "serious offences").

🧠 Memory trick

The big change of 2015 in one line: children of 16–18 accused of heinous offences may, in some cases, be tried as adults.

2

Key definitions

S.2

Child: below 18. Child in conflict with law: alleged or found to have committed an offence while under 18. Child in need of care and protection: homeless, begging, abused, orphaned, at risk of child marriage, etc. Offences: petty (maximum up to 3 years), serious (3 to 7 years; since 2021 also offences with a maximum above 7 years but no minimum or a minimum below 7), heinous (minimum 7 years or more).

🎨 Picture it

Two kinds of children: one who did wrong (child in conflict with law) and one who was wronged (child in need of care and protection).

🧠 Memory trick

Petty · Serious · Heinous = 3 · 3–7 · 7+. Heinous is about the minimum punishment of 7 years, not the maximum.

🏘️Real-life exampleTwo children, two paths
  1. 📍 What happened

    Police find a 14-year-old begging at a traffic signal. The same week, a 15-year-old is caught stealing a bicycle.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.2: a child in need of care and protection (e.g. begging, homeless, abused) and a child in conflict with law (alleged to have committed an offence while under 18).

  3. ✅ Result

    The beggar child goes to the Child Welfare Committee; the bicycle thief to the Juvenile Justice Board.

3

Principles

S.3

Sixteen principles, including: presumption of innocence, dignity, participation, best interest of the child, family responsibility, safety, non-stigmatising semantics, privacy, institutionalisation as a last resort, repatriation, fresh start, diversion and natural justice.

🧠 Memory trick

Six to quote first: "In Dignity, Best Families Stay Fresh" → presumption of Innocence, Dignity, Best interest, Family responsibility, Safety, Fresh start.

🗣️ Jargon buster

Non-stigmatising semantics: no accusing labels for children. That is why the law speaks of a "child in conflict with law".

🏘️Real-life exampleNo "juvenile criminal" in the news
  1. 📍 What happened

    A local paper wants to print "Juvenile thief caught" with the boy's photo and school name.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.3 principles include dignity, privacy, non-stigmatising semantics and a fresh start; S.74 bars disclosing the child's identity.

  3. ✅ Result

    The paper cannot publish his photo, name or school.

4

Two bodies: JJB and CWC

S.4S.27

The Juvenile Justice Board (S.4) deals with children in conflict with law; the Child Welfare Committee (S.27) deals with children in need of care and protection. Both must sit in child-friendly premises, not in a regular courtroom.

🎨 Picture it

One building, two doors: the JJB for the child who did wrong, the CWC for the child who was wronged. Neither sits in an ordinary courtroom.

🔢 Number trick

S.4 sets up the Board; S.27 sets up the Committee.

🏘️Real-life exampleNot in a courtroom
  1. 📍 What happened

    A 15-year-old is brought for inquiry. His family expects a dock and a judge in robes.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    The JJB deals with children in conflict with law and the CWC with children in need of care; both sit in child-friendly premises.

  3. ✅ Result

    The inquiry happens in a child-friendly room, not an ordinary courtroom.

5

Treatment of 16–18

S.15S.18(3)S.19S.21

The Board makes a preliminary assessment of the child's mental and physical capacity, ability to understand consequences, and the circumstances (S.15). If it finds trial as an adult necessary, it transfers the case to the Children's Court (S.18(3), S.19). Even then: no death sentence or life without release (S.21), and he stays in a place of safety till 21.

Case story Shilpa Mittal: a 17-year-old driving his father's Mercedes at high speed killed a young man in Delhi. The offence charged had a maximum of 10 years but no minimum. The Supreme Court held it was not "heinous" (which needs a minimum of 7 years), so he could not be tried as an adult; Parliament then amended the Act in 2021 to call such offences "serious".
🎨 Picture it

A locked side-room: only for 16–18 + heinous, and only after the Board's preliminary assessment (S.15) sends him to the Children's Court.

🎬 Case link

Shilpa Mittal: an offence with a maximum of 10 years and no minimum is not heinous, so no adult trial.

🧠 Memory trick

Even in the side-room: no death, no life without release (S.21), and a place of safety till 21.

🏘️Real-life exampleA 17-year-old and a heinous offence
  1. 📍 What happened

    A 17-year-old is accused of a heinous offence (minimum 7 years or more).

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.15: the Board makes a preliminary assessment of his mental and physical capacity and understanding; if needed it transfers him to the Children's Court to be tried as an adult. Even then, no death sentence or life without release.

  3. ✅ Result

    He may be tried as an adult, but can never get death or life without release.

6

Child-friendly safeguards

S.10S.12S.23S.24S.74

S.10: no lock-up or jail; S.12: bail as a rule; S.23: no joint trial with adults; S.24: no disqualification from the finding; S.74: identity must not be disclosed in the media; S.75: cruelty to a child is an offence. Courts decide age (S.94), hear appeals (S.101), and supervise Boards; a juvenility claim can be raised before any court, at any stage, even after the case ends (S.9(2)).

💬 In simple words
  • Bail: release from custody on a promise to come back to court.
🔢 Number trick

The safeguard numbers: 10 no lock-up · 12 bail · 23 no joint trial · 24 no disqualification · 74 no identity in the media · 75 cruelty is an offence.

🌍 Real life

A newspaper cannot print a child's name, school or photo in a case report (S.74).

🗣️ Jargon buster

A claim of juvenility can be raised before any court, at any stage, even after the case ends (S.9(2)).

🏘️Real-life exampleNo lock-up for a child
  1. 📍 What happened

    Police arrest a 16-year-old for a petty theft and want to keep him in the station lock-up overnight.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.10: no lock-up or jail for a child. S.12: bail as a rule. S.23: no joint trial with adults.

  3. ✅ Result

    He must be placed with a child welfare officer and produced before the Board, and bail is the normal course.

🔁 Recall: Picture two doors in one building: JJB for the child who did wrong, CWC for the child who was wronged; and one locked side-room (Children's Court) only for 16–18 + heinous.

Composition of JJB

S.4

A Metropolitan Magistrate or Judicial Magistrate of the first class (not the Chief Magistrate) with at least 3 years' experience, as the Principal Magistrate, and two social workers, at least one a woman, with 7 years' work in child welfare or a professional degree in child psychology, psychiatry, sociology or law.

🧠 Memory trick

1 + 2: one Magistrate (with 3 years' experience) + two social workers, at least one a woman.

🎨 Picture it

A three-member panel at a table, not a judge on a high bench: the Board sits in a child-friendly room.

🏘️Real-life exampleWho sits on the Board?
  1. 📍 What happened

    A district sets up its Juvenile Justice Board.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.4: a Judicial Magistrate first class with 3 years' experience as Principal Magistrate, plus two social workers, at least one a woman.

  3. ✅ Result

    Three members: one judicial officer and two child-welfare experts, one of them a woman.

2

Powers

S.8S.9(1)

The Board has the powers of a Magistrate under the BNSS, and exclusive power to deal with children in conflict with law in its area. A Magistrate who is not a Board must forward the child to the Board (S.9(1)).

🎨 Picture it

Exclusive territory: in its area, only the Board deals with children in conflict with law. Any other Magistrate must forward the child to it (S.9(1)).

🏘️Real-life exampleBrought to the wrong Magistrate
  1. 📍 What happened

    Police produce a 15-year-old before an ordinary Magistrate who is not a member of the Board.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.8: the Board has exclusive power over children in conflict with law. S.9(1): any other Magistrate must forward the child to the Board.

  3. ✅ Result

    The Magistrate records his opinion and sends the child to the Board.

3

Functions

S.8(3)S.12S.14S.18

Ensure the informed participation of the child and parents; respect the child's rights; provide legal aid; provide an interpreter; direct the Probation Officer to prepare a social investigation report within 15 days; decide bail (S.12); conduct the inquiry (S.14) and pass orders (S.18); inspect observation homes and special homes regularly.

💬 In simple words
  • Probation: release of a convicted person without jail, on good behaviour under supervision.
🔢 Number trick

15 days: the Probation Officer's social investigation report.

🧠 Memory trick

The Board's job list, in order: participation → rights → legal aid → interpreter → report → bail → inquiry → orders → inspect homes.

🏘️Real-life exampleUnderstanding the child's life
  1. 📍 What happened

    Before deciding what to do with a 14-year-old caught for a theft, the Board wants to know about his family, school and friends.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.8(3): the Board directs the Probation Officer to prepare a social investigation report within 15 days, and ensures legal aid and an interpreter.

  3. ✅ Result

    The report helps the Board pick an order that fits the child, not just the offence.

4

CWC

S.27S.31

A Chairperson and four members, at least one a woman and one an expert on children. It is the final authority on care, protection, treatment, development and rehabilitation of children in need; it can place a child in a children's home, with a fit person, or in foster care, and declare a child legally free for adoption. A child must be produced before it within 24 hours (S.31).

🧠 Memory trick

JJB = 1 judge + 2 helpers; CWC = 1 head + 4 helpers.

🔢 Number trick

24 hours: a child in need of care must be produced before the CWC (S.31).

🎨 Picture it

The CWC is the child's guardian committee: final authority on care, and it can declare a child legally free for adoption.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe abandoned newborn
  1. 📍 What happened

    A newborn baby is found abandoned near a hospital.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.27: the Child Welfare Committee is the final authority on care and protection; it can place a child in a home or foster care, and declare a child legally free for adoption.

  3. ✅ Result

    The CWC takes charge, and after the legal process can declare the baby free for adoption.

5

Age determination

S.94S.12S.18S.24

The Board or Committee goes by: (i) the school or matriculation date-of-birth certificate; failing that (ii) a birth certificate from a corporation, municipality or panchayat; only in their absence (iii) an ossification or other medical test, completed within 15 days.

Example: a 15-year-old is caught for a mobile phone theft. Police produce him before the Board within 24 hours; he is released on bail to his mother (S.12); the Probation Officer reports on his family and school; the Board advises him and orders 2 months' community service (S.18). No criminal record follows him (S.24).
🧠 Memory trick

School first, Birth next, Bones last: school certificate → municipal or panchayat birth certificate → ossification test (within 15 days).

🎬 Case link

Pratap Singh: juvenility is judged by age on the date of the offence.

🏘️Real-life exampleHow old is he really?
  1. 📍 What happened

    A boy says he is 16; the police say 19. His school certificate shows he is 16.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.94: age is decided first by the school or matriculation certificate, then a municipal or panchayat birth certificate, and only then a medical test.

  3. ✅ Result

    The school certificate decides it: he is 16, and the Board deals with him.

🔁 Recall: JJB = "1 judge + 2 helpers (one a woman)"; CWC = "1 head + 4 helpers". One judges the child who erred; the other shelters the child who suffered.

Aim

S.39

Rehabilitation and social reintegration, primarily through family-based care (restoration to the family, adoption, foster care, sponsorship); institutional care is the last resort.

🎨 Picture it

Home first, institution last: restore to the family, adoption, foster care, sponsorship; an institution only as a last resort.

🏘️Real-life exampleBack home first
  1. 📍 What happened

    A 13-year-old who ran away and got into petty crime has loving parents willing to take him back.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.39: rehabilitation primarily through family-based care; institutional care is the last resort.

  3. ✅ Result

2

Orders by JJB (S.18)

S.18

(a) send home after advice or admonition; (b) group counselling; (c) community service; (d) fine, payable by the child or parents (subject to labour laws); (e) release on probation of good conduct for up to 3 years under a parent or fit person; (f) probation under a fit facility; (g) a special home for up to 3 years for reformative services, education and skills.

🎨 Picture it

A dimmer switch, softest to strongest: advise → counsel → community service → fine → probation → special home (max 3 years).

🌍 Real life

A 15-year-old caught for a phone theft: bail to his mother, a report on his family and school, advice and 2 months' community service. No record follows him.

🏘️Real-life exampleAdmonition, counselling or a special home
  1. 📍 What happened

    The Board finds that a 15-year-old committed a petty theft for the first time.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.18: orders range from advice or admonition, counselling, community service, probation up to 3 years, to a special home up to 3 years.

  3. ✅ Result

    For a first petty offence, the Board will usually admonish and counsel him and send him home.

3

Institutional care

S.47S.48S.49S.50

Observation homes (S.47) for children during the inquiry; special homes (S.48) for children found to have committed an offence; a place of safety (S.49) for those aged 16–18 accused or found guilty of heinous offences; children's homes (S.50) and specialised adoption agencies for children in need of care.

🧠 Memory trick

"Only Some People Care" → Observation home (S.47) · Special home (S.48) · Place of safety (S.49) · Children's home (S.50).

🏘️Real-life exampleWhere does the child stay?
  1. 📍 What happened

    One child is awaiting inquiry; another has been found to have committed an offence; a third is an orphan.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Observation home (S.47) during inquiry; special home (S.48) after a finding; children's home (S.50) for children in need of care.

  3. ✅ Result

    Three children, three different homes, each suited to their situation.

4

Non-institutional care

S.56S.44S.45

Adoption (S.56–73; adoption orders now by the District Magistrate), foster care (S.44), sponsorship (S.45: financial support to families for education and health), and after-care.

🧠 Memory trick

A-F-S-A: Adoption · Foster care · Sponsorship · After-care.

🗣️ Jargon buster

Sponsorship = financial support to a family for the child's education and health (S.45).

🏘️Real-life exampleA family that cannot pay school fees
  1. 📍 What happened

    A widow can care for her son but cannot afford his schooling, so he is at risk of being sent to an institution.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Non-institutional care includes sponsorship (S.45): financial support to families for education and health, along with foster care and adoption.

  3. ✅ Result

    Sponsorship lets the boy stay at home and keep studying.

5

After-care

S.46

A child leaving an institution at 18 may be given financial support and help up to 21 to rejoin society: education, skills, a place to stay.

Case story Pratap Singh: an accused in a murder case said he was below 18 on the date of the offence, but above that age when produced in court. A Constitution Bench held that age on the date of the offence decides whether he is a juvenile; so he had to be dealt with under the juvenile law.
🔢 Number trick

18 → 21: help with education, skills and a place to stay until 21, after leaving an institution at 18.

🏘️Real-life exampleTurning 18 in a children's home
  1. 📍 What happened

    A girl who grew up in a children's home turns 18 with no family to return to.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.46: after-care up to 21: financial support, education, skills and a place to stay.

  3. ✅ Result

    She gets support till 21 to finish a course and start working.

🔁 Recall: Think of a ladder down from home: family (restoration, foster, adoption) → community (counselling, service, probation) → institution (special home, max 3 years) → after-care till 21.

7Golden rules

  • ★ 1Age on the date of the offence decides juvenility, not the age when caught or tried. Pratap Singh
  • ★ 2Under 7: no offence at all (BNS 20). 7 to 12: no offence if the child lacked maturity to understand (BNS 21).
  • ★ 3A child is never sentenced to death or to life imprisonment without the possibility of release (S.21).
  • ★ 4Only a heinous offence (minimum 7 years) by a child of 16–18 can lead to trial as an adult, after preliminary assessment. Shilpa Mittal
  • ★ 5No joint trial of a child with an adult (S.23).

8Part C: problem routes

Every problem takes the same four stops: issue → law → apply → conclusion.

1. A boy of 17 commits a serious offence; he is caught at 19 (or 21) and tried and convicted by an ordinary criminal court. Is it legal? What is the correct procedure?

  1. 🎯 IssueIs a person who was a child on the date of the offence entitled to be dealt with under the JJ Act though now an adult?
  2. 📜 LawS.2(13): a child in conflict with law is one under 18 on the date of the offence Pratap Singh. S.6: a person apprehended after 18 for an offence committed under 18 is dealt with as a child. S.9: any court must forward such a person to the JJB; the claim can be raised at any stage. S.94: age determination.
  3. 🔍 ApplyHe was 17 on the date of the offence, so the ordinary criminal court had no power to try him. The Board must inquire; if the offence is serious, the JJB orders under S.18; if heinous and he was 16–18, preliminary assessment (S.15) decides whether the Children's Court may try him.
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe conviction by the ordinary court is illegal; the case must go to the JJB, which deals with him under the JJ Act (special home or other orders; no death or life without release).

2. A boy aged 16 years 6 months is involved in kidnapping and murder of a woman. Is he punishable? By which court? What punishment?

  1. 🎯 IssueHow is a 16–18-year-old accused of a heinous offence dealt with?
  2. 📜 LawMurder (BNS 103) is heinous (minimum life). S.15: preliminary assessment by the JJB within 3 months. S.18(3), S.19: transfer to the Children's Court, which may try him as an adult. S.21: no death or life imprisonment without possibility of release.
  3. 🔍 ApplyIf the JJB finds he had the mental and physical capacity and understood the consequences, the Children's Court tries him as an adult; else the JJB inquires and may send him to a special home for up to 3 years.
  4. ✅ ConclusionHe may be tried as an adult by the Children's Court after preliminary assessment, but he cannot get death or life without release; he stays in a place of safety till 21.

3. Two boys aged 4 and 5 break open shop locks to steal; on the way back they injure a man who dies a week later. Are they punishable?

  1. 🎯 IssueAre very young children criminally responsible?
  2. 📜 LawBNS 20: nothing is an offence which is done by a child under 7 years. BNS 21: nothing is an offence by a child of 7 to 12 who has not attained sufficient maturity of understanding to judge the nature and consequences of his conduct.
  3. 🔍 ApplyBoth boys are under 7, so whatever they did is not an offence. They are children in need of care and protection, to be produced before the CWC. (A 12-year-old: if he had sufficient maturity, the JJB deals with him for a petty offence.)
  4. ✅ ConclusionNot punishable (BNS 20); they go to the Child Welfare Committee for care and protection, not to any court.

4. X (14) and Z (19) were jointly tried by a Sessions Court for murdering a constable and acquitted. Was the trial proper?

  1. 🎯 IssueCan a child and an adult be tried together?
  2. 📜 LawS.23: no joint proceedings of a child in conflict with law with a person who is not a child. S.8: the JJB has exclusive jurisdiction over the child. Murder by a 14-year-old cannot be tried as an adult (S.15 applies only to 16–18).
  3. 🔍 ApplyX's trial by the Sessions Court was without jurisdiction; Z (adult) could be tried by the Sessions Court separately.
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe joint trial was improper as to X: his case had to be inquired into by the JJB; Z alone could be tried by the Sessions Court.

5. A boy of 13 committed chain-snatching, theft of a laptop and a purse within one year. How are "charges" framed, which forum, what punishment?

  1. 🎯 IssueHow does the law deal with repeated offences by a 13-year-old?
  2. 📜 LawSnatching (BNS 304) and theft (BNS 303(2)) are punishable up to 3 years: petty offences. The JJB conducts an inquiry, not a trial; there is no charge-sheet "conviction" (S.14). Orders under S.18.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe Board holds one inquiry covering all three incidents, calls for a social investigation report, and decides the most suitable order.
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe JJB (not a criminal court) inquires; it may order counselling, community service, probation, or at most a special home for up to 3 years; no "punishment" as for adults.
