🔓Bail

Bail is a promise in exchange for freedom: "bail, not jail".

🧠Mind map

1The big picture

Bail is a promise in exchange for freedom. The accused says, "Let me go home; I will come back whenever the court calls me", and signs a bond (with or without sureties) to back that promise. Krishna Iyer J. put the whole subject in four words: "bail, not jail". A person is presumed innocent until proved guilty, so keeping him in prison before trial must be the exception.

Think of three doors. For a bailable offence the door is open as of right. For a non-bailable offence the door opens at the court's discretion. And anticipatory bail is a key you get before anyone tries to lock you in.

1

Meaning

S.2

For the first time, the BNSS defines bail (S.2): the release of a person accused or suspected of an offence from custody, on conditions imposed by an officer or court, on his executing a bond or bail bond. A bail bond is an undertaking with sureties; a bond is a personal bond without sureties.

📖 Word origin

Bail comes from the Old French baillier, "to hand over". The accused is handed over to the care of his sureties instead of the jail.

🧠 Memory trick

Bail bond = your promise + a friend who guarantees it (surety). Bond = your promise alone. One extra word, one extra person.

2

Bailable v. non-bailable

BNS 115(2)BNS 103BNS 64

A bailable offence is one shown as bailable in the First Schedule or made bailable by another law; every other offence is non-bailable. Bailable offences are generally less serious (e.g. simple hurt, BNS 115(2)); non-bailable ones are serious (e.g. murder, BNS 103; rape, BNS 64).

🌍 Real life

Two neighbours fight and one slaps the other: simple hurt (BNS 115(2)), bailable, so he gets bail as of right, from the police officer or the court. A murder (BNS 103) is non-bailable: only a court can decide.

🎨 Picture it

The First Schedule is the menu card of the BNSS: every offence has a column saying "bailable" or "non-bailable". You don't guess; you read the menu.

3

Bail in bailable offences

S.478

The person shall be released on bail, by the police officer or the court, at any stage. It is a right, not a favour. If he cannot furnish a surety within a week, he is presumed indigent and released on his own bond without sureties.

🎨 Picture it

A pre-paid ticket: in a bailable offence the officer cannot refuse it. It is a right, not a favour.

🔢 Number trick

4-7-8: read the 7 in the middle as 7 days. No surety within a week means he is presumed indigent (too poor) and goes home on his own bond.

📖 Word origin

Indigent is from Latin indigere, "to be in need": a person too poor to arrange a surety.

4

Bail in non-bailable offences

S.480S.483

A Magistrate may release him, but not where there are reasonable grounds to believe he committed an offence punishable with death or life imprisonment, or a cognizable offence with serious previous convictions; even then a child, a woman, or a sick or infirm person may be released. The High Court and Court of Session have wider powers (S.483). Factors: nature and gravity of the charge, evidence, risk of fleeing, tampering with witnesses, antecedents.

Case story Satender Kumar Antil: a man accused of a bribery offence was never arrested during the CBI investigation, but when the charge sheet came, the court refused bail and issued a warrant. The Supreme Court said: "bail is the rule and jail the exception"; filing a charge sheet does not mean arrest; police must follow the notice-before-arrest rule; and it grouped offences into categories so courts decide bail quickly and uniformly.
🎨 Picture it

A traffic signal. Red for the Magistrate: death or life offences, or a cognizable offence with serious previous convictions. But a child, a woman, or a sick or infirm person may still cross. The High Court and Sessions Court hold a bigger key (S.483).

🧠 Memory trick

Five things every judge weighs, "Good Evidence Finds Wise Answers": Gravity of the charge, Evidence, Fleeing risk, Witness tampering, Antecedents.

5

Kinds of bail

S.478S.480S.483S.482S.187(3)

(1) Regular bail after arrest (S.478, S.480, S.483); (2) interim bail while a bail application is pending; (3) anticipatory bail before arrest (S.482); (4) default (statutory) bail when the charge sheet is not filed within 60 or 90 days (S.187(3)); (5) bail pending appeal (S.430); (6) release of undertrials after half (first-time offenders: one-third) of the maximum sentence (S.479).

🧠 Memory trick

The six kinds as a sentence: "Real Indians Always Drink Adrak-chai Usually": Regular, Interim, Anticipatory, Default, Appeal, Undertrial.

🎨 Picture it

Put them on a clock: before arrest (anticipatory) → after arrest (regular, interim) → police too slow (default, 60/90 days) → custody too long (undertrial, S.479) → after conviction (pending appeal, S.430).

6

Bonds

S.484S.486S.491S.494

The amount must be fixed with regard to the circumstances and shall not be excessive (S.484); the bond binds the accused to attend court; sureties declare their solvency (S.486); if the bond is forfeited, the court may recover the penalty (S.491); for a child, a bond by sureties alone may be accepted (S.494).

📖 Word origin

Surety (from Latin securitas, "safety") is the guarantor, like a loan guarantor at a bank. Forfeiture means losing the promised amount if the accused does not turn up.

🎨 Picture it

The bond is the doorman: it lets the accused out, but makes sure he comes back to court.

7

Cancellation

S.480(5)S.483

A court that granted bail may order re-arrest (S.480(5)); the High Court and Sessions Court may cancel bail (S.483). Grounds: misusing liberty, tampering with evidence, threatening witnesses, committing another offence, absconding.

🎨 Picture it

A red card in football: misuse the freedom and you are sent off, back to custody.

🌍 Real life

An accused on bail phones a witness and threatens him. The prosecution applies; the court that granted bail orders re-arrest (S.480(5)), or the High Court or Sessions Court cancels it (S.483). This is Part C problem 2 below.

📖 Word origin

Abscond is from Latin abscondere, "to hide away": running off so the court cannot find you.

🔁 Recall: Picture three doors: open (bailable), guarded (non-bailable), and a key in advance (anticipatory). Then add the bond as the doorman.

3Anticipatory bail

Exam question: Anticipatory bail (S.482): meaning, conditions, principles

🧠 Say it: “My Way: Can Eat Dosa Comfortably”

1

Meaning

A direction that, if the person is arrested, he shall be released on bail. It is granted before arrest to a person who has reason to believe he may be arrested for a non-bailable offence. Purpose: no one should be humiliated or jailed because of a false or motivated accusation.

📖 Word origin

Anticipatory: from Latin anticipare, "to take beforehand". The bail order comes before the arrest.

🎨 Picture it

An umbrella bought before the rain. If the arrest (rain) comes, he is released on bail at once.

🌍 Real life

A person learns that someone has filed a false complaint of a serious, non-bailable offence against him. Instead of waiting to be arrested and humiliated, he applies under S.482 first.

2

Who grants

S.482(1)

The High Court or the Court of Session only (not a Magistrate).

🎨 Picture it

Only two big shops sell this umbrella: the High Court and the Court of Session. The Magistrate's shop does not stock it.

3

Conditions

S.482(2)S.480(3)S.482(3)

The court may impose conditions, including that he shall make himself available for interrogation by the police as and when required; shall not induce, threaten or promise anyone acquainted with the facts; shall not leave India without permission; and other conditions as under S.480(3). If he is later arrested without warrant, he is released on bail (S.482(3)).

🧠 Memory trick

Police · Witnesses · Passport: be available to the police for interrogation, don't pressure witnesses, don't leave India without permission.

4

Exclusions

BNS 65BNS 70(2)S.482(4)S.18

Not available for rape of a woman under 16 (BNS 65) or gang rape of a woman under 18 (BNS 70(2)) (S.482(4)). Special laws may also bar it (e.g. the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, S.18).

🔢 Number trick

Both exclusions protect young girls: 16 → BNS 65 (rape of a woman under 16) and 18 → BNS 70(2) (gang rape of a woman under 18). The younger age goes with the smaller section number.

5

Duration

S.482

Anticipatory bail is not limited to a fixed period and can continue till the end of the trial, unless the court limits it for special reasons Sushila Aggarwal.

S.482 no longer lists the old factors (gravity of accusation, antecedents, risk of fleeing) in the text; courts still consider them as part of judicial discretion. Anticipatory bail is not a blanket protection for future offences; it is offence-specific.

🎨 Picture it

A warranty with no expiry date: it runs till the trial ends unless the court limits it for special reasons.

🎬 Case link

Remember it with Sushila Aggarwal (2020): "No expiry date on liberty."

6

Case law

  • Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia v. State of Punjab: The restrictions were struck down: anticipatory bail is a wide discretion to protect liberty, decided case by case.
  • Sushila Aggarwal v. State (NCT of Delhi): It need not be time-bound; it can continue till the end of the trial, unless the court limits it for special reasons.
Case story Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia: a Punjab Minister accused of corruption sought anticipatory bail; the High Court laid down strict limits on when it can be granted. A Constitution Bench struck those limits down: the power is wide and discretionary; it must not be cut down by rigid rules, and the court weighs each case on its facts.
🧠 Memory trick

1980 opened the door wide; 2020 removed the clock. Sibbia (1980): wide discretion, no rigid rules. Sushila Aggarwal (2020): no fixed time limit.

🔁 Recall: Think of an umbrella bought before the rain: only big shops sell it (HC and Sessions), it comes with rules (S.482(2)), some storms are excluded (BNS 65, 70(2)), and it lasts till the rain stops (trial ends).

6Golden rules

  • ★ 1Bailable = right; non-bailable = discretion. The First Schedule to the BNSS tells you which.
  • ★ 2"Bail is the rule, jail the exception"; arrest is not automatic even after a charge sheet. Satender Kumar Antil
  • ★ 3Anticipatory bail is not limited in time; it can last till the end of the trial. Sushila Aggarwal
  • ★ 4Poverty is no reason to keep a man in jail: an indigent accused can be released on a personal bond without sureties (S.478).
  • ★ 5If investigation is not complete within 60 / 90 days, the accused gets default bail as of right (S.187(3)).

7Part C: problem routes

Every problem takes the same four stops: issue → law → apply → conclusion.

1. A was arrested for a bailable offence and refused bail. What is his remedy under the BNSS?

  1. 🎯 IssueCan bail be refused in a bailable offence, and what can A do?
  2. 📜 LawS.478: in a bailable offence the person shall be released on bail; if he cannot give a surety within a week, on his own bond. S.47(2): he must be told of his right to bail. S.483: the High Court and Sessions Court can direct release. Art. 21, Constitution.
  3. 🔍 ApplyBail in a bailable offence is a right, not a discretion. Refusal by the police or Magistrate is illegal (unless he earlier broke bail conditions: S.478(2)).
  4. ✅ ConclusionA must be released at once; he can apply to the Magistrate under S.478, then to the Sessions Court or High Court under S.483, and if detained illegally, file habeas corpus.

2. A, who is on bail, threatens the witnesses (or commits another offence). What can be done?

  1. 🎯 IssueCan bail be cancelled for misuse of liberty?
  2. 📜 LawS.480(5): the court that granted bail may direct re-arrest and commit to custody. S.483: High Court / Sessions Court may cancel. Grounds: interfering with witnesses, tampering with evidence, absconding, committing another offence.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThreatening witnesses directly interferes with a fair trial. The witnesses or prosecution apply for cancellation; a fresh offence is also a separate case.
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe prosecution or witnesses can apply for cancellation of bail; the court will cancel it and send A to custody. The witnesses may also seek protection under the Witness Protection Scheme (S.398).

3. Laxmi, an undertrial for 6 months, is granted bail but cannot meet the conditions (or a poor man cannot pay the bond). Remedy?

  1. 🎯 IssueCan poverty keep an accused in jail after bail is granted?
  2. 📜 LawS.478(1) proviso: an indigent person (cannot give surety within a week) may be released on his own bond. S.484: bond amount shall not be excessive; the court may reduce it. Art. 21.
  3. 🔍 ApplyBail granted but unaffordable is bail denied. She should apply for reduction of the bond amount and release on personal bond.
  4. ✅ ConclusionShe should apply to modify the conditions; the court can reduce the amount and release her on a personal bond without sureties.

4. Ranjith, accused of corruption, was refused bail by the trial court and the higher court. Advise him.

  1. 🎯 IssueWhat further remedies does Ranjith have?
  2. 📜 LawS.483: High Court's special power. Art. 136: Supreme Court. S.187(3): default bail if no charge sheet in 60/90 days. S.479: undertrial release after half (first-time offender: one-third) of maximum. A fresh application lies on changed circumstances Satender Kumar Antil.
  3. 🔍 ApplyIf the High Court refused, he can approach the Supreme Court by special leave; he can also file a fresh application if circumstances change (charge sheet filed, long custody, delay in trial).
  4. ✅ ConclusionApply to the Supreme Court (Art. 136), or renew the application on changed circumstances, default bail or S.479.

5. A person arrested on suspicion of terrorism: can he be released on bail?

  1. 🎯 IssueIs bail available in grave offences under special laws?
  2. 📜 LawS.480(1): no bail by a Magistrate if there are reasonable grounds to believe the offence is punishable with death or life. Special laws (e.g. UAPA S.43D(5)) add stricter conditions. But if the court finds he is not involved, there are no "reasonable grounds", and he should be released.
  3. 🔍 ApplyOnce the court itself finds he was not involved, the bar does not apply; poverty cannot stop release (S.478 proviso logic, S.484).
  4. ✅ ConclusionYes, he should be released, if necessary on a personal bond; if no evidence remains, the police should also seek his discharge.
