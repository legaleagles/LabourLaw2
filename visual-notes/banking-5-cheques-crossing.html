✖️Cheques, Crossing and Drafts

An open cheque is cash in an envelope. Two lines, a bank's name, "not negotiable" and "A/c payee" are its seat belts.

🧠Mind map

1The big picture

An open cheque is like cash in an envelope: whoever holds it can walk to the counter and take the money. Draw two parallel lines across its corner and it becomes a cheque that can be paid only into a bank account. Now if it is stolen, the money leaves a trail, because it must pass through some bank account.

Add the words "Account Payee" and only the payee's own account can receive it. Add "Not Negotiable" and nobody who takes it can get a better title than the person who gave it to him. These small marks are the cheque's seat belts.

1

Definition (S.6)

S.6

A cheque is a bill of exchange drawn on a specified banker and not expressed to be payable otherwise than on demand; it includes the electronic image of a truncated cheque and a cheque in electronic form.

🎨 Picture it

A cheque is a bill wearing a bank uniform: always on a banker, always on demand.

🗣️ Jargon buster

It includes an electronic cheque and the image of a truncated cheque.

2

Essentials

(1) In writing; (2) an unconditional order; (3) drawn on a specified banker; (4) payable on demand; (5) signed by the drawer; (6) a certain sum of money; (7) a certain payee (or bearer); (8) dated (an undated cheque is incomplete). No stamp is needed.

🧠 Memory trick

Eight ticks: Writing · Order · Banker · Demand · Signed · Sum · Payee · Date. No stamp needed.

3

Kinds

Bearer (anyone holding it; transfer by delivery), order (payee or his order; transfer by indorsement), crossed and open, ante-dated and post-dated, stale (older than three months), mutilated, self cheque, banker's cheque / pay order, electronic and truncated cheques (cheque truncation system clears the image, not the paper).

📖 Word origin

Stale = older than 3 months. Post-dated = dated in the future.

🎬 Case link

Anil Kumar Sawhney: a post-dated cheque is only a bill until its date; validity runs from the date written on it.

4

Bill v. cheque

S.84S.31S.30

(1) A cheque is always drawn on a banker; a bill on anyone. (2) A cheque is payable on demand only; a bill may be payable after a period. (3) A cheque needs no acceptance. (4) No days of grace for a cheque. (5) A cheque can be crossed; a bill cannot. (6) A cheque needs no stamp. (7) The drawer of a cheque can stop payment; an accepted bill cannot be countermanded. (8) Delay in presenting a cheque discharges the drawer only to the extent of his actual loss (S.84); delay in presenting a bill discharges the drawer fully.

Example: Sita gives Gopal a cheque "Pay Gopal or order ₹10,000" on her SBI account. SBI (drawee) must pay Gopal on demand if Sita has funds; if SBI wrongly refuses, it must compensate Sita (S.31), and Gopal can sue Sita (S.30).
🧠 Memory trick

A cheque: Banker · Demand · No acceptance · No grace days · Can be crossed.

5

Drawee's liability

S.31

The drawee bank, having sufficient funds of the drawer properly applicable, must pay the cheque when duly required and, in default, compensate the drawer. The bank owes no duty to the payee or holder (no privity), who must sue the drawer.

🧠 Memory trick

No privity between the bank and the payee: the payee sues the drawer, the drawer sues the bank.

🌍 Real life

SBI wrongly refuses Sita's cheque: SBI compensates Sita (S.31), and Gopal sues Sita (S.30).

🔁 Recall: A cheque is a bill that wears a bank uniform: always on a banker, always on demand, never waits for acceptance or grace days, and can wear crossing lines.

3Crossing

Exam question: Crossing of cheques: meaning, objects, kinds, who may cross, and "account payee"

1

Meaning

Crossing is two parallel transverse lines on the face of a cheque, with or without words, which direct the paying banker to pay the amount only to a banker, not over the counter.

🎨 Picture it

Two parallel lines are a seat belt: no cash at the counter, only into a bank account.

2

Objects

S.128S.131

(1) Safety: a thief or finder cannot take cash at the counter; (2) traceability: the money goes into an account, so the receiver can be traced; (3) protection of the true owner and of honest bankers (S.128, S.131).

🧠 Memory trick

Safety · Trace · Protect: a thief can't cash it, the money leaves a trail, honest owners and bankers are protected.

3

General crossing

S.123S.126

Two parallel lines, with or without the words "and company" or any abbreviation, and with or without "not negotiable". Effect: paid only to a banker (S.126).

🧠 Memory trick

Two lines → only to a banker (S.123, S.126).

4

Special crossing

S.124S.126S.127

The name of a banker is added (lines are not essential). Effect: paid only to the banker named, or his agent for collection (S.126). A cheque crossed specially to two banks is refused unless one is the other's agent (S.127).

🧠 Memory trick

A bank's name → only to that bank (S.124). Crossed to two banks? Refused, unless one is the other's agent (S.127).

5

Not negotiable

S.130

The cheque can still be transferred, but the transferee cannot get or give a better title than his transferor. If it was stolen, even an honest buyer gets no title.

🧠 Memory trick

Transfer yes, better title no.

🌍 Real life

A "not negotiable" cheque is stolen and sold: even an honest buyer gets no title.

6

Account payee

S.131

Not in the Act; it is a banking practice backed by RBI directions: the collecting banker must credit the proceeds only to the payee's account. Collecting it for anyone else is negligence and costs the bank its S.131 protection. Tax law requires account payee cheques or electronic transfer for large loans and deposits.

Example: a cheque crossed "A/c payee, Ravi" falls into a stranger's hands. He cannot cash it at the counter and no bank may credit it to his account. Ravi's money is safe.
🌍 Real life

A cheque crossed "A/c payee, Ravi" falls into a stranger's hands: no bank may credit it to anyone but Ravi.

🗣️ Jargon buster

Not in the Act: a banking practice backed by RBI directions. Collecting it for someone else is negligence and loses S.131 protection.

7

Who may cross

S.125

The drawer; the holder (may cross an open cheque generally or specially, turn a general crossing into a special one, and add "not negotiable"); and a banker to whom a cheque is crossed specially, who may cross it specially to another banker as its agent for collection.

🧠 Memory trick

Drawer · Holder · Banker can cross (S.125).

8

Paying banker's duty

S.129S.128

Paying a crossed cheque against the crossing makes the banker liable to the true owner for any loss (S.129); paying in due course according to the crossing protects it (S.128).

🔢 Number trick

Against the crossing → liable to the true owner (S.129). According to the crossing, in due course → protected (S.128).

🔁 Recall: Count the seat belts: two lines (general), a bank's name (special), "not negotiable" (no better title), "A/c payee" (only the payee's account).

4Drafts and other instruments

Exam question: Bank drafts, dividend warrants and other instruments; deemed negotiable instruments

  1. Demand draft
  2. Negotiability of a draft
  3. Dividend warrant
  4. Other bank instruments
  5. Not negotiable
1

Demand draft

An order by one office of a bank on another office of the same bank to pay a sum on demand to a named person or his order. The buyer pays the bank first, so the payee gets near-certain payment.

🎨 Picture it

A demand draft is the bank drawing on itself: the buyer pays first, so payment is near-certain.

2

Negotiability of a draft

S.131AS.85A

A draft payable to order is a negotiable instrument by mercantile usage (it is a bill of exchange drawn by a bank). It can be indorsed and crossed; the crossing rules and the collecting banker's protection apply to drafts (S.131A), and the paying office is protected by S.85A. Since the bank itself is the drawer, the buyer usually cannot stop payment once it has been delivered to the payee.

🔢 Number trick

Crossing rules and the collecting banker's protection apply to drafts too (S.131A).

3

Dividend warrant

An order by a company on its banker to pay a shareholder his dividend. It is treated like a cheque (payable on demand, can be crossed and collected through a bank).

🎨 Picture it

A dividend warrant is the company's cheque to its shareholder.

4

Other bank instruments

S.1

Banker's cheque / pay order (for local payment); traveller's cheque (prepaid, signed once on purchase and again on encashment; now rare); hundis (indigenous instruments governed by local usage, saved by S.1 NI Act); bearer debentures and share warrants to bearer (negotiable by custom).

🧠 Memory trick

Pay order = local · traveller's cheque = sign twice · hundi = governed by local usage.

5

Not negotiable

Postal orders, money orders, fixed deposit receipts and share certificates. They may be transferred or encashed, but they do not carry the special features of negotiable instruments (better title, presumptions).

🧠 Memory trick

Not negotiable: postal orders, money orders, FD receipts, share certificates.

🔁 Recall: Think of a bank counter selling paper: a DD (bank on itself), a pay order (local), a dividend warrant (company's cheque); the post office next door sells postal orders and money orders, which are not negotiable.

7Golden rules

  • ★ 1A cheque is a bill drawn on a banker, payable on demand; it includes an electronic cheque and the image of a truncated cheque (S.6).
  • ★ 2A crossing is a direction to the paying banker: pay only through a bank.
  • ★ 3"Not negotiable" does not stop transfer; it stops a better title (S.130).
  • ★ 4A post-dated cheque is a bill until its date; its validity runs from the date written on it. Anil Kumar Sawhney
  • ★ 5If a cheque is not presented in reasonable time and the bank fails meanwhile, the drawer is discharged to the extent of his loss (S.84).

8Part C: problem routes

Every problem takes the same four stops: issue → law → apply → conclusion.

1. A, indebted to B, gives B a cheque, which B presents in due time. Payment is refused because the bank has failed. What are B's rights?

  1. 🎯 IssueWho bears the loss when a cheque presented in time is not paid because the bank failed?
  2. 📜 LawS.30: the drawer is liable to the holder if the cheque is dishonoured. S.84: only if the holder delays presentment and the drawer suffers loss through the bank's failure is the drawer discharged to that extent, the holder becoming a creditor of the bank.
  3. 🔍 ApplyB presented in due time, so S.84 does not help A. The cheque was dishonoured; A's original debt revives.
  4. ✅ ConclusionB can sue A on the cheque and on the original debt. A must claim his balance from the failed bank's liquidator. (Had B delayed, the loss would have fallen on B to the extent of A's loss.)

2. Ram could not present a cheque from his friend because he was busy. The last day of its validity (or limitation) falls on a holiday. Advise Ram.

  1. 🎯 IssueWhat happens if the last day for presenting a cheque or suing falls on a holiday?
  2. 📜 LawRBI rule: a cheque is valid for three months from its date; there is no grace for a holiday, so it should be presented on the last working day before. Limitation Act S.4: if the period for a suit expires on a day the court is closed, it may be filed on reopening. General Clauses Act S.10 applies to S.142 complaints. The debt itself is recoverable within three years.
  3. 🔍 ApplyRam should present the cheque before the holiday (e.g. through a drop box or electronic deposit). If it becomes stale, the cheque cannot be presented, but the debt survives.
  4. ✅ ConclusionPresent the cheque on the preceding working day; if it lapses, ask the friend to revalidate it or issue a fresh cheque, or sue for the debt within limitation. A court filing that falls due on a holiday can be made on the next working day.

3. A cheque payable to "X or bearer" is lost. Y finds it and (a) cashes it at the bank, or (b) gives it to Z, who takes it honestly for value. Rights of X?

  1. 🎯 IssueWhat title does a finder or a later holder get to a bearer cheque?
  2. 📜 LawS.85(2): a banker paying a bearer cheque in due course is discharged. S.58: a finder gets no title. S.47, S.9: a bearer cheque passes by delivery; a holder in due course gets a good title even from a finder.
  3. 🔍 Apply(a) The bank paid the bearer in due course before any stop order: it is protected. (b) Z is a holder in due course and gets a good title.
  4. ✅ ConclusionX cannot recover from the bank or from Z; he can recover from Y. Had X informed the bank before payment, the bank would have been liable if it paid. Lesson: always cross cheques.

4. A cheque crossed "A/c payee — Ravi" is indorsed by Ravi to Shyam. Shyam's bank credits it to Shyam's account. Ravi later says his signature was forged. Is Shyam's bank protected?

  1. 🎯 IssueCan a collecting banker credit an account payee cheque to someone other than the payee?
  2. 📜 Law"Account payee" is a direction to the collecting banker to credit only the payee's account. S.131: protection only if the banker acts without negligence.
  3. 🔍 ApplyCrediting an account payee cheque to a third party's account, without enquiry, is negligence.
  4. ✅ ConclusionShyam's bank loses S.131 protection and is liable to Ravi (the true owner) for conversion.
