🪜Appeals, Review and Revision

Four doors for the loser: appeal ("the judge was wrong"), review ("look again"), revision ("supervise the lower court"), reference ("guide me on the law").

🧠Mind map

1The big picture

A losing party has four doors, and each opens for a different reason. Appeal: "the judge was wrong; let a higher court re-hear it." Review: "the same judge made an obvious mistake, or I found new evidence; please look again." Revision: "the lower court acted without jurisdiction or illegally; High Court, please supervise." Reference: "I, the lower court, have a doubt about the law; High Court, please guide me."

An appeal is a creature of statute: it exists only where a law gives it. The first appeal re-opens facts and law; the second appeal is allowed only on a substantial question of law; and revision is the High Court's supervisory eye, not another appeal.

2Appeals

1

Meaning and rules

An appeal is the judicial examination by a higher court of a decision of a lower court, to correct errors of fact or law. The CPC does not define it. Rules: (1) it is a creature of statute, not an inherent right; (2) it is a vested right from the date of the suit, so later laws cannot take it away unless they say so expressly; (3) an appeal is a continuation of the suit.

🧠 Memory trick

An appeal is a creature of statute: no law, no appeal.

🗣️ Jargon buster

It is a vested right from the date of the suit; later laws cannot take it away unless they say so.

2

First appeal (S.96)

S.96O.XLIS.96(2)S.96(3)

From every decree passed by a court of original jurisdiction, unless barred; an ex parte decree too (S.96(2)); but not from a consent decree (S.96(3)). It lies on questions of fact and of law; the first appellate court is the final court of facts.

🧠 Memory trick

First for facts: the first appellate court is the last court of facts.

🗣️ Jargon buster

Ex parte decree: appealable (S.96(2)). Consent decree: no appeal (S.96(3)).

3

Second appeal (S.100)

S.100S.102S.100A

To the High Court from a decree passed in appeal by a subordinate court, only if the case involves a substantial question of law. The memorandum states the question; the High Court frames it and hears the appeal on it. No second appeal in money suits up to ₹25,000 (S.102). S.100A: no further (Letters Patent) appeal from a single judge.

Case story Santosh Hazari: a trial court held a man was forcibly dispossessed in 1981 and decreed his suit. The first appellate court reversed it in a few cryptic lines, finding adverse possession, and the High Court refused to hear the second appeal. The Supreme Court sent it back: a first appellate court that reverses must engage with the evidence and give its own reasons, and a "substantial" question of law is one that is debatable, not settled, and decides the case.
🧠 Memory trick

Second for law: only a substantial question of law, framed by the High Court.

🎬 Case link

Santosh Hazari: a substantial question is debatable, unsettled and decisive; reverse only with your own reasons.

🔢 Number trick

No second appeal in money suits up to ₹25,000 (S.102).

4

Appeals from orders

S.104O.XLIII r.1O.VII r.10O.IX r.13O.XXXIX r.1

Only the orders listed are appealable, e.g. returning a plaint (O.VII r.10), refusing to set aside an ex parte decree (O.IX r.13), on temporary injunctions (O.XXXIX r.1, 2, 2A, 4), appointing a receiver (O.XL r.1). S.104(2): no appeal lies from an order passed in appeal under this section. S.105: an error in a non-appealable order can be raised in the appeal against the final decree.

🧠 Memory trick

Only listed orders are appealable (O.XLIII): returning a plaint, refusing to set aside an ex parte decree, injunctions, receivers.

🗣️ Jargon buster

No appeal from an order passed in appeal (S.104(2)).

6

Procedure (O.XLI)

O.XLIS.107

r.1: a memorandum of appeal setting out the grounds concisely, accompanied by a copy of the judgment; in an appeal against a money decree, the disputed amount must be deposited or security given (r.1(3)). r.3: rejection or return for amendment. r.5: no automatic stay; stay of execution for sufficient cause and on security. r.11: dismissal at the admission stage after hearing the appellant. r.22: cross-objection by the respondent. r.23–25: remand. r.27: additional evidence only in limited cases. r.31: the appellate judgment states the points, the decision and the reasons. Powers (S.107): decide finally, remand, frame issues and refer them, take additional evidence.

First appeal for facts, second appeal for law, and appeals from orders only where the Code lists them.

🔢 Number trick

Appeal against a money decree? Deposit the disputed amount or give security (r.1(3)).

🧠 Memory trick

No automatic stay: ask for one (r.5).

🔁 Recall: Santosh Hazari: reverse with reasons; a substantial question is debatable and decisive.

1

Reference (S.113)

S.113O.XLVI

A subordinate court may state a case and refer a doubt on a question of law to the High Court. It must refer where the case involves the validity of an Act or rule, which it considers invalid but which the High Court or Supreme Court has not yet so declared. The High Court answers; the lower court decides accordingly.

🎨 Picture it

Reference: the lower court raises its hand and asks the High Court about the law.

🗣️ Jargon buster

It must refer when it thinks an Act or rule is invalid but no higher court has said so yet.

2

Review (S.114)

S.114O.XLVIIO.XLVII r.1

A person aggrieved by a decree or order from which no appeal is allowed, or an appeal is allowed but not preferred, may apply to the same court that passed it on these grounds (O.XLVII r.1): (1) discovery of new and important evidence which, despite due diligence, was not within his knowledge or could not be produced earlier; (2) a mistake or error apparent on the face of the record; (3) any other sufficient reason (analogous to the first two).

🧠 Memory trick

Same court, three grounds: new evidence · error apparent · other sufficient reason.

3

Limits of review

Art. 124

It is not an appeal in disguise; an error that needs a long process of reasoning is not "apparent" (Aribam Tuleshwar Sharma v. Aribam Pishak Sharma, 1979). Ordinarily the same judge hears it (r.5). No review of an order rejecting a review (r.9). Limitation: 30 days (Art. 124).

🧠 Memory trick

Review is not an appeal in disguise.

🗣️ Jargon buster

An error that needs a long process of reasoning is not "apparent" (Aribam Tuleshwar Sharma, 1979). No review of an order rejecting a review (r.9).

4

Revision (S.115)

S.115

The High Court may call for the record of a case decided by a subordinate court in which no appeal lies, if that court (a) exercised a jurisdiction not vested in it, (b) failed to exercise a jurisdiction so vested, or (c) acted illegally or with material irregularity in exercising it. Proviso (1999, in force 2002): the High Court shall not vary or reverse an order unless, had it been made in the applicant's favour, it would have finally disposed of the suit or proceeding.

Case story Shiv Shakti: after the 2002 amendment, High Courts dismissed revisions against interim orders such as refusals of injunction. The Supreme Court agreed: revision now lies only if a decision in the applicant's favour would end the suit; interim orders are outside it, and pending revisions were not saved, since revision is not a vested right.
🧠 Memory trick

Three jurisdiction errors: Not vested · Not exercised · Illegally exercised.

🎬 Case link

Shiv Shakti: revision lies only if a decision in the applicant's favour would end the suit.

5

Other points

S.115(2)S.115(3)Art. 131

It is a supervisory power, not an appeal: the High Court does not re-assess facts. S.115(2): no revision where an appeal lies to the High Court or a subordinate court. S.115(3): a revision does not operate as a stay unless the High Court stays the proceedings. Limitation: 90 days (Art. 131). Where revision is barred, Article 227 (supervisory jurisdiction) may still be invoked.

🎨 Picture it

Revision is a supervisor checking the lower court's power, not a re-hearing of facts.

🔢 Number trick

90 days (Art. 131); no stay unless the High Court orders one (S.115(3)).

6Golden rules

  • ★ 1Appeal is not an inherent right; it exists only where a statute gives it.
  • ★ 2The first appellate court must deal with the evidence and give its own reasons, especially when it reverses. Santosh Hazari
  • ★ 3Second appeal lies only on a substantial question of law, framed by the High Court (S.100). Santosh Hazari
  • ★ 4Review is not an appeal in disguise; it is heard by the same court.
  • ★ 5Since 2002, no revision against an interim order that would not finally decide the suit if decided in the applicant's favour. Shiv Shakti

7Part C: problem routes

Every problem takes the same four stops: issue → law → apply → conclusion.

1. Mr. G is aggrieved by an order passed by the District Court in an appeal. Can he prefer any further appeal?

  1. 🎯 IssueIs a further appeal available against a District Court's decision in appeal?
  2. 📜 LawS.100: second appeal to the High Court from a decree passed in appeal, only on a substantial question of law. S.104(2): no appeal lies from an order passed in appeal under S.104. S.115: revision where no appeal lies. Art. 227: supervisory jurisdiction.
  3. 🔍 ApplyIf the District Court's decision in appeal is a decree (an appeal from a decree under S.96), G can file a second appeal in the High Court if a substantial question of law arises. If it is an order passed in an appeal from an order (S.104/O.XLIII), no further appeal lies; G may seek revision (if the S.115 proviso is met) or invoke Article 227.
  4. ✅ ConclusionSecond appeal under S.100 if it is an appellate decree and a substantial question of law arises; if it is an appellate order, no further appeal (S.104(2)), only revision or Art. 227.

2. A judgment was delivered against X by a lower court. He wants to file a review petition in the next appellate court. Maintainable?

  1. 🎯 IssueWhich court hears a review?
  2. 📜 LawS.114 and O.XLVII r.1: the application for review is made to the court which passed the decree or order. r.5: ordinarily the same judge hears it.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe appellate court did not pass the judgment, so it cannot review it. X's remedy in the appellate court is an appeal, not a review.
  4. ✅ ConclusionNot maintainable: a review lies only before the same court that passed the judgment; in the higher court X must file an appeal.

3. The trial court refuses a temporary injunction; the defendant's appeal is decided; the plaintiff files a revision in the High Court against the interim order. Maintainable?

  1. 🎯 IssueDoes revision lie against an interim order?
  2. 📜 LawS.115 proviso (2002): revision lies only if the order, had it been made in the applicant's favour, would have finally disposed of the suit Shiv Shakti. Art. 227 remains available.
  3. 🔍 ApplyEven if the injunction were granted, the suit would continue; the order does not finally decide the suit.
  4. ✅ ConclusionRevision is not maintainable against such an interim order; the party may invoke Article 227 in a fit case.

4. The first appellate court reversed the trial court's decree in a few lines without discussing the evidence. Remedy?

  1. 🎯 IssueIs a cryptic reversing judgment open to challenge in second appeal?
  2. 📜 LawO.XLI r.31: the appellate judgment must state the points, the decision and reasons. S.100: second appeal on a substantial question of law Santosh Hazari.
  3. 🔍 ApplyReversing without engaging with the trial court's reasons is a failure of the first appellate court's duty, which raises a substantial question of law.
  4. ✅ ConclusionFile a second appeal under S.100; the High Court can set aside the judgment and remand it for a reasoned decision.
