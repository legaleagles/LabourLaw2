🏛️Criminal Courts and Sessions Trial

Criminal courts are a staircase: 1 · 3 · 7 · any. And a Sessions trial is a play in seven scenes.

🧠Mind map

1The big picture

Criminal courts are a staircase. At the bottom, the Judicial Magistrate of the second class (up to 1 year); above him, the first class Magistrate (up to 3 years); then the Chief Judicial Magistrate (up to 7 years); and at the top of the district, the Court of Session, which can pass any sentence, even death (but death needs the High Court's confirmation). The First Schedule tells you which step each offence climbs to.

A Sessions trial is like a play in acts: the Magistrate commits the case, the Public Prosecutor opens it, the judge frames the charge (or discharges), the prosecution and defence lead evidence, both argue, and the judge delivers judgment within 30 days (extendable to 45).

2Criminal courts

Exam question: Classes of criminal courts and their powers: Court of Session, Sessions Judge, Chief Judicial Magistrate (S.6–23)

  1. Classes of courts
  2. Court of Session
  3. Magistrates
  4. Sentencing powers
  5. Executive Magistrates
1

Classes of courts

S.6

Besides the High Courts and courts under other laws, there are: (1) Courts of Session; (2) Judicial Magistrates of the first class; (3) Judicial Magistrates of the second class; (4) Executive Magistrates. (The BNSS has dropped the separate category of Metropolitan Magistrates.)

🧠 Memory trick

S · 1 · 2 · E: Sessions, first class, second class, Executive.

🗣️ Jargon buster

The BNSS dropped Metropolitan Magistrates as a separate class.

2

Court of Session

S.8S.213

One for every sessions division, presided over by a Sessions Judge appointed by the High Court; Additional Sessions Judges may also be appointed. It tries the most serious offences, mostly on committal by a Magistrate (S.213).

📖 Word origin

Session comes from Latin sessio, "a sitting": the court that sits for the gravest cases.

🎨 Picture it

The top step of the district staircase. Most cases reach it by committal from a Magistrate (S.213).

3

Magistrates

In every district, a Chief Judicial Magistrate heads the Judicial Magistrates; Judicial Magistrates of the first and second class are appointed by the High Court in consultation with the State. Special Judicial Magistrates may be conferred powers for particular cases.

🎨 Picture it

The CJM is the captain of the district's Judicial Magistrates.

4

Sentencing powers

S.22S.407S.25

The High Court may pass any sentence authorised by law. The Sessions Judge may pass any sentence authorised by law, but a death sentence is subject to confirmation by the High Court (S.407). The CJM: any sentence except death, life, or imprisonment above 7 years. JMFC: up to 3 years, fine up to ₹50,000, or both, or community service. JMSC: up to 1 year, fine up to ₹10,000, or both, or community service. For several offences at one trial, sentences may run consecutively within limits (S.25).

🧠 Memory trick

Prison: 1 · 3 · 7 · any. Fines: ₹10,000 · ₹50,000 for JMSC and JMFC.

🔢 Number trick

A Sessions Judge can sentence to death, but the High Court must confirm it (S.407).

🗣️ Jargon buster

Community service is now a sentence a Magistrate can pass.

5

Executive Magistrates

S.148S.163BNS 303(2)BNS 318(4)BNS 103

The District Magistrate, Additional DM, Sub-Divisional Magistrates and others appointed by the State. They do not try offences; they keep the public peace (e.g. dispersal of unlawful assemblies, S.148–151; orders in urgent cases of nuisance, S.163), and hold inquests in certain cases.

Example: a theft (BNS 303(2), up to 3 years) is triable by any Magistrate; cheating (BNS 318(4), up to 7 years) by a JMFC; murder (BNS 103) and rape (BNS 64) only by the Court of Session. Check the sixth column of the First Schedule.
🎨 Picture it

Executive Magistrates are peacekeepers, not judges: they do not try offences.

🌍 Real life

When an area is put under urgent restrictions because of danger or nuisance, that is an Executive Magistrate's order under S.163.

🔁 Recall: Climb the staircase 1 · 3 · 7 · any: JMSC (1 year) → JMFC (3 years) → CJM (7 years) → Sessions (any sentence; death confirmed by the HC).

3Inquiry, investigation, trial

Exam question: Inquiry, investigation and trial · Venue and place of trial (S.197–209)

  1. Investigation
  2. Inquiry
  3. Trial
  4. Ordinary place of trial
  5. Special rules
1

Investigation

S.193

Collection of evidence by the police (or a person authorised by a Magistrate); it ends with the police report (S.193).

🧠 Memory trick

Police investigate · Magistrates inquire · Courts try.

2

Inquiry

S.2S.225S.144

Every inquiry, other than a trial, conducted by a Magistrate or Court: e.g. inquiry before issuing process on a complaint (S.225), inquiry into maintenance (S.144), into security for keeping the peace, or the committal stage.

🗣️ Jargon buster

Inquiry = anything a Magistrate or court does that is not a trial: before issuing process (S.225), maintenance (S.144), the committal stage.

3

Trial

Not defined; it is the judicial examination of the guilt or innocence of the accused, which begins with framing of the charge (or in summons cases, stating the accusation) and ends in acquittal or conviction. Kinds: Sessions trial, warrant-case trial, summons-case trial, summary trial.

🎨 Picture it

A trial has two bookends: it opens with the charge and closes with acquittal or conviction.

🧠 Memory trick

Four kinds: Sessions · Warrant · Summons · Summary.

4

Ordinary place of trial

S.197

Every offence is ordinarily inquired into and tried by a court within whose local jurisdiction it was committed.

🧠 Memory trick

The crime's address is the court's address (S.197).

5

Special rules

S.198S.199S.201S.202S.208

Where it is uncertain in which area the offence was committed, or it was committed partly in one area and partly in another, or is a continuing offence, it may be tried in any of those areas (S.198). Where an act is done in one area and its consequence follows in another, in either (S.199). Kidnapping or abduction: where the person was kidnapped, conveyed, concealed or detained; theft: also where the stolen property was possessed (S.201). Offences by letters or electronic communication: where sent or received (S.202). Offences outside India by an Indian citizen: as if committed where he is found, with Central Government sanction (S.208).

Example (asked Nov 2020): a girl is kidnapped in Warangal, taken to Hyderabad, raped and murdered there. Courts at Warangal (kidnapping, S.201) and Hyderabad (rape and murder, S.197) both have jurisdiction, and all offences, being one transaction, can be tried together (S.243) by the Court of Session.
🎨 Picture it

A crime in pieces can be tried where any piece happened: act here, consequence there (S.199); kidnapped here, concealed there (S.201).

🌍 Real life

A message sent from one city and received in another: either city can try it (S.202).

🔁 Recall: Police investigate, Magistrates inquire, Courts try; and the trial goes where the crime happened, or anywhere its pieces happened.

4Sessions trial

Exam question: Trial before a Court of Session (S.248–260)

🧠 Say it: “Cool Officers Detect Problems, Prosecutors Defend Justice”

  1. CCoolCommittal
  2. OOfficersOpening by Public Prosecutor
  3. DDetectDischarge or charge
  4. PProblemsPlea
  5. PProsecutorsProsecution evidence
  6. DDefendDefence
  7. JJusticeArguments and judgment
1

Committal

S.232

When an offence is triable exclusively by the Court of Session, the Magistrate commits the case after supplying documents to the accused, within 90 days of cognizance (extendable to 180).

📖 Word origin

Commit = hand the case over: the Magistrate commits it to the Court of Session.

🔢 Number trick

90 days from cognizance, extendable to 180.

2

Opening by Public Prosecutor

S.248

The trial is conducted by the Public Prosecutor, who opens the case by describing the charge and the evidence he will lead.

🎨 Picture it

The Prosecutor's opening is the trailer of the case: the charge and the evidence he will lead.

3

Discharge or charge

S.250

The accused may apply for discharge within 60 days of committal; if the judge finds no sufficient ground after hearing both sides, he discharges him with reasons. If there is ground to presume the offence, he frames the charge in writing within 60 days of the first hearing on charge, and reads and explains it.

🧠 Memory trick

No ground → discharge. Ground to presume → charge.

🔢 Number trick

60 + 60: discharge application within 60 days of committal; charge within 60 days of the first hearing on charge.

4

Plea

S.252

If the accused pleads guilty, the judge records it and may convict; otherwise the trial proceeds.

🎨 Picture it

One question: "Guilty or not?" A guilty plea may end the play early.

5

Prosecution evidence

S.253S.254S.255

The judge fixes dates and takes all prosecution evidence (S.254), including by audio-video means; the accused cross-examines. If there is no evidence that the accused committed the offence, the judge acquits him (S.255).

🗣️ Jargon buster

S.255: if there is no evidence that he did it, acquittal, without even calling on the defence.

6

Defence

S.256

Otherwise the accused is called on to enter upon his defence and lead evidence; he may also file a written statement. Process to compel witnesses is issued unless the request is vexatious.

🧠 Memory trick

The defence may bring witnesses; the court compels them unless the request is vexatious.

7

Arguments and judgment

S.257S.258(2)S.358

The prosecutor sums up, the defence replies. The judge delivers judgment within 30 days of the arguments, extendable to 45 days for reasons recorded. If convicted, the judge hears the accused on sentence before passing it (S.258(2)).

Case story Hardeep Singh: during a murder trial, a witness's evidence pointed to persons not named in the charge sheet, and the court summoned them. A Constitution Bench held that the court may summon any person who appears from the evidence (even the examination-in-chief) to be guilty, but the evidence must show more than a prima facie case: strong enough that, unrebutted, it would lead to conviction (S.358 BNSS).
🔢 Number trick

30 days after arguments, up to 45 with reasons.

🧠 Memory trick

Convicted? Hear him on sentence first (S.258(2)).

🎬 Case link

Hardeep Singh: the court can summon anyone the evidence shows to be guilty, even if not named in the charge sheet (S.358).

🔁 Recall: Act it out as a 7-scene play: commit → open → charge → plea → prosecution → defence → judgment; the numbers to remember are 90 · 60 · 60 · 30/45 days.

5Prosecutor, defence, police

Exam question: Public Prosecutor, withdrawal from prosecution, defence counsel and the police (S.18–20, S.360)

🧠 Say it: “Prosecutors Rarely Withdraw; Defence Persists”

  1. PProsecutorsPublic Prosecutor
  2. RRarelyRole
  3. WWithdrawWithdrawal
  4. DDefenceDefence counsel
  5. PPersistsPolice
1

Public Prosecutor

S.18S.19S.20

Appointed for the High Court and every district, from advocates with at least 7 years' practice; Assistant Public Prosecutors conduct cases before Magistrates (S.19); a Directorate of Prosecution supervises them (S.20). A victim may engage an advocate to assist the prosecution.

🔢 Number trick

7 years' practice to be a Public Prosecutor. S.18 PP · S.19 APP · S.20 Directorate of Prosecution.

🌍 Real life

A victim may engage an advocate of her own to assist the prosecution.

2

Role

He is an officer of the court, not a mere agent of the police: his duty is to help the court find the truth, place all evidence fairly (even evidence favouring the accused), and not to secure a conviction at any cost.

🎨 Picture it

The Prosecutor is a referee who wears one team's jersey: he places all the evidence fairly, even what helps the accused.

🧠 Memory trick

Fair, not fierce.

3

Withdrawal

S.360

The Public Prosecutor may, with the consent of the court, withdraw from the prosecution at any time before judgment: before charge, the accused is discharged; after charge, acquitted. The BNSS adds that no withdrawal is allowed without hearing the victim. The court must be satisfied that he applied his mind independently and in public interest, not for extraneous reasons (Sheonandan Paswan v. State of Bihar, 1987).

🧠 Memory trick

Before charge → discharge. After charge → acquittal.

🗣️ Jargon buster

BNSS: no withdrawal without hearing the victim. Sheonandan Paswan (1987): the court checks that the Prosecutor acted independently and in public interest.

4

Defence counsel

Art. 22S.341

Every accused has a right to be defended by a lawyer of his choice (Art. 22(1)); legal aid at State expense if he has no means (S.341). The defence lawyer must defend fearlessly, but must not mislead the court.

🧠 Memory trick

Fearless, not false. Defend boldly, never mislead the court.

5

Police

They prevent and detect crime, register FIRs, investigate, arrest, search and seize, and file the police report; a police officer is not a prosecutor, and a confession to him is inadmissible.

🧠 Memory trick

Investigate, not prosecute. A confession to the police is inadmissible.

🔁 Recall: Three players, one rule each: Prosecutor = fair, not fierce; Defence = fearless, not false; Police = investigate, not prosecute.

8Golden rules

  • ★ 1Sessions Judge: any sentence, but death needs High Court confirmation (S.22, S.407).
  • ★ 2CJM: up to 7 years · JMFC: 3 years / ₹50,000 / community service · JMSC: 1 year / ₹10,000 (S.23).
  • ★ 3Ordinary rule: an offence is tried where it was committed (S.197); if acts or consequences spread over areas, in any of them (S.198–201).
  • ★ 4The trial court can summon any other person who appears from the evidence to be guilty (S.358). Hardeep Singh
  • ★ 5Withdrawal from prosecution needs the court's consent, and the BNSS requires the victim to be heard (S.360).

9Part C: problem routes

Every problem takes the same four stops: issue → law → apply → conclusion.

1. Within one year a person committed theft, cheating and criminal trespass. Which court tries him, and what punishment?

  1. 🎯 IssueWhich court has jurisdiction over several different offences, and what sentence can it pass?
  2. 📜 LawTheft BNS 303(2): up to 3 years, fine, or both. Cheating BNS 318(4): up to 7 years and fine. Criminal trespass BNS 329(3): up to 3 months or fine up to ₹5,000, or both. First Schedule: theft by any Magistrate; cheating by JMFC. S.241: a separate charge for each distinct offence; S.242 (same kind) does not apply as they are different kinds; S.243 only if one transaction. S.23 and S.25: JMFC sentencing limits and consecutive sentences.
  3. 🔍 ApplyAll three are triable by a JMFC, separately charged; he can be tried at one trial if they form one series, otherwise in separate cases.
  4. ✅ ConclusionA Judicial Magistrate of the first class can try all three, framing separate charges; punishment within the BNS limits for each and the JMFC's powers (3 years / ₹50,000 per offence, sentences can run consecutively under S.25).

2. Two persons took away a box of gold chains from a shop; at home they found it empty. Have they committed any offence? Which court? Punishment?

  1. 🎯 IssueIs it theft, or attempt to commit theft, when the box turns out to be empty?
  2. 📜 LawBNS 303: theft (dishonestly moving movable property out of a person's possession). BNS 62: attempt to commit an offence punishable with imprisonment, up to half of the longest term. BNS 3(5): common intention. Classic illustration: breaking open a box to steal jewels and finding it empty is an attempt to commit theft.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThey dishonestly took the box itself, which is theft of the box; and as to the gold chains, they did everything towards stealing them, so it is an attempt to commit theft. Both acted with common intention.
  4. ✅ ConclusionYes: theft of the box and attempt to commit theft of the chains (BNS 303(2), 62 with 3(5)); triable by any Magistrate; attempt punishable up to half the term for theft (up to 1½ years), or fine, or both.

3. At 2 a.m., A committed robbery, extortion and murder (or set fire to a house for robbery, burning three people to death). How to file the case, which court, what punishment?

  1. 🎯 IssueProcedure and forum for grave offences in one transaction.
  2. 📜 LawFIR under S.173 (cognizable). Murder BNS 103: death or life and fine. Robbery BNS 309(4): rigorous imprisonment up to 10 years (up to 14 years if on a highway between sunset and sunrise). Extortion BNS 308(2): up to 7 years. Mischief by fire to destroy a house BNS 326(g). Causing disappearance of evidence BNS 238. S.243: offences in one transaction tried together. S.232: committal to Sessions.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe family or any informant lodges an FIR; police investigate and file the charge sheet; the Magistrate commits the case; the Court of Session tries all offences together.
  4. ✅ ConclusionFIR at the police station → investigation → committal → trial by the Court of Session; punishment up to death or life imprisonment for murder, with sentences for the other offences.

4. A subordinate makes a defamatory statement against a senior police officer. How to proceed, and which court?

  1. 🎯 IssueWho can prosecute for defamation, and before which court?
  2. 📜 LawDefamation BNS 356: simple imprisonment up to 2 years, or fine, or both, or community service. S.222 BNSS: no court takes cognizance of defamation except on a complaint by the person aggrieved; where the defamed person is a public servant and it relates to his public functions, the Public Prosecutor may file a complaint in the Court of Session with the Government's sanction.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe officer may file a private complaint before the Magistrate; if it concerns his official conduct, the State may prosecute through the Public Prosecutor before the Sessions Court. Departmental action is a separate route.
  4. ✅ ConclusionOnly on a complaint: by the officer before a Magistrate, or, for defamation about his official duties, by the Public Prosecutor (with sanction) directly before the Court of Session.
