🏏Defence and Trial

The plaintiff fires the first shot; the defendant can defend, hit back, or stay away. Then the court lines up the real points of fight and hears the match, with only three rain breaks.

🧠Mind map

1The big picture

The plaintiff has fired the first shot (the plaint). Now the defendant gets three choices: defend (written statement), hit back (set-off or counter-claim), or stay away (and face an ex parte decree). Whatever he picks, the court then lines up the real points of fight (issues), hears the evidence and decides.

Imagine a cricket match: the plaint and written statement are the two team sheets, the issues are the rules of the match, the hearing is the play, and adjournments are rain breaks. Too many breaks and the match never ends, so the Code now allows a party only three.

2Written statement

Exam question: Written statement (O.VIII r.1–5, 10)

  1. Meaning
  2. Time (r.1)
  3. Contents and denial
  4. Failure to file (r.10)
  5. Other rules
1

Meaning

O.VI r.1

The defendant's reply to the plaint: his pleading, admitting or denying each allegation and stating new facts in defence. It is a "pleading" under O.VI r.1, so it states material facts, not evidence.

🎨 Picture it

A cricket match: the plaint and the written statement are the two team sheets.

🗣️ Jargon buster

It is a pleading: it states material facts, not evidence (O.VI r.1).

2

Time (r.1)

Within 30 days from service of summons; the court may allow more time for reasons recorded, but not beyond 90 days. In commercial suits, the outer limit of 120 days is strict.

Case story Kailash v. Nanhku: in an election case, the winning candidate's written statement was filed 5 days late because his advocate's clerk failed to file it (the advocate's nephew had just died). The High Court refused to take it. The Supreme Court took it on record with ₹5,000 costs: the 90-day rule is directory, because "all the rules of procedure are the handmaid of justice", but extensions are an exception, for recorded reasons.
🧠 Memory trick

30 → 90 → 120: 30 days; at most 90; 120 in commercial suits.

🎬 Case link

Kailash v. Nanhku: 90 days is directory in ordinary suits; procedure is the handmaid of justice.

3

Contents and denial

r.1A: documents relied on are filed with it. r.2: new facts must be specially pleaded, e.g. fraud, limitation, release, payment, that the suit is not maintainable. r.3: denial must be specific; a general denial is not enough. r.4: evasive denial is no denial. r.5: every allegation not denied specifically or by necessary implication is taken as admitted (except against a person under disability).

🧠 Memory trick

Not denied = admitted (r.5). Evasive denial = admission (r.4).

🌍 Real life

The plaint says "the defendant signed the pro-note on 1 June". A reply saying only "all allegations are denied" is a general denial, which is not enough (r.3).

🧠 Memory trick

Plead these specially (r.2): Fraud · Limitation · Release · Payment · not maintainable.

4

Failure to file (r.10)

If the defendant does not file it within the time allowed, the court shall pronounce judgment against him, or make such order as it thinks fit, and a decree follows.

🎨 Picture it

No reply, no defence: the court may pronounce judgment against him (r.10).

5

Other rules

r.7: each defence on separate grounds stated separately. r.8: a new ground arising after the written statement may be raised. r.9: no further pleading after the written statement except with the court's leave (a "replication" by the plaintiff). r.6–6G: set-off and counter-claim (next answer).

Deny specifically, plead new facts expressly, and file in time: what is not denied is admitted.

📖 Word origin

A replication is the plaintiff's reply to the written statement; allowed only with the court's leave (r.9).

🔁 Recall: What is not denied is admitted (r.5); Kailash v. Nanhku: procedure is the handmaid of justice.

3Set-off and counter-claim

Exam question: Set-off and counter-claim: conditions, legal and equitable set-off, differences (O.VIII r.6, r.6A–6G)

  1. Set-off
  2. Conditions
  3. Equitable set-off
  4. Counter-claim
  5. Further rules
  6. Differences
1

Set-off

A cross-claim for money by the defendant against the plaintiff, which the defendant asks the court to deduct from the plaintiff's claim. It has the same effect as a plaint in a cross-suit, so the court passes one decree for the balance.

🧠 Memory trick

Shield and sword: set-off shields, counter-claim strikes.

🌍 Real life

A sues B on a bill for ₹500; B holds a judgment against A for ₹1,000: both definite money claims, so B can set off.

2

Conditions

(1) the suit is for recovery of money; (2) the sum is ascertained; (3) legally recoverable (not time-barred); (4) recoverable by the defendant (all defendants) from the plaintiff (all plaintiffs); (5) within the court's pecuniary limits; (6) both parties fill the same character in both claims.

Illustrations to r.6: A sues B on a bill of exchange for ₹500; B holds a judgment against A for ₹1,000: both are definite money claims, so B can set off. But if B claims unascertained damages for A's failure to insure his goods, he cannot. A and B together sue C for ₹1,000: C cannot set off a debt owed to him by A alone.
🧠 Memory trick

Six conditions, "My Aunt Rarely Sells Large Saris": Money suit · Ascertained sum · Recoverable · Same parties · within Limits · Same character.

3

Equitable set-off

Allowed by courts even for an unascertained sum, when both claims arise out of the same transaction and it would be unfair to make the defendant pay first and sue later. It is a matter of the court's discretion, not a right. Example: an agent sues for his commission; the principal claims damages for the agent's negligence in the same agency.

🎨 Picture it

Same transaction, same fairness: the court won't make him pay first and sue later.

🌍 Real life

An agent sues for his commission; the principal claims damages for the agent's negligence in the same agency: equitable set-off.

4

Counter-claim

The defendant may set up any right or claim against the plaintiff, whether for damages or not, on a cause of action accruing before or after the suit but before he files his defence (or before the time for it expires), within the court's pecuniary limits. It is treated as a plaint; the court passes one final judgment on both claims (r.6A(2)). The counter-claim need not be connected with the plaintiff's cause of action (Jag Mohan Chawla v. Dera Radha Swami Satsang, 1996).

🎨 Picture it

A counter-claim is a sword: any claim, money or not, treated as a plaint, even if it arose after the suit (but before the defence).

5

Further rules

r.6B: stated in the written statement. r.6C: the plaintiff may ask that it be excluded and tried as a separate suit (before issues are framed). r.6D: it survives even if the main suit is stayed, discontinued or dismissed. r.6E: if the plaintiff does not reply, judgment may be given on it. r.6F: if the defendant gets the balance, a decree is passed in his favour. r.6G: rules of written statement apply to the reply.

🧠 Memory trick

It survives even if the main suit is stayed, discontinued or dismissed (r.6D).

6

Differences

a shielda sword
Naturereduces the claiman independent cross-suit
Suit typeonly money suitsany suit
Claimascertained money (legal)any claim, money or otherwise
Causeexisting at the suit datemay arise even after the suit, before the defence

Set-off is a shield, counter-claim is a sword; both save a second suit.

🧠 Memory trick

The six rows, "Never Sue Clients Carelessly, Avoid Disputes": Nature · Suit type · Claim · Cause · Amount · if the suit is Dismissed.

🔁 Recall: Six conditions of legal set-off: money suit, ascertained, recoverable, same parties, within limits, same character.

4Ex parte decree

Exam question: Ex parte decree: consequences of non-appearance, remedies, setting aside (O.IX)

  1. Meaning
  2. Who is absent, what follows
  3. Setting aside (r.13)
  4. Remedies
  5. Sufficient cause
1

Meaning

O.IX r.6

A decree passed in the absence of the defendant, after he was duly served but did not appear (O.IX r.6(1)(a)). It is a valid decree, binding until set aside.

🎨 Picture it

A match where one team didn't turn up: valid and binding, until set aside.

2

Who is absent, what follows

Art. 122

r.3: neither party appears: suit may be dismissed; fresh suit or restoration allowed (r.4). r.6: only the plaintiff appears and summons was duly served: the court proceeds ex parte; if not duly served, fresh summons. r.7: the defendant may later appear and, on showing good cause, be heard from that stage. r.8: only the defendant appears: suit dismissed (unless he admits part). r.9: then no fresh suit on the same cause; plaintiff may apply to restore it for sufficient cause (Art. 122: 30 days).

🧠 Memory trick

Neither appears → dismissal possible (r.3). Only the plaintiff, and summons duly served → ex parte (r.6).

3

Setting aside (r.13)

Art. 123

The defendant applies to the court that passed it, within 30 days of the decree, or of his knowledge of it if summons was not duly served (Art. 123), and shows that (1) summons was not duly served, or (2) he was prevented by sufficient cause from appearing. Proviso: a mere irregularity in service is no ground if he knew of the date in time. Explanation: no r.13 application once an appeal against the decree has been decided on any ground other than withdrawal.

🔢 Number trick

30 days from the decree, or from knowledge if summons was not duly served (Art. 123).

🧠 Memory trick

Two grounds only: not served, or sufficient cause.

4

Remedies

O.IX r.13S.96(2)O.XLVII

(1) application under O.IX r.13; (2) appeal under S.96(2); (3) review (O.XLVII); (4) a suit to set it aside if obtained by fraud. The r.13 application and the appeal can be pursued side by side (Bhanu Kumar Jain v. Archana Kumar, 2005).

🧠 Memory trick

Four remedies: r.13 · appeal · review · fraud suit. The r.13 application and the appeal can run side by side.

5

Sufficient cause

S.5O.IX r.13

is read liberally: illness, accident, the advocate's mistake, wrong information about the date. The court may set aside on terms as to costs or payment into court (r.13). Notice to the opposite party is compulsory before setting aside (r.14). If the delay is beyond 30 days, file with an S.5 Limitation Act application Kailash v. Nanhku.

An ex parte decree is not the end: the defendant gets a second chance if he moves quickly and honestly.

Example: Balu fell ill and missed the hearing; an ex parte decree was passed. He applies under O.IX r.13 with a medical certificate within 30 days. The court sets aside the decree on his paying ₹2,000 costs to the plaintiff, and the suit is heard afresh.
🌍 Real life

Balu missed the hearing because he was ill; with a medical certificate, within 30 days, the decree is set aside on his paying ₹2,000 costs.

🗣️ Jargon buster

Notice to the other side is compulsory before setting aside (r.14).

🔁 Recall: Four remedies: r.13, appeal, review, fraud suit; r.13 and appeal can run side by side.

5Issues, stages, hearing

Exam question: Framing of issues · Stages of a suit · Hearing, affidavits and adjournments (O.XIV, O.XVIII, O.XIX, O.XVII)

🧠 Say it: “Plaint → Summons → Statement → Issues → Evidence → Judgment”

  1. PPlaintIssues
  2. SSummonsMaterials (r.3)
  3. SStatementPreliminary issue
  4. IIssuesStages of a suit
  5. EEvidenceHearing and affidavits
  6. JJudgmentAdjournments and costs
1

Issues

O.XIV r.1

Issues arise when a material proposition of fact or law is affirmed by one party and denied by the other. Material propositions are those the plaintiff must allege to show a right to sue, or the defendant must allege to make out his defence. Each forms a separate issue: of fact or of law. They are framed at the first hearing, after reading the pleadings and examining the parties.

🎨 Picture it

Issues are the rules of the match: every proposition one side affirms and the other denies.

2

Materials (r.3)

The court frames issues from: (a) allegations on oath by the parties or persons present for them; (b) allegations in the pleadings or answers to interrogatories; (c) contents of documents produced by either party. r.4: it may examine witnesses or documents first. r.5: it may amend, add or strike out issues before the decree.

🧠 Memory trick

Oath · Pleadings · Documents: the three sources of issues (r.3).

3

Preliminary issue

The court must decide all issues, but where an issue of law on jurisdiction or a legal bar (e.g. limitation, res judicata) can dispose of the suit, it may try that first.

Example: Ravi sues Somu for ₹50,000 on a pro-note. Somu says the signature is forged and the suit is time-barred. Issues: (1) Did Somu execute the pro-note? (2) Is the suit within limitation? (3) To what relief is Ravi entitled? Issue 2 can be tried first under r.2.
🌍 Real life

Ravi sues on a pro-note; Somu says forged and time-barred. The limitation issue can be tried first, because it can end the suit.

4

Stages of a suit

S.26O.VIIS.27O.VO.IX

(1) Plaint (S.26, O.VII) → (2) summons (S.27, O.V) → (3) appearance / ex parte (O.IX) → (4) written statement, set-off, counter-claim (O.VIII) → (5) examination of parties, ADR referral (O.X, S.89) → (6) discovery, admissions, documents (O.XI–XIII) → (7) framing of issues (O.XIV) → (8) witnesses summoned (O.XVI) → (9) hearing and evidence (O.XVIII) → (10) arguments → (11) judgment and decree (O.XX) → (12) appeal / execution.

🧠 Memory trick

Plaint → Summons → Statement → Issues → Evidence → Judgment.

5

Hearing and affidavits

O.XVIIIO.XIX

r.1: the plaintiff has the right to begin, unless the defendant admits the facts and argues only law or new facts. r.3A: a party who wishes to testify appears before his other witnesses. r.4: examination-in-chief by affidavit; cross-examination before the court or a commissioner. r.2: oral arguments, and written arguments with leave. Affidavits (O.XIX): r.1 the court may order any fact proved by affidavit; r.3 affidavits state only what the deponent knows, except in interlocutory applications (belief with grounds).

🧠 Memory trick

The plaintiff begins (r.1); evidence-in-chief by affidavit (r.4); a party testifies before his other witnesses (r.3A).

6

Adjournments and costs

O.XVIIO.IXS.35S.35AS.35B

r.1: granted only for sufficient cause, with costs, and not more than three times to a party during the hearing. r.2: if a party is absent on the adjourned date, the court proceeds under O.IX; where his evidence is largely recorded, it may decide on merits. r.3: if he fails to produce evidence, the court may decide forthwith. Costs: S.35 (costs follow the event), S.35A (compensatory costs up to ₹3,000 for false or vexatious claims), S.35B (costs for causing delay).

Issues focus the fight; the hearing decides it; the limit on adjournments keeps it moving.

Case story Salem Advocate Bar: lawyers in Salem challenged the 1999 and 2002 CPC amendments that limited adjournments and pushed ADR. The Supreme Court upheld them; in the 2005 follow-up it said the three-adjournment limit is the rule, to be crossed only for reasons beyond a party's control, with costs, and approved model rules for S.89 mediation.
🔢 Number trick

3 adjournments per party during the hearing, each with costs.

🎨 Picture it

Adjournments are rain breaks: too many and the match never ends.

🎬 Case link

Salem Advocate Bar: three adjournments is the rule, crossed only for reasons beyond a party's control.

🔁 Recall: Issues = the rules of the match; adjournments = rain breaks, max 3 (Salem Advocate Bar).

8Golden rules

  • ★ 1Every allegation not specifically denied is deemed admitted (O.VIII r.5). Evasive denial = admission.
  • ★ 2The 90-day limit for a written statement is directory in ordinary suits; mandatory (120 days) in commercial suits. Kailash v. Nanhku
  • ★ 3Set-off = an ascertained money claim, same parties, same character. Counter-claim = any claim, like a cross-suit.
  • ★ 4Against an ex parte decree, the defendant can apply under O.IX r.13 and appeal under S.96(2), side by side.
  • ★ 5Not more than three adjournments to a party during the hearing (O.XVII r.1). Salem Advocate Bar

9Part C: problem routes

Every problem takes the same four stops: issue → law → apply → conclusion.

1. Agent A sues his master M for arrears. M wants to set off damages for goods damaged by A's negligence. Can he?

  1. 🎯 IssueCan an unascertained claim for damages be set off against a money claim?
  2. 📜 LawO.VIII r.6: legal set-off needs an ascertained sum. Equitable set-off: allowed for an unascertained sum arising out of the same transaction. O.VIII r.6A: counter-claim for any claim, including damages.
  3. 🔍 ApplyM's damages are unascertained, so no legal set-off. But they arise from the same agency relationship as A's claim, so equitable set-off can be allowed; alternatively M can file a counter-claim.
  4. ✅ ConclusionYes, as an equitable set-off (same transaction), or by a counter-claim under O.VIII r.6A; not as a legal set-off.

2. Ram and Shyam sell wheat (₹10,000) to Sohan and Mohan. Sohan sells cloth (₹12,000) to Shyam and sues him. Shyam claims set-off of the wheat price. Will he succeed?

  1. 🎯 IssueCan a debt owed jointly to two persons be set off in a suit by one of the debtors against one of the creditors?
  2. 📜 LawO.VIII r.6: the sum must be recoverable by the defendant from the plaintiff, and both must fill the same character. Illustrations (f) and (g): a debt due from or to one person alone cannot be set off against a joint claim, and vice versa.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe wheat price is owed by Sohan and Mohan jointly to Ram and Shyam jointly. The cloth price is owed by Shyam alone to Sohan alone. The parties do not fill the same character in the two claims.
  4. ✅ ConclusionNo. Shyam cannot set off the wheat price; he must sue for it separately (with Ram, against Sohan and Mohan).

3. A sues B for a ₹50,000 debt. B files a counter-claim for ₹40,000 for A's breach of another contract. Can he?

  1. 🎯 IssueCan a counter-claim be based on a different transaction?
  2. 📜 LawO.VIII r.6A: the defendant may set up any right or claim against the plaintiff on a cause of action accruing before he files his defence, whether for damages or not, within pecuniary limits; it need not be connected with the plaintiff's claim.
  3. 🔍 ApplyB's claim is against the plaintiff A, arose before the defence, and is within limits. The court decides both together and passes a decree for the balance (r.6A(2), r.6F).
  4. ✅ ConclusionYes. B can file the counter-claim in his written statement; it is treated as a cross-suit.

4. The defendant did not appear and did not file a written statement. What are the consequences? What if he had filed it and then stayed away?

  1. 🎯 IssueConsequences of non-appearance, before and after a written statement.
  2. 📜 LawO.IX r.6: if summons duly served, the court proceeds ex parte. O.VIII r.10: failure to file a written statement: judgment may be pronounced. O.XVII r.2 and its Explanation: absence on an adjourned date after pleadings; where his evidence is largely recorded, the court may decide on merits.
  3. 🔍 ApplyWithout a written statement, the plaint allegations stand unrebutted and an ex parte decree follows. With a written statement, the court still proceeds ex parte, but must consider the pleadings on record and decide on the evidence.
  4. ✅ ConclusionHe faces an ex parte decree in both cases; his remedy is O.IX r.13 (within 30 days) or an appeal under S.96(2).

5. A suit was dismissed for default. What remedies does the plaintiff have?

  1. 🎯 IssueHow can a plaintiff revive a suit dismissed for his absence?
  2. 📜 LawO.IX r.8–9: if only the defendant appears, the suit is dismissed and a fresh suit is barred, but the plaintiff may apply to restore it for sufficient cause (Art. 122: 30 days). O.IX r.3–4: if both are absent, fresh suit (within limitation) or restoration. O.XLIII r.1(c): appeal against an order rejecting restoration.
  3. 🔍 ApplyHe applies to restore under r.9 with reasons (illness, wrong date, advocate's mistake); delay beyond 30 days with an S.5 application.
  4. ✅ ConclusionApply to restore under O.IX r.9 (or r.4); if refused, appeal under O.XLIII r.1(c). A fresh suit is barred after a r.8 dismissal.

6. The defendant files his written statement on the 100th day after service, citing his advocate's illness. Must the court reject it?

  1. 🎯 IssueIs the 90-day limit in O.VIII r.1 an absolute bar?
  2. 📜 LawO.VIII r.1 proviso: 30 days, extendable up to 90 days for recorded reasons. The limit is directory in ordinary civil suits Kailash v. Nanhku; in commercial suits, 120 days is mandatory (Commercial Courts Act, 2015; SCG Contracts v. K.S. Chamankar, 2019).
  3. 🔍 ApplyIn an ordinary suit, a genuine reason (advocate's illness, proved) can justify acceptance as an exception, with costs. In a commercial suit, the court has no power to accept it after 120 days.
  4. ✅ ConclusionIn an ordinary suit the court may accept it, for recorded reasons and on costs; in a commercial suit it must refuse after 120 days.

7. On the day fixed for evidence, the plaintiff asks for a fourth adjournment. What should the court do?

  1. 🎯 IssueCan a party get more than three adjournments?
  2. 📜 LawO.XVII r.1 proviso: no adjournment more than three times to a party during the hearing; r.2: proceed under O.IX if a party is absent; r.3: decide forthwith if he fails to produce evidence. S.35B: costs for delay Salem Advocate Bar.
  3. 🔍 ApplyA fourth adjournment is allowed only for reasons beyond the plaintiff's control (e.g. sudden hospitalisation), with costs; otherwise the court closes his evidence and proceeds.
  4. ✅ ConclusionRefuse it unless the cause is beyond his control; if granted, impose costs under S.35B; otherwise proceed under O.XVII r.2 or r.3.

8. After issues are framed, the defendant realises no issue was framed on limitation, which he had pleaded. Can he ask for it?

  1. 🎯 IssueCan issues be added after they are framed?
  2. 📜 LawO.XIV r.5: the court may amend or frame additional issues at any time before passing a decree. r.2(2): an issue of law on a legal bar (such as limitation) may be tried first. S.3 Lim. Act: the court must examine limitation even if not raised.
  3. 🔍 ApplyLimitation was pleaded in the written statement, so it is a material proposition in dispute and should have been an issue.
  4. ✅ ConclusionYes. The court can frame an additional issue under O.XIV r.5, and may try it first as a preliminary issue under r.2.
