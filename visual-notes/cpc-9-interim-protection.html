🧊Interim Protection

A suit takes years; interim orders freeze the battlefield until the war is decided.

🧠Mind map

1The big picture

A suit can take years. Meanwhile the defendant may sell the land, pull down the house, empty the shop or run away. If the court did nothing until the final decree, the plaintiff might win a decree for something that no longer exists. Interim orders freeze the battlefield until the war is decided.

The court's tools, in order of strength: caveat (please hear me first), status quo (nobody move), temporary injunction (stop that act), receiver (the court takes charge of the property), commission (the court sends someone to look and measure), attachment before judgment (the property is locked till the decree), and arrest before judgment (for a defendant about to flee). Behind all of them stands S.151: the court's inherent power to do justice.

2Temporary injunction, caveat

Exam question: Temporary injunctions and interlocutory orders (O.XXXIX) · Status quo · Caveat (S.148A)

  1. Meaning
  2. When (r.1–2)
  3. Three tests
  4. Ex parte injunction
  5. Other rules
  6. Status quo
  7. Caveat
1

Meaning

S.94(c)O.XXXIXS.38

An injunction is a court order restraining a party from doing an act (or, if mandatory, directing him to do one). A temporary injunction lasts until the suit is decided or until further orders (S.94(c), O.XXXIX); a permanent injunction is granted by the decree (S.38, Specific Relief Act).

📖 Word origin

Injunction, from Latin injungere, "to order": stop doing that (or, if mandatory, do this).

🧠 Memory trick

Temporary = till the suit ends (O.XXXIX). Permanent = by the decree (S.38, Specific Relief Act).

2

When (r.1–2)

Where it is proved by affidavit or otherwise that (a) the property in dispute is in danger of being wasted, damaged or alienated by a party, or wrongfully sold in execution; (b) the defendant threatens to remove or dispose of his property to defraud creditors; (c) the defendant threatens to dispossess the plaintiff or otherwise cause injury to him in relation to the property in dispute; and (r.2) to restrain a breach of contract or other injury.

🧠 Memory trick

Waste · Defraud · Dispossess: property in danger, assets being moved to cheat creditors, a threat to throw the plaintiff out.

3

Three tests

(1) Prima facie case: a bona fide, substantial question that needs a trial; (2) balance of convenience: more harm to the applicant if refused than to the other side if granted; (3) irreparable injury: harm that money cannot adequately compensate (Dalpat Kumar v. Prahlad Singh, 1992).

Case story Mahadeo Shelke: tenants evicted by a final order of the Pune corporation (affirmed up to the Supreme Court) filed a fresh suit for an injunction against being dispossessed. The Supreme Court refused it: possession after a final eviction order is unlawful, so there is no prima facie case, and neither balance of convenience nor irreparable injury favoured them; it even directed damages against them if the suit failed.
🧠 Memory trick

PBI: Prima facie case · Balance of convenience · Irreparable injury. All three must favour the applicant.

🎨 Picture it

Three traffic lights, all green, or no injunction.

🎬 Case link

Mahadeo Shelke: no right, no injunction; tenants under a final eviction order had no prima facie case.

4

Ex parte injunction

Normally notice is given to the other side first. Without notice, only where delay would defeat the object; the court records reasons, and the applicant must at once send the other side copies of the application, affidavit and plaint and file an affidavit of compliance. r.3A: the court must try to decide the application within 30 days. Factors: serious mischief, conduct, delay, good faith, limited duration (Morgan Stanley Mutual Fund v. Kartick Das, 1994).

🧠 Memory trick

Without notice only if delay would defeat the purpose; reasons recorded; copies sent to the other side at once.

🔢 Number trick

30 days: the court tries to decide the application after an ex parte injunction (r.3A).

5

Other rules

O.XLIII r.1

r.2A: disobedience: attachment of property and civil prison up to 3 months. r.4: the order can be discharged, varied or set aside. r.5: an injunction against a corporation binds its officers. r.6–10: interlocutory orders: sale of perishable goods, detention, preservation, inspection, deposit of money. An order on a temporary injunction is appealable (O.XLIII r.1(r)).

🔢 Number trick

Disobey it: attachment + civil prison up to 3 months (r.2A).

🗣️ Jargon buster

An injunction against a company binds its officers (r.5).

6

Status quo

O.XXXIXS.151

An order directing the parties to maintain the existing position (possession, construction, title) as on a date, until further orders. It is passed under O.XXXIX or S.151 when the court needs time to examine the matter.

🎨 Picture it

A freeze frame: nobody moves (possession, construction, title) until further orders.

7

Caveat

S.148A

A person who expects an application to be made against him in a suit may lodge a caveat, so that no order is passed without hearing him. He must serve notice of it on the expected applicant by registered post; the applicant must then serve the caveator with copies. A caveat is valid for 90 days.

Interim relief preserves the subject matter so that the final decree is not an empty paper.

📖 Word origin

Caveat is Latin for "let him beware": "hear me before you pass any order".

🔢 Number trick

A caveat lives 90 days (S.148A(5)).

🔁 Recall: Mahadeo Shelke: no right, no injunction. A caveat lasts 90 days.

3Receiver and commissions

Exam question: Appointment of receiver (O.XL) and commissions (S.75, O.XXVI)

🧠 Say it: “Receiver protects, Commissioner reports”

  1. RReceiverReceiver (O.XL)
  2. pprotectsWhen used
  3. CCommissionerCommissions (S.75, O.XXVI)
  4. rreportsExample
1

Receiver (O.XL)

O.XL r.1

Where it appears just and convenient, the court may appoint a receiver of any property, before or after the decree; remove any person from its possession or custody; commit it to the receiver; and give him powers to bring and defend suits, realise, manage, protect, preserve and improve the property, collect rents and profits, and execute documents. He is an officer of the court; his possession is the court's possession.

🎨 Picture it

A receiver is the court's caretaker: he holds and manages the property for the court.

2

When used

Property in danger of waste; partnership or family business in dispute; rents and profits likely to be lost; mortgage suits. r.3: he gives security, submits accounts, pays the amounts due, and is personally liable for loss caused by wilful default or gross negligence. r.2: the court fixes his remuneration.

🌍 Real life

Partners fighting over a business, or rents likely to be lost: the court puts a receiver in charge.

🗣️ Jargon buster

He gives security, renders accounts, and is personally liable for loss from wilful default or gross negligence.

3

Commissions (S.75, O.XXVI)

S.75O.XXVIS.133

The court may issue a commission: (a) to examine a witness (sick, exempt under S.133, outside its jurisdiction: r.1–8); (b) for local investigation (e.g. measuring land, finding encroachment: r.9); (c) to examine or adjust accounts (r.11); (d) to make a partition (r.13); (e) for a scientific investigation (r.10A), a ministerial act (r.10B) or sale of movables (r.10C). The commissioner's report and evidence form part of the record.

A receiver protects the property; a commissioner brings the facts to the court.

🎨 Picture it

A commissioner is the court's eyes and measuring tape: examines witnesses, inspects land, checks accounts, makes partitions.

4

Example

O.XXVI r.9
Example: B says A encroached 150 sq. yards while building. The court appoints an Advocate-Commissioner with a surveyor under O.XXVI r.9 to measure the plots and report, and meanwhile orders status quo on the construction.
🧠 Memory trick

Receiver protects; commissioner reports.

🔁 Recall: A receiver holds the property for the court; a commissioner looks and measures for the court.

4Arrest, attachment before judgment

Exam question: Arrest and attachment before judgment (O.XXXVIII) · Compensation for wrongful orders (S.95)

  1. Arrest before judgment (r.1)
  2. Attachment before judgment (r.5)
  3. Case story
  4. Further rules and S.95
1

Arrest before judgment (r.1)

S.16(a)

In any suit other than a suit for immovable property under S.16(a)–(d), if the defendant, with intent to delay the plaintiff, avoid the court or obstruct execution, (i) has absconded or left the court's limits, (ii) is about to abscond, or (iii) has disposed of or removed property; or is about to leave India in circumstances likely to obstruct execution, the court may issue a warrant to bring him before it to show cause why he should not furnish security for his appearance. r.2–4: if he fails to give security, he may be committed to civil prison until the suit is decided (at most six months).

🧠 Memory trick

No arrest in land suits (suits under S.16(a)–(d)).

🗣️ Jargon buster

Arrest needs intent to delay, avoid the court or obstruct execution.

2

Attachment before judgment (r.5)

If the defendant, with intent to obstruct or delay the execution of any decree, is about to dispose of the whole or part of his property, or remove it from the court's limits, the court may direct him to furnish security or produce the property, or show cause; it may also order conditional attachment. The plaintiff must specify the property and its value. Attachment without following r.5(1) is void (r.5(4)).

🎨 Picture it

Lock the property till the decree, but only if he is about to dispose of or remove it to defeat the decree.

🧠 Memory trick

It is not a way to turn an unsecured debt into a secured one.

3

Case story

  • Raman Tech. & Process Engg. Co. v. Solanki Traders: Set aside: the power is drastic; it is not meant to secure an unsecured debt; a prima facie case and intent to defeat the decree must be shown.
Case story Raman Tech: a supplier sued at the Medchal court for ₹99,200 and sought security or attachment because the defendants were moving machinery. The High Court ordered security; the Supreme Court set it aside: attachment before judgment is a drastic power, not a device to convert an unsecured debt into a secured one; the plaintiff must show a prima facie case and an intent to defeat the decree, and shifting machinery to new premises is not enough.
🎬 Case link

Raman Tech: a suit is not a mortgage; prove intent to defeat the decree.

4

Further rules and S.95

S.95

r.6: attachment if no cause is shown and no security given. r.8: claims of third parties to the attached property are decided as in execution. r.9: attachment withdrawn when security is given or the suit is dismissed. r.10: it does not affect the rights of strangers or prior encumbrances. r.11: attached property need not be re-attached in execution. r.12: no attachment of agricultural produce in an agriculturist's possession. S.95: if arrest, attachment or injunction was obtained on insufficient grounds, the court may award compensation up to ₹50,000 to the defendant.

These are strong weapons against a defendant planning to cheat the decree, so courts use them only on clear proof.

🔢 Number trick

Arrest, attachment or injunction on insufficient grounds: compensation up to ₹50,000 (S.95).

🔁 Recall: Raman Tech: a suit is not a mortgage; prove intent to defeat the decree.

5Inherent powers, restitution

Exam question: Inherent powers of civil courts (S.151) · Restitution (S.144)

  1. S.151
  2. Limits
  3. Examples
  4. Restitution (S.144)
1

S.151

S.151

"Nothing in this Code shall be deemed to limit or otherwise affect the inherent power of the court to make such orders as may be necessary for the ends of justice or to prevent abuse of the process of the court." It is not a new power given by the Code; it is inherent in every court.

🎨 Picture it

S.151 is the court's spare key: for the gaps the Code does not cover.

🧠 Memory trick

Two purposes: ends of justice · prevent abuse of process.

2

Limits

O.XXXIX

(1) It cannot be used where the Code expressly provides a remedy, or against its provisions; (2) it is procedural and cannot create substantive rights (Padam Sen v. State of U.P., 1961); (3) used with care, for reasons recorded. But the Code's provisions are not exhaustive: a court can grant an injunction in a case not covered by O.XXXIX (Manohar Lal Chopra v. Seth Hiralal, 1962).

🧠 Memory trick

Never against an express provision, and it creates no new substantive rights (Padam Sen, 1961).

3

Examples

S.10S.148S.152S.153

Recalling an order obtained by fraud; consolidating suits; staying a suit outside S.10; restoring a proceeding; setting aside an order passed without notice; correcting the court's own mistake. Companion sections: S.148 (enlarge time, up to 30 days in total), S.152 (clerical or arithmetical errors), S.153 (amendment of proceedings).

🔢 Number trick

S.148: enlarge time (up to 30 days in total). S.152: correct clerical or arithmetical errors.

4

Restitution (S.144)

S.144S.144(2)S.151

Where a decree or order is varied or reversed in appeal, revision or other proceeding, the court of first instance shall, on application, restore the parties to the position they would have occupied but for that decree, including refund with interest, damages and mesne profits. S.144(2): no separate suit for this. Principle: actus curiae neminem gravabit (an act of the court shall prejudice no one).

S.151 is the court's reserve power for justice where the Code is silent.

🎨 Picture it

Restitution is the rewind button: if a decree is reversed, put the parties back where they were.

🌍 Real life

Money paid under a decree that is later reversed in appeal comes back, with interest.

🔁 Recall: S.151 fills gaps, never overrides the Code; S.144 puts parties back where they were.

8Golden rules

  • ★ 1Three tests for a temporary injunction: prima facie case, balance of convenience, irreparable injury; all three must favour the applicant. Mahadeo Shelke
  • ★ 2Attachment before judgment is drastic: not to turn an unsecured debt into a secured one; prove intent to defeat the decree. Raman Tech
  • ★ 3No arrest before judgment in suits for immovable property (O.XXXVIII r.1 excludes S.16(a)–(d) suits).
  • ★ 4A caveat lasts 90 days; the caveator must be heard before an order is passed (S.148A).
  • ★ 5S.151 fills gaps for the ends of justice, but cannot be used against an express provision of the Code.

9Part C: problem routes

Every problem takes the same four stops: issue → law → apply → conclusion.

1. Smt. Sudha sues Mr. Kondal for a perpetual injunction to stop him and his men entering her house. Pending the suit she wants a temporary injunction. Discuss.

  1. 🎯 IssueIs Sudha entitled to a temporary injunction pending the suit?
  2. 📜 LawO.XXXIX r.1(c) and r.2: injunction where the defendant threatens to dispossess or injure the plaintiff in relation to the property. Three tests: prima facie case, balance of convenience, irreparable injury Mahadeo Shelke. r.3: ex parte only if delay defeats the object.
  3. 🔍 ApplyIf she shows title or settled possession (documents, tax receipts), the threat of entry, that her family will suffer more than Kondal if refused, and that forcible entry cannot be compensated in money, all three tests are met.
  4. ✅ ConclusionYes, she can get a temporary injunction under O.XXXIX r.1–2 restraining Kondal till the suit is decided; ex parte if the threat is immediate, with r.3 compliance.

2. P sues D for dissolution of partnership and accounts. D is hurriedly shifting the firm's goods and assets. What can P do?

  1. 🎯 IssueWhat interim protection is available against a defendant removing assets?
  2. 📜 LawO.XXXVIII r.5: security or attachment before judgment if D, with intent to obstruct execution, is about to remove property Raman Tech. O.XXXIX r.1(a)–(b): injunction against wasting or removing property. O.XL r.1: receiver.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe assets belong to the partnership, which is the subject matter of the suit; D's hurried removal shows intent to defeat P's share.
  4. ✅ ConclusionP can seek an injunction restraining removal, the appointment of a receiver for the firm's assets, and security or attachment before judgment under O.XXXVIII r.5.

3. X wants the defendant arrested before judgment in a suit for recovery of immovable property. Can he?

  1. 🎯 IssueIs arrest before judgment available in a suit for immovable property?
  2. 📜 LawO.XXXVIII r.1: arrest before judgment lies in any suit "other than a suit of the nature referred to in section 16, clauses (a) to (d)", i.e. suits for recovery, partition, mortgage or other rights in immovable property.
  3. 🔍 ApplyX's suit is for recovery of immovable property, which falls in S.16(a). The land cannot run away.
  4. ✅ ConclusionNo. Arrest before judgment is barred in such suits; X can seek a temporary injunction or a receiver instead.

4. A agreed to sell his property to B and took a good advance. Prices rose; A refuses to register unless B pays the current market price. B's remedy?

  1. 🎯 IssueWhat remedy and immediate protection does the buyer have?
  2. 📜 LawS.10, Specific Relief Act: suit for specific performance. O.XXXIX r.1(a): injunction against alienating the property pending the suit. S.52 TPA: lis pendens binds a transferee pending the suit.
  3. 🔍 ApplyB has paid an advance and is ready and willing to pay the agreed price; A is trying to change the bargain and may sell to someone else.
  4. ✅ ConclusionB should sue for specific performance and immediately seek a temporary injunction restraining A from alienating the property; any sale during the suit is subject to its result (lis pendens).

5. A, while building on his plot, encroaches 150 sq. yards of B's adjacent plot and refuses to stop. B's remedy and immediate step?

  1. 🎯 IssueHow to stop ongoing construction on an encroached portion.
  2. 📜 LawSuit for declaration, mandatory injunction (removal) and permanent injunction (S.38–39 SRA). O.XXXIX r.1(c), r.2: temporary injunction. O.XXVI r.9: commission for local investigation.
  3. 🔍 ApplyConstruction, once completed, creates hardship and more litigation, so it must be stopped at once; the boundary dispute needs measurement.
  4. ✅ ConclusionB should sue for mandatory and permanent injunction, and immediately seek an ex parte temporary injunction (or status quo) stopping construction, with an Advocate-Commissioner to measure the plots.

6. B lodged a caveat. The plaintiff obtained an ex parte temporary injunction without notice to B. Valid?

  1. 🎯 IssueCan an order be passed without hearing a caveator?
  2. 📜 LawS.148A: where a caveat has been lodged, the applicant must serve notice of his application on the caveator, and the court must give notice to the caveator before passing an order; a caveat is valid for 90 days.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe caveat was in force, and B was not heard.
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe order is liable to be set aside; B can apply to vacate it (O.XXXIX r.4) and it should be heard afresh with notice to him.

7. The defendant disobeys a temporary injunction and continues construction. What can the plaintiff do?

  1. 🎯 IssueWhat are the consequences of disobeying an injunction?
  2. 📜 LawO.XXXIX r.2A: the court may order attachment of the property of the person guilty of disobedience and detention in civil prison for up to 3 months; the attached property may be sold if the disobedience continues for a year. The court can also order the construction to be demolished to restore the position.
  3. 🔍 ApplyConstruction in breach of an injunction is a clear violation.
  4. ✅ ConclusionApply under O.XXXIX r.2A for attachment and civil prison, and for restoration of the original position.
