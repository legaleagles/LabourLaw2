✍️Endorsement and Dishonour of Cheques (S.138)

An endorsement passes the baton; a bounced cheque starts a clock: 30 days to notice, 15 days to pay, a month to complain.

🧠Mind map

1The big picture

An endorsement is a signature that passes the baton. Sita writes "Pay Gopal or order" and signs on the back of her cheque: Gopal now owns it, and Sita has promised to pay him if the bank does not. Each signature on the back adds one more person who will answer if the cheque fails.

And when a cheque bounces, the law does more than allow a civil suit. Since 1989, S.138 makes it a crime, because a cheque is a promise people rely on. But the law is fair to the drawer too: he first gets a written notice and 15 days to pay. Only then can the payee go to court.

1

Meaning (S.15)

S.15

When the maker or holder signs on the back or face of an instrument (or on a slip attached, an allonge) for the purpose of negotiation, he is said to indorse it; he is the indorser, and the person to whom it is indorsed is the indorsee.

📖 Word origin

Endorse, from Latin in dorsum, "on the back": the signature usually goes on the back of the instrument.

🎨 Picture it

A relay baton: each signature passes it on and adds one more person who answers if it fails.

🗣️ Jargon buster

An allonge is a slip attached to the instrument when there is no room left for signatures.

2

Essentials

S.46S.48

(1) Signed by the holder; (2) on the instrument or an allonge; (3) for the whole amount; (4) followed by delivery (S.46, S.48); (5) signature matches the name of the payee as written.

🧠 Memory trick

Signed · On it · Whole amount · Delivered · Name matches.

3

Blank and full

S.16S.49

Blank (general): only the signature; the instrument becomes payable to bearer. Full (special): "Pay Gopal or order" + signature; payable to the named indorsee or his order. A holder may convert a blank indorsement into full by writing a direction above the signature (S.49).

Example: a cheque "Pay Sita or order". Sita signs only "Sita" on the back and hands it to Gopal: that is a blank indorsement and Gopal can pass it on by mere delivery. If Sita writes "Pay Gopal or order, Sita", it is a full indorsement and Gopal must indorse it to pass it on.
🧠 Memory trick

Blank = just a signature → payable to bearer. Full = "Pay Gopal or order" → payable to the named person.

4

Restrictive

S.50

Words that stop further negotiation or limit the indorsee's authority: "Pay Gopal only", "Pay Gopal for my use", "Pay Gopal or order for collection".

🧠 Memory trick

Spot the words: "only", "for my use", "for collection" = restrictive.

5

Sans recours and facultative

S.52

The indorser may exclude his own liability: "Pay Gopal or order, sans recours (without recourse to me)". A facultative indorsement gives up a right of the indorser, e.g. "notice of dishonour waived".

📖 Word origin

Sans recours is French for "without recourse": "don't blame me if it bounces". Facultative: gives up a right, e.g. "notice of dishonour waived".

6

Conditional

S.52

The indorser may make his liability, or the indorsee's right to receive the amount, depend on a specified event, even one that may never happen: "Pay Gopal on his marriage".

🌍 Real life

"Pay Gopal on his marriage", or "Pay C if he returns from London": valid, but C gets nothing until the event happens.

7

Partial

S.56

An indorsement transferring only part of the amount is not valid for negotiation. If part has been paid, that may be noted and the instrument negotiated for the balance.

🎨 Picture it

You cannot hand over half a baton: "Pay B ₹500" on a ₹1,000 note is not a valid negotiation (S.56).

8

Effect

S.50S.35S.93S.122

The property in the instrument passes to the indorsee, with the right to negotiate it further (S.50); the indorser becomes liable to every later holder if it is dishonoured, provided notice of dishonour is given (S.35, S.93). Estoppel: an indorser cannot deny the genuineness of earlier signatures to a holder in due course (S.122).

🧠 Memory trick

Property passes to the indorsee; the indorser is liable to every later holder, if notice of dishonour is given.

🔁 Recall: Picture a relay race: the baton (instrument) passes with a signature. Blank = anyone can run; full = named runner; restrictive = last runner; sans recours = "don't blame me"; conditional = "only if"; partial = you cannot hand over half a baton.

3Cheque bounce (S.138)

Exam question: Dishonour of cheques: S.138 and the penal provisions of the NI Act

1

Object

S.138

Chapter XVII (S.138–148), added in 1989, gives credibility to cheques by making dishonour an offence. It is basically a civil wrong with a criminal sanction Meters and Instruments.

🔢 Number trick

Chapter XVII (S.138–148), added in 1989.

🎬 Case link

Meters and Instruments: a civil wrong with a criminal sanction; compensation can close the case.

2

Ingredients (S.138)

S.138

(1) A cheque drawn by a person on an account maintained by him; (2) for the discharge of a legally enforceable debt or liability (not a gift or donation); (3) presented within its validity (three months); (4) returned unpaid because the amount is insufficient or exceeds the arrangement. Courts include "account closed", "payment stopped" and "signature differs" when caused by the drawer Laxmi Dyechem.

🧠 Memory trick

Own account · legal Debt · within 3 months · Returned · Notice · 15 days unpaid.

🎬 Case link

Laxmi Dyechem: "account closed", "stop payment", "signature differs" can also be S.138 offences.

3

Notice and time limits

The payee must send a written demand within 30 days of receiving the bank's return memo; the drawer has 15 days from receipt of the notice to pay. The offence is complete only when he fails to pay within those 15 days.

🧠 Memory trick

The 30 · 15 · 30 clock: notice within 30 days, 15 days to pay, then a month to complain.

4

Punishment

S.140S.139

Imprisonment up to two years, or fine up to twice the cheque amount, or both. It is not a defence that the drawer had no reason to believe the cheque would bounce (S.140).

Case story Rangappa: the drawer admitted his signature but said the cheque was a lost blank cheque that had been misused. The Supreme Court held that S.139 presumes not only that the cheque was received for a debt, but that a legally enforceable debt existed; the drawer must rebut it with probable evidence, and his bare story failed.
🧠 Memory trick

"2 and 2": up to two years, or a fine up to twice the amount, or both.

🗣️ Jargon buster

"I didn't think it would bounce" is no defence (S.140).

5

Presumption (S.139)

Once the cheque is admitted to be signed, S.139 presumes that the holder received it for the discharge of a legally enforceable debt; the drawer must rebut it by probable evidence, not a bare story Rangappa.

🎨 Picture it

A signed cheque is a presumed IOU: the burden shifts to the drawer.

🎬 Case link

Rangappa: the presumption covers the existence of a legally enforceable debt.

6

Companies (S.141)

S.141

The company and every person in charge of and responsible for its business at the time are liable, unless they prove lack of knowledge or due diligence. Nominee directors of government or financial institutions are excluded. The company itself must be made an accused for directors to be prosecuted (Aneeta Hada v. Godfather Travels, 2012).

🧠 Memory trick

The company and everyone in charge of its business is liable, unless they show no knowledge or due diligence. Nominee directors are excluded.

7

Procedure (S.142–147)

S.142S.142(2)S.143S.144S.145

Cognizance only on a written complaint by the payee or holder in due course, within one month of the cause of action (delay may be condoned); triable by a Metropolitan Magistrate or Judicial Magistrate of the first class; jurisdiction where the payee's bank branch is (S.142(2), 2015). Summary trial (S.143); summons by post or courier (S.144); evidence on affidavit (S.145); the bank's return memo is proof of dishonour (S.146); the offence is compoundable (S.147).

🔢 Number trick

Complaint within 1 month · before a Magistrate of the first class · in the court where the payee's bank branch is · summary trial (S.143).

🔁 Recall: Remember the 30-15-30 clock: 30 days to send notice, 15 days for the drawer to pay, then a month to file the complaint. Then "2 and 2": two years or twice the amount.

4NI Amendment, 2018

Exam question: The Negotiable Instruments (Amendment) Act, 2018: salient features

🧠 Say it: “Why Is Daddy Running Cash?”

  1. WWhyWhy the amendment
  2. IIsInterim compensation (S.143A)
  3. DDaddyDeposit in appeal (S.148)
  4. RRunningRefund and recovery
  5. CCashCourts on the amendment
1

Why the amendment

Cheque cases were clogging criminal courts and drawers used delay tactics (appeals, adjournments) while the payee waited years. The amendment, in force from 1 September 2018, makes the drawer pay something up front.

🔢 Number trick

In force from 1 September 2018.

🎨 Picture it

Delay no longer pays: the drawer must pay something up front.

2

Interim compensation (S.143A)

S.143A

The trial court may order the drawer to pay interim compensation of up to 20% of the cheque amount: in a summary or summons trial when he pleads not guilty, and in other cases when the charge is framed. It must be paid within 60 days (extendable by 30 days).

🧠 Memory trick

Pay 20 to fight: up to 20% interim compensation at trial (S.143A).

🔢 Number trick

Paid within 60 days, extendable by 30.

3

Deposit in appeal (S.148)

S.148

When a convicted drawer appeals, the appellate court may order him to deposit at least 20% of the fine or compensation awarded by the trial court (in addition to any interim compensation), within 60 days (extendable by 30 days). The court may release it to the complainant during the appeal.

🧠 Memory trick

Pay 20 to appeal: at least 20% of the fine or compensation deposited (S.148).

4

Refund and recovery

If the drawer is acquitted, the complainant must refund the interim compensation with interest at the RBI bank rate, within 60 days. Interim compensation is recoverable like a fine; any final fine or compensation is reduced by it.

🧠 Memory trick

Win, and get it back with interest at the RBI bank rate, within 60 days.

5

Courts on the amendment

S.143AS.148

S.143A is prospective: it does not apply to complaints filed before 1 September 2018 (G.J. Raja v. Tejraj Surana, 2019). S.148 applies to appeals filed after that date even if the complaint was older (Surinder Singh Deswal v. Virender Gandhi, 2019); deposit is the rule, but the appellate court may exempt in an exceptional case for recorded reasons (Jamboo Bhandari v. M.P. State Industrial Development Corporation, 2023).

🗣️ Jargon buster

S.143A is prospective: not for complaints before 1 September 2018 (G.J. Raja, 2019). S.148 applies to appeals filed after that date (Surinder Singh Deswal).

🔁 Recall: "Pay 20 to fight, pay 20 to appeal": 20% interim at trial (S.143A) and 20% deposit in appeal (S.148); get it back with interest if you win.

7Golden rules

  • ★ 1Indorsement + delivery transfers an order instrument; a blank indorsement makes it payable to bearer (S.16, S.49).
  • ★ 2An indorsement for part of the amount is not valid for negotiation (S.56).
  • ★ 3Once a cheque is admitted to be signed, the court presumes a legally enforceable debt; the drawer must disprove it. Rangappa
  • ★ 4"Account closed", "stop payment" and "signature differs" can also lead to S.138 liability. Laxmi Dyechem
  • ★ 5S.138 is mainly about compensating the payee; courts encourage quick settlement. Meters and Instruments

8Part C: problem routes

Every problem takes the same four stops: issue → law → apply → conclusion.

1. An indorsement on a cheque says "Pay C if he returns from London". C has not returned but wants to claim the amount. Can he succeed?

  1. 🎯 IssueWhat is the effect of a conditional indorsement?
  2. 📜 LawS.52: an indorser may, by express words, make his liability or the indorsee's right to receive the amount depend on a specified event, although it may never happen.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe indorser made C's right to receive conditional on his return from London. The event has not happened.
  4. ✅ ConclusionNo. Until C returns from London, he cannot claim on the indorsement or hold the indorser liable. (A paying banker that pays in due course is still discharged; but C has no enforceable right.)

2. The holder of a promissory note for ₹1,000 writes on it "Pay B ₹500" and indorses it. Is this valid?

  1. 🎯 IssueCan an instrument be indorsed for part of its amount?
  2. 📜 LawS.56: a writing that transfers only part of the amount due is not valid for negotiation; but where part has been paid, that may be noted and the instrument negotiated for the balance.
  3. 🔍 ApplyNothing has been paid on the note. The indorsement tries to split ₹1,000 into ₹500.
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe partial indorsement is invalid for negotiation: B does not become a holder and cannot sue on the note. The note itself remains valid in the original holder's hands. If ₹500 had been paid, he could note it and indorse the balance.

3. A is the holder of a bill payable to the order of B. Explain the steps of an endorsement in blank.

  1. 🎯 IssueHow is a bill negotiated by blank indorsement?
  2. 📜 LawS.15, S.16: indorsement in blank = only the signature. S.46, S.48: delivery completes negotiation. S.49: conversion into full indorsement. S.35: liability of indorser.
  3. 🔍 Apply(1) The payee/holder B signs his name on the back exactly as written; (2) delivers the bill to the transferee; (3) the bill now becomes payable to bearer and can pass by mere delivery; (4) any holder may convert it to a full indorsement by writing "Pay X or order" above B's signature.
  4. ✅ ConclusionA blank indorsement is just the holder's signature plus delivery; it turns the bill into a bearer instrument, and the indorser is liable to every later holder.

4. B accepted a bill drawn in Paris. The indorsee, with the drawer's consent, altered the place of drawing from Paris to Rome. Is B liable?

  1. 🎯 IssueIs changing the place of drawing a material alteration, and what is its effect?
  2. 📜 LawS.87: a material alteration makes the instrument void against any party who was a party at the time of the alteration and did not consent. Changing the place of drawing alters the law that governs the bill, so it is material (Koch v. Dicks, 1933).
  3. 🔍 ApplyB was a party (acceptor) and did not consent. The drawer and the indorsee consented.
  4. ✅ ConclusionB is not liable on the bill. The drawer and the indorsee, who agreed to the change, remain liable.

5. X accepts a bill for the accommodation of A (drawer). A transfers it to B, and B to C, without consideration. C transfers it to D for value. D transfers it to E without consideration. X dishonours it. Rights of A, B, C, D and E?

  1. 🎯 IssueWho can sue whom on an accommodation bill passed with and without consideration?
  2. 📜 LawS.43: a party who gets an instrument without consideration has no rights against the party who gave it to him; but if any party transferred it to a holder for consideration, that holder and every later holder deriving title from him may recover from the transferor for consideration and any prior party. An accommodation party is liable to a holder for value.
  3. 🔍 ApplyA, B and C gave nothing, so none of them can sue the person he took from, and A cannot sue X (the accommodating party). D paid value to C: D can sue C, B, A and X. E paid nothing to D, so he cannot sue D, but he derives title from D (a holder for value), so he can sue C, B, A and X.
  4. ✅ ConclusionA, B and C have no claims. D can recover from C, B, A and X. E can recover from C, B, A and X, but not from D.

6. A signs a cheque "A, Agent". It is dishonoured. Is A personally liable?

  1. 🎯 IssueDoes adding the word "agent" protect the signer?
  2. 📜 LawS.28: an agent who signs without indicating that he signs as agent and that he does not intend to incur personal liability is personally liable. S.27: to bind the principal, the agent must sign "for and on behalf of" him.
  3. 🔍 Apply"A, Agent" does not name any principal; the word "agent" merely describes A. It does not show that he signed only for someone else.
  4. ✅ ConclusionYes, A is personally liable. He should have signed "for and on behalf of P, A, Agent".

7. Amit gives a private school a cheque for ₹40,000 for fees. It returns unpaid: "Account closed". What can the school do?

  1. 🎯 IssueIs "account closed" a dishonour under S.138, and what are the steps?
  2. 📜 LawS.138 covers a cheque returned because the account has been closed (NEPC Micon Ltd v. Magma Leasing Ltd, 1999) Laxmi Dyechem. S.142: complaint within one month; jurisdiction where the payee's bank is. S.143A: interim compensation. Civil remedy: summary suit under O.XXXVII CPC.
  3. 🔍 ApplyFees were a legally enforceable liability. The return memo is proof of dishonour (S.146).
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe school should send Amit a written demand within 30 days of the return memo; if he does not pay within 15 days of receiving it, file a complaint under S.138 within the next month, ask for interim compensation, and may also file a summary civil suit.

8. A gives B a cheque on 24 August 2023. On 21 September the bank receives an order nisi (garnishee order) attaching A's account. The same day B presents the cheque; it is returned because of the garnishee order. Was the bank right? Remedies of B?

  1. 🎯 IssueCan a bank dishonour a cheque because of a garnishee order, and is A liable?
  2. 📜 LawO.XXI r.46 CPC: from service of the order nisi, the bank must not pay out of the attached account. S.31 NI Act: duty to pay exists only when funds are "properly applicable". S.30: drawer's civil liability on dishonour. S.138 applies where the cheque returns because the drawer's funds are insufficient.
  3. 🔍 ApplyOnce the order nisi was served, A's balance was frozen by law; the bank had to refuse payment. B has no claim against the bank. A remains liable to B on the cheque and the debt. Whether S.138 applies depends on why the account could not pay: if the attachment arose from A's own default and left no funds for B, a court may treat it as insufficiency; if A had arranged funds and the freeze was beyond his control, the criminal charge is weak.
  4. ✅ ConclusionThe bank was right to dishonour; B cannot sue the bank. B can recover from A by a civil suit (summary suit), and can send a S.138 notice; if A does not pay within 15 days, B may file a complaint, which will turn on whether A had arranged funds.
