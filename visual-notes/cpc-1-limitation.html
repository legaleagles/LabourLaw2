⏰Law of Limitation

1The big picture

Every right to go to court comes with a clock. Ravi lends Somu ₹50,000 on a promissory note payable on demand, dated 1 June 2022. The Limitation Act, 1963 gives Ravi 3 years to sue (Art. 35). If he sleeps till July 2025, the court must send him away. Not because Somu is honest, but because Ravi was late.

Two old maxims explain why: Vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt (the law helps those who are awake, not those who sleep on their rights) and Interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium (it is in the public interest that litigation must end). Evidence fades, witnesses die, and people need certainty.

1

Meaning

Limitation is the fixed time within which a suit, appeal or application must be filed. The Limitation Act, 1963 (in force from 1 January 1964) lists these periods in its Schedule of 137 Articles. It is a statute of repose: after a time, disputes must rest.

💬 In simple words
  • Limitation: a time limit to go to court; miss it and the court will not hear you.
  • Statute of repose: a law that lets old disputes rest, so people are not dragged to court forever.
🎨 Picture it

Every right comes with a clock. The Schedule (137 Articles) sets each clock.

🔢 Number trick

The Act of 1963, in force from 1 January 1964: 32 sections, 5 Parts, 137 Articles.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe neighbour's loan
  1. 📍 What happened

    A Kukatpally shop owner lends ₹50,000 to a neighbour on 10 January 2021. No document, no repayment. He keeps reminding him by phone, and finally decides to sue in March 2024.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    A suit for money lent must be filed within 3 years from the date of the loan (Art. 19). Phone reminders do not stop or restart the clock.

  3. ✅ Result

    The last day was 10 January 2024. Filed in March 2024, the suit is time-barred and will be dismissed.

2

Object

(1) Certainty and peace: interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium. (2) Diligence: vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt. (3) Fair trial: old claims are hard to prove or disprove. (4) Stale claims should not be used to harass.

📖 Word origin

Vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt: the law helps the awake, not the sleeping. Interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium: the State wants litigation to end.

🧠 Memory trick

Four objects: Peace · Diligence · Fair trial · No harassment by stale claims.

🏘️Real-life exampleA claim from 2003
  1. 📍 What happened

    In 2025 a man sues the family of his late uncle, saying the uncle owed him money from 2003. The uncle is dead, the witnesses have moved away, and no papers survive.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Limitation exists for certainty and a fair trial: old claims are hard to prove or disprove, and stale claims should not be used to harass.

  3. ✅ Result

    The heirs are not forced to defend a 22-year-old claim they cannot check. The law says: disputes must rest after a fixed time.

3

S.3 bar

S.3S.4S.24

Every suit, appeal or application filed after the prescribed period shall be dismissed, although limitation has not been set up as a defence. So the court must check limitation on its own. S.3 is subject to S.4 to S.24.

🎨 Picture it

The referee blows the whistle even if no player complains: the court checks limitation on its own, even if the defendant never raises it (S.3).

🏘️Real-life exampleThe defendant who said nothing
  1. 📍 What happened

    A trader is sued for a loan given 4 years earlier. He files a written statement on the merits but never mentions limitation.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.3: a late suit shall be dismissed although limitation has not been set up as a defence.

  3. ✅ Result

    The judge notices the dates and dismisses the suit on his own. Limitation is the court's duty, not just the defendant's argument.

💬 In simple words
  • Condonation: the court forgiving a delay and hearing the case anyway, if you show a good reason.
  • Legal disability: being a minor or of unsound mind, so the law does not expect you to protect your own rights yet.
🧠 Memory trick

Five Parts: Preliminary · Limitation · Computation · Ownership by possession · Miscellaneous.

🏘️Real-life exampleHow an advocate uses the Act
  1. 📍 What happened

    A client walks in with a 2022 pro-note and asks, "Can I still sue?"

  2. ⚖️ The law

    The advocate checks in order: the Schedule (which Article and how many years), then Part II (any disability?), then Part III (any exclusion, acknowledgment or part payment that changes the dates?).

  3. ✅ Result

    Three questions answer almost every limitation problem: which Article · when did time start · did anything extend or restart it?

5

General principles

S.3S.27S.9S.5S.6

(write at least 6): (1) the court applies limitation on its own (S.3); (2) it bars the remedy, not the right; (3) exception: for property the right itself is extinguished (S.27); (4) once time begins to run, it does not stop (S.9); (5) courts cannot extend time on sympathy, only through the Act (S.5 is for appeals and applications, never suits); (6) sufficient cause is read liberally Katiji; (7) legal disability postpones the start, capped at 3 years after it ends (S.6, S.8); (8) acknowledgment and part payment before expiry restart the clock (S.18, S.19).

💬 In simple words
  • Sufficient cause: a genuine reason you could not help, like illness, not carelessness.
  • Acknowledgment: a signed written admission that you still owe the money.
🧠 Memory trick

Start your list with these four: court acts on its own · remedy not right · property is the exception · the clock never stops.

6

Remedy, not right

S.25(3)

After the period ends, the debt is not dead; only the court route is closed. So: if the debtor pays, he cannot ask for it back; the creditor can adjust money of the debtor he lawfully holds; and a written promise to pay a time-barred debt is a valid contract (S.25(3) Contract Act).

Case story PNB v. Surendra Prasad Sinha: a guarantor's loan became time-barred. The bank still adjusted his fixed deposit against it, and he filed a criminal case against the bank officers. The Supreme Court quashed it: the door to court closed, but the debt stayed, so the bank could use money it already held.
🌍 Real life

A friend repays your loan five years late. He cannot later demand the money back: the debt never died, only the right to sue for it.

🎬 Case link

PNB v. Surendra Prasad Sinha: the bank could still adjust a time-barred debt against the guarantor's deposit.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe promise made after the deadline
  1. 📍 What happened

    A borrower took ₹1 lakh in 2018 and never repaid. In 2025, long after the 3 years ended, he signs a letter: "I promise to pay you the ₹1 lakh I borrowed."

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Limitation bars the remedy, not the right. A written, signed promise to pay a time-barred debt is a valid contract under S.25(3) of the Contract Act.

  3. ✅ Result

    The lender can now sue, but on the 2025 promise, not on the old loan.

7

Conclusion

The Act balances two values: it shuts stale claims for certainty, but it saves honest litigants through disability, exclusion, acknowledgment and condonation.

🎨 Picture it

A strict clock with honest exceptions: disability, exclusion, acknowledgment, condonation.

🔁 Recall: The clock: it starts (Schedule), it runs (S.9), it can pause (S.4–15), restart (S.18–19), and ring (S.3). After the ring, the debt lives but the court door is shut.

3Legal disability 🔥🔥 Important

📝 How it is asked“Legal disability and its effect on the computation of limitation (S.6–9)”

  • short answer (Part A): 2015 · 2019 · May 2023 · Aug 2025
  • essay (Part B): 2018 · Nov 2020

6 times in past papers · Asked often: be ready for both a short note and an essay.

  1. Meaning
  2. S.6
  3. S.7
  4. S.8 cap
  5. S.9
  6. Example
1

Meaning

The law does not expect a child or a person of unsound mind to protect his own rights, so it postpones the clock. Only three legal disabilities: minor (including a child in the womb), insane, idiot. Illness, imprisonment, poverty or ignorance are not legal disabilities.

🧠 Memory trick

MII: Minor · Insane · Idiot. Jail, illness, poverty and ignorance are not legal disabilities.

🌍 Real life

A man in jail for the whole limitation period gets no extension for being in jail.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe 15-year-old landlord
  1. 📍 What happened

    A boy of 15 inherits a small shop in Secunderabad. The tenant stops paying rent. His elder brother, who could have helped, was in jail that whole time.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Only minority, insanity and idiocy are legal disabilities. Jail, illness, poverty and ignorance are not.

  3. ✅ Result

    The boy's clock is postponed until he turns 18. The brother gets no extension for being in jail.

2

S.6

S.6

If the person is under disability when the clock should start, he may sue within the same period counted from when the disability ends.

S.6 in detail: (1) two disabilities at once, or a new one before the first ends: counted from when both end; (2) disability lasts till death: his legal representative gets the same period from the death; (3) if the legal representative is himself disabled, the same rules apply to him; (4) if he dies after the disability ends but within his extended time, his legal representative gets the remaining benefit.

🎨 Picture it

For a disabled person the clock starts when the disability ends, not when the right arose.

🏘️Real-life exampleWhen the disability lasts till death
  1. 📍 What happened

    A man has been of unsound mind since before a loan he gave fell due. He never recovers and dies in 2024. His son is his legal representative.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.6: if the disability lasts till death, the legal representative gets the same period, counted from the death.

  3. ✅ Result

    For a loan (3 years), the son can sue until 2027, counted from his father's death.

3

S.7

S.7

If one of several persons is disabled but the others can give a valid discharge without him, time runs against all; if not, it runs against none until one can.

🌍 Real life

A debt is owed to a firm of three: one partner is insane, one a minor, but the third can give a valid discharge alone, so time runs against all three.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe joint family with a minor
  1. 📍 What happened

    A buyer owes a Hindu joint family ₹6 lakh for land. The youngest son is a minor; the father, as karta, is an adult who can give a valid receipt for the whole family.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.7: if one joint creditor can give a valid discharge without the disabled person, time runs against all of them.

  3. ✅ Result

    The minor's age does not help. The family must sue within the normal 3 years.

4

S.8 cap

S.8S.6S.7

S.6 and S.7 can never extend the period beyond 3 years after the disability ends (or death); no extension at all for pre-emption suits.

🔢 Number trick

The cap: never more than 3 years after the disability ends. And no extension at all for pre-emption suits.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe girl dispossessed at 4
  1. 📍 What happened

    A girl's land is taken from her when she is 4. Possession suits have 12 years (Art. 65), so the ordinary period ends when she is 16, still a minor.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.6 postpones the start to majority, but S.8 caps the extension at 3 years after the disability ends.

  3. ✅ Result

    Not 12 years from 18 (age 30), but till age 21: 3 years after she turns 18.

5

S.9

S.9

Once the clock has started, a later disability does not stop it. (Exception: if the debtor becomes administrator of the creditor's estate, the clock pauses during that administration.)

Disability postpones the start, never pauses a running clock, and is always capped at 3 years after it ends.

🧠 Memory trick

Once started, never stops: a disability that arrives later changes nothing.

🏘️Real-life exampleInsanity that came later
  1. 📍 What happened

    A loan fell due when the lender was healthy. A year later he became of unsound mind.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.9: once time begins to run, a later disability does not stop it.

  3. ✅ Result

    His family must still sue within the original 3 years. Disability helps only if it exists when the clock should start.

6

Example

Art. 65S.6S.8
Worked example: a right to possession (12 years, Art. 65) accrues when Arun is 10. The ordinary period ends at 22. S.6 would give 12 years from 18, but S.8 caps the extension at 3 years after majority (age 21). Since the ordinary period (22) is later, Arun can sue up to 22. Had the right accrued at age 5, the ordinary period would end at 17; then the cap gives him till 21.
🧠 Memory trick

Always compare two dates, the ordinary deadline and 3 years after the disability ends, and use the later one.

🔁 Recall: Disability postpones the start (S.6), may not help co-owners (S.7), is capped at 3 years (S.8), and never stops a running clock (S.9).

4Condonation (S.5) 🔥🔥 Important

  • essay (Part B): Aug 2021

6 times in past papers · Asked often: be ready for both a short note and an essay.

  1. Meaning
  2. Scope

1

Meaning

S.5

Under S.5 the court may admit an appeal or application after the time limit if the person shows sufficient cause for not filing in time.

🎨 Picture it

A second chance, but only for appeals and applications.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe accident during the appeal period
  1. 📍 What happened

    A man loses a civil suit in the Junior Civil Judge's court. His appeal to the District Court has 30 days (Art. 116). On day 5 he is hit by a bus and stays in hospital till day 40. He appeals on day 45 with his discharge summary.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.5: an appeal can be admitted late if the person shows sufficient cause for not filing in time.

  3. ✅ Result

    Hospitalisation is a genuine reason beyond his control, so the court condones the delay and hears the appeal.

2

Scope

Not for suits, and not for applications under Order XXI CPC (execution). Explanation: being misled by an order, practice or judgment of the High Court can be sufficient cause.

💬 In simple words
  • Execution: actually enforcing a decree, so the winner gets what the court gave him.
🧠 Memory trick

S.5 = Second chance for appeals, never for suits. Not for execution applications under Order XXI either.

🗣️ Jargon buster

Being misled by an order, practice or judgment of the High Court can itself be sufficient cause.

🏘️Real-life exampleTrying S.5 for a suit
  1. 📍 What happened

    A businessman forgets to file a money recovery suit and is 3 months late. He asks the court to "condone the delay" because he was busy.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.5 applies only to appeals and applications, never to suits, and not to execution applications under Order XXI.

  3. ✅ Result

    The court has no power to condone. The suit is dismissed as time-barred, whatever the reason.

3

Sufficient cause

= a genuine reason beyond the person's control, without negligence or bad faith: illness, death in the family, the advocate's fault, being misled by the court. Courts read it liberally, so that substantial justice beats technicality Katiji.

🌍 Real life

Illness, a death in the family, or the advocate's fault can be sufficient cause; deliberate delay cannot.

🎬 Case link

Katiji: substantial justice beats technicality; the State is treated like any other litigant.

🏘️Real-life exampleA death in the family
  1. 📍 What happened

    An appellant's father dies in the middle of the 30-day appeal period. Busy with the funeral and the family rites, he files 12 days late, attaching the death certificate.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Sufficient cause = a genuine reason without negligence or bad faith, read liberally so that justice beats technicality (Katiji).

  3. ✅ Result

    The reason is honest and proved, so the delay is condoned.

4

Case stories

  • Collector, Land Acquisition, Anantnag v. Mst. Katiji: Condone it. "Sufficient cause" must be read liberally; substantial justice beats technicality, and the State is treated like any other litigant.
  • N. Balakrishnan v. M. Krishnamurthy: Condoned. "Length of delay is no matter, acceptability of the explanation is the only criterion." The other side was paid ₹10,000.
Case story Katiji: the State filed its appeal against a huge compensation award just 4 days late. The High Court threw it out. The Supreme Court condoned the delay and laid down the golden rules: a litigant gains nothing by delay; refusing condonation can kill a good case at the door; no pedantic "explain every day"; substantial justice wins over technicality; no presumption of bad faith; and the State is treated like any other litigant.
Case story N. Balakrishnan: a litigant was 883 days late because his advocate took ₹2,000, did nothing and quietly left practice. The Supreme Court condoned the delay: "length of delay is no matter, acceptability of the explanation is the only criterion". But it made him pay ₹10,000 to the other side.
🔢 Number trick

4 days (Katiji) and 883 days (Balakrishnan): both condoned. Count the honesty of the reason, not the days.

🎬 Case link

N. Balakrishnan: "length of delay is no matter, acceptability of the explanation is the only criterion".

5

Limits

Liberal does not mean automatic. Delay caused by deliberate inaction, a casual approach or a tactic to harass is not condoned. Courts may put the applicant on costs to compensate the other side. Once the first court accepts the explanation, a higher court interferes only if that discretion was perverse.

Count the honesty of the reason, not the number of days.

🎨 Picture it

Liberal is not automatic: casual or tactical delay is not condoned, and the court may put the applicant on costs.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe company that waited on purpose
  1. 📍 What happened

    A company loses a case and waits 200 days to see if the other side will settle. Then it appeals, saying only "the file was under process".

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Liberal is not automatic: delay from deliberate inaction, a casual approach or a tactic is not condoned.

  3. ✅ Result

    The vague excuse fails and the appeal is dismissed. Even when delay is condoned, the court can order costs.

🔁 Recall: Katiji (4 days, State) and Balakrishnan (883 days, advocate's fault): count the honesty of the reason, not the days.

1

S.18 acknowledgment

S.18

A written admission of liability, made before the period expires, in writing and signed by the party (or his agent), starts a fresh period from the date it was signed.

No promise to pay is needed, and the exact amount need not be stated (Explanation (a)); but it must admit a present, subsisting liability. Oral admissions do not count, and oral evidence of what the writing "meant" is not allowed.

Case story Shapoor Mazda: a debtor wrote to his mortgagee that "a payment of 10,000 will stop the sale". He never said "I owe you". The Supreme Court still held it a valid acknowledgment, because the letter admitted the existing debt. Admit the debt, restart the clock.
🎨 Picture it

A signed "I owe you" restarts the clock from the day it is signed.

🎬 Case link

Shapoor Mazda: "a payment of 10,000 will stop the sale" admitted the debt, so it was a valid acknowledgment.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe letter that restarted the clock
  1. 📍 What happened

    A farmer bought seeds on credit from a dealer in March 2022 and never paid. In January 2025, before the 3 years ran out, he wrote and signed: "I know I still owe you for the 2022 seeds; I will clear it after harvest."

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.18: a written, signed admission of liability made before the period expires starts a fresh period. No promise to pay is needed.

  3. ✅ Result

    A brand-new 3 years runs from January 2025, so the dealer can sue till January 2028.

2

Requirements

Three requirements: the acknowledgment must be made before the period expires, in writing, and signed by the party or his agent (S.18). No promise to pay is needed and the amount need not be stated, but it must admit a present, subsisting liability. Oral admissions do not count.

🧠 Memory trick

B-W-S: Before expiry · in Writing · Signed.

🏘️Real-life exampleThree tries, one success
  1. 📍 What happened

    (1) The debtor admits the debt aloud in front of two witnesses. (2) He writes "I owe you" but does not sign. (3) He signs a clear admission, but only after the 3 years have ended.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.18 needs all three: before expiry · in writing · signed.

  3. ✅ Result

    None of the three works: oral, unsigned and too late. Only a signed writing made in time restarts the clock.

3

S.19 part payment

S.19

Payment of part of a debt (or of interest on it) by the person liable or his agent, before the period expires, starts a fresh period from the date of payment, provided the payment is shown in the payer's handwriting or in a writing signed by him. A cash payment with no written trace does not help.

🌍 Real life

Pay part of the debt by cheque or with a signed receipt: a cash payment with no written trace does not restart the clock.

🏘️Real-life exampleCheque or cash?
  1. 📍 What happened

    A debtor pays ₹5,000 towards an old loan before the period ends. In one version he pays by signed cheque; in the other he hands over cash and nobody writes anything.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.19: part payment starts a fresh period only if it appears in the payer's handwriting or a writing signed by him.

  3. ✅ Result

    The cheque restarts the clock from the date of payment. The unrecorded cash does not.

4

Example

Art. 35
Example: pro-note on demand dated 1 Jun 2022 (3 years, Art. 35). On 1 Jan 2025 Somu signs a letter admitting the debt: Ravi can now sue till 1 Jan 2028. If Somu instead pays ₹5,000 by cheque on 1 Mar 2025, the new period runs till 1 Mar 2028.
🔢 Number trick

Note of 1 Jun 2022 → letter admitting the debt on 1 Jan 2025 → a fresh 3 years, to 1 Jan 2028.

5

After expiry

S.18S.19S.25(3)

Neither S.18 nor S.19 can revive a debt once the period has ended. Only a fresh written, signed promise to pay under S.25(3) of the Contract Act can, and then the suit is on that new promise.

Both give a completely new period, not just extra days, but only if they come before the clock runs out.

🗣️ Jargon buster

After expiry, only a fresh written, signed promise to pay (S.25(3), Contract Act) helps, and the suit is on that new promise.

🏘️Real-life examplePaying after the deadline
  1. 📍 What happened

    A 2018 loan became time-barred in 2021. In 2025 the borrower pays ₹2,000 towards it.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.18 and S.19 work only before the period expires. They cannot revive a dead remedy.

  3. ✅ Result

    The rest of the debt stays time-barred. But he cannot ask for the ₹2,000 back: the debt itself never died.

🔁 Recall: Shapoor Mazda: "a payment of 10,000 will stop the sale" = admit the debt, restart the clock.

6Computing time 🔥🔥 Important

📝 How it is asked“Computation and extension of limitation: S.4, S.9, S.12–15 (with S.5, 6, 18, 19)”

1

S.4 court closed

S.4

The period is strict, but the Act itself gives fixed ways to exclude, extend or restart time. Courts cannot add others.

If the period ends on a day the court is closed, file on the day it reopens. A court closed for part of its normal working hours counts as closed.

🌍 Real life

The last day falls in the summer vacation: file on the day the court reopens (S.4).

🏘️Real-life exampleThe Dussehra holiday
  1. 📍 What happened

    The 30-day appeal period ends on a day the court is closed for the Dussehra holidays.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.4: if the period ends when the court is closed, file on the day it reopens.

  3. ✅ Result

    Filed on the reopening day, the appeal is in time.

2

S.12 first day / copy time

S.12

Exclude the first day. For an appeal, revision or review, also exclude the day of judgment and the time taken to get the certified copy.

💬 In simple words
  • Review: asking the same court to correct an obvious mistake in its own decision.
  • Revision: the High Court checking whether a lower court acted beyond or without its power.
🧠 Memory trick

Day one doesn't count; copy-time doesn't count (S.12).

🏘️Real-life exampleWaiting for the certified copy
  1. 📍 What happened

    A decree is passed on 1 May. The losing party applies for a certified copy on 3 May; it is ready on 20 May.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.12: exclude the day of judgment, and for an appeal the time needed to get the certified copy.

  3. ✅ Result

    The 17 days spent waiting for the copy are not counted against the appeal period.

3

S.13–15 exclusions

S.13S.14S.15S.80

Exclude time spent on an application to sue or appeal as an indigent person. S.14. Exclude time spent in good faith, with due diligence, in a court without jurisdiction. S.15. Exclude time under a stay or injunction, and the period of a required notice (e.g. 2 months under S.80 CPC).

💬 In simple words
  • Jurisdiction: the power of a court to hear a particular case.
  • Indigent person: a person too poor to pay the court fee, allowed to sue without paying it first.
🧠 Memory trick

Pauper · Wrong court · Stay: time spent suing as an indigent (S.13), in a court without jurisdiction in good faith (S.14), or under a stay or required notice (S.15) is excluded.

🔢 Number trick

The 2-month notice under S.80 CPC is excluded (S.15).

🏘️Real-life exampleThe wrong court
  1. 📍 What happened

    A builder sues in a Hyderabad court in good faith, but after 2 years the plaint is returned because the court has no territorial jurisdiction.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.14: time spent in good faith, with due diligence, in a court without jurisdiction is excluded. S.15 also excludes the 2-month notice to the Government under S.80 CPC.

  3. ✅ Result

    When he refiles in the right court, the 2 wasted years are excluded.

4

S.9 continuous running

S.9S.4S.12

"When time begins to run, it will continue to run": no later disability or inability stops it. The clock pauses only where the Act itself says so (S.4, S.12–15).

🧠 Memory trick

A non-stop clock: it pauses only where the Act says so.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe creditor who went abroad
  1. 📍 What happened

    A lender's 3-year period starts running. A year later he takes a job in Dubai for 2 years.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.9: when time begins to run, it continues to run. Only the Act's own exclusions pause it.

  3. ✅ Result

    Going abroad is not an exclusion. He must sue within the original 3 years.

5

Five ways to save time

S.4S.5S.6S.12S.18

(for "When can the period be extended?"): (1) court closed (S.4); (2) sufficient cause for appeals and applications (S.5) Katiji N. Balakrishnan; (3) legal disability (S.6–8); (4) exclusions (S.12–15); (5) fresh start by acknowledgment (S.18) Shapoor Mazda or part payment (S.19).

The clock is strict, but it bends for honest causes the Act itself recognises.

Example: a District Court decree is passed on 1 May. The 90-day appeal period to the High Court (Art. 116) is counted from the next day, plus the days taken to get the certified copy. If the last day falls during summer vacation, the appeal filed on reopening day is in time (S.4).
💬 In simple words
  • Decree: the court's final, formal decision on who wins the suit.
🧠 Memory trick

The five C's: Closed court · sufficient Cause · Child and other disabilities · Cut-outs (exclusions) · Confession (acknowledgment and part payment).

🔁 Recall: Five ways to save time: court closed, sufficient cause, disability, exclusions, fresh start.

7Adverse possession, easement 🔥🔥 Important

📝 How it is asked“How and when a person's right to property is extinguished · Adverse possession · Easement by prescription (S.25, S.27, Art. 64–65)”

  • essay (Part B): Feb 2024 (easement)
  • problem (Part C): 2017 · 2018 · Sep 2024 · Feb 2025

5 times in past papers · Asked often: be ready for both a short note and an essay.

  1. S.27 extinction
  2. Art. 64–65
  3. Adverse possession
  4. Sword and shield
  5. S.25 easement
1

S.27 extinction

S.27

Usually limitation only bars the remedy. Part IV is the exception: time can transfer ownership itself.

When the period for a suit for possession of any property ends, the owner's right to that property is extinguished, not just his remedy.

🎨 Picture it

Here time transfers ownership: the owner's right itself dies (S.27), not just his remedy.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe plot the owner forgot
  1. 📍 What happened

    An owner settles abroad and never visits his plot on the city's outskirts. From 2008 a neighbour openly fences it, builds a shed and grows vegetables there. The owner sues in 2024.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.27: when the period to sue for possession ends, the owner's right itself is extinguished, not just his remedy.

  3. ✅ Result

    If the neighbour proves 12 years of open, hostile possession, the owner's title is lost.

2

Art. 64–65

Art. 65Art. 64

(possession based on title): 12 years from when the defendant's possession becomes adverse to the plaintiff. Art. 64 (possession based on earlier possession, after being dispossessed): 12 years from dispossession.

🧠 Memory trick

64 = possession lost (12 years from dispossession). 65 = title (12 years from when possession became adverse).

🏘️Real-life exampleTitle or possession?
  1. 📍 What happened

    Two people go to court. One shows a sale deed and says, "The land is mine." The other says, "I lived here for years until I was thrown out last year."

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Art. 65 (suit on title): 12 years from when the other's possession became adverse. Art. 64 (suit on earlier possession): 12 years from dispossession.

  3. ✅ Result

    The first sues under Art. 65, the second under Art. 64, without needing to prove title.

3

Adverse possession

It must be nec vi, nec clam, nec precario: not by force, not secret, not by permission. It must also be continuous, peaceful, open and hostile to the true owner's title for 12 years. A tenant, licensee or relative allowed to stay is never in adverse possession. A trespasser's long possession alone is not enough.

Case story Ravinder Kaur Grewal: earlier the Supreme Court had said adverse possession is only a shield. In 2019 a three-judge bench overruled that: after 12 years the possessor gets the title that was extinguished, and can defend with it and also sue on it, as a sword and a shield.
💬 In simple words
  • Adverse possession: getting ownership by openly occupying someone else's land, against their will, for 12 years.
📖 Word origin

Nec vi, nec clam, nec precario: not by force, not in secret, not by permission.

🌍 Real life

A tenant, licensee or relative allowed to stay can never claim adverse possession, however long he stays.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe watchman of 25 years
  1. 📍 What happened

    A watchman has lived in a hut on a factory's land for 25 years with the owner's permission. He now claims the land by adverse possession.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    Possession must be nec vi, nec clam, nec precario: not by force, not secret, not by permission, and hostile to the owner.

  3. ✅ Result

    His stay was by permission, so no number of years makes it adverse. The claim fails.

4

Sword and shield

After 12 years the owner's right is extinguished (S.27) and the possessor gets title, which he can use both as a shield (to defend) and as a sword (to sue) Ravinder Kaur Grewal.

🎬 Case link

Ravinder Kaur Grewal (2019): adverse possession is both a sword and a shield.

🏘️Real-life exampleUsing the title to sue
  1. 📍 What happened

    A family has openly held a field as owners for 14 years. The original owner's heirs now try to throw them out by force.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    After 12 years the possessor gets the extinguished title and can use it as a shield and as a sword (Ravinder Kaur Grewal).

  3. ✅ Result

    The family can sue for an injunction on its own title, not just defend.

5

S.25 easement

S.25S.27

Light and air to a building, a way, a watercourse or any other easement, enjoyed peaceably, openly, as of right and without interruption for 20 years, becomes absolute and indefeasible. Against Government property the period is 30 years. The 20 years must end within 2 years before the suit in which it is claimed.

S.27 and S.25 protect long, open, settled enjoyment, and penalise an owner who sleeps for 12 years.

💬 In simple words
  • Easement: a right to use someone else's land in a limited way, like a path or light and air.
🔢 Number trick

20 years for an easement; 30 against Government property; the 20 years must end within 2 years before the suit.

🌍 Real life

A window that has received light and air openly and without interruption for 20 years: the right to that light becomes absolute.

🏘️Real-life exampleThe path to the field
  1. 📍 What happened

    For 22 years a farmer has walked openly through his neighbour's land to reach his own field, as of right, without anyone stopping him. The new owner of the land puts up a gate.

  2. ⚖️ The law

    S.25: an easement enjoyed peaceably, openly, as of right and without interruption for 20 years becomes absolute (30 years against Government property).

  3. ✅ Result

    The farmer has a right of way, and the neighbour cannot block it.

🔁 Recall: Possession must be nec vi, nec clam, nec precario (no force, no secrecy, no permission); after 12 years it becomes Ravinder Kaur's sword and shield.

10Golden rules

  • ★ 1Limitation bars the remedy, not the right. The debt still exists; only the court route is closed. PNB v. Surendra Prasad Sinha
  • ★ 2S.5 is never available for a suit. It saves only appeals and applications (and not O.XXI execution applications).
  • ★ 3Only three legal disabilities: minor, insane, idiot ("MII"). Imprisonment, illness and poverty are not legal disabilities.
  • ★ 4Once time starts running, it never stops (S.9).
  • ★ 5Acknowledgment counts only if it is before expiry, in writing, and signed. Shapoor Mazda

11Part C: problem routes

Every problem takes the same four stops: issue → law → apply → conclusion.

1. The plaintiff was in jail for the whole limitation period. Can he get the delay condoned after release?

  1. 🎯 IssueIs imprisonment a legal disability, and can delay in filing a suit be condoned?
  2. 📜 LawS.6 lists only minor, insane, idiot. S.5 applies to appeals and applications only, not suits. S.3: a late suit must be dismissed.
  3. 🔍 ApplyImprisonment is not among the three disabilities, so S.6 does not postpone the clock. And S.5 cannot rescue a suit at all.
  4. ✅ ConclusionNo. The suit is barred and will be dismissed under S.3. (S.14 would help only if he had meanwhile pursued the claim in good faith in a wrong court.)

2. A owes a debt to a firm of B, C and D. B is insane, C is a minor, D can give a discharge alone. When does time run?

  1. 🎯 IssueDoes the disability of B and C stop time running against the firm's claim?
  2. 📜 LawS.7: if one of several persons jointly entitled is disabled, but a discharge can be given without his concurrence, time runs against all.
  3. 🔍 ApplyD can validly receive payment and give a discharge without B and C.
  4. ✅ ConclusionTime runs against all three (B, C and D) from the date the debt became due. B's insanity and C's minority give no extension.

3. A right to sue for immovable property accrued to a minor. After majority he stayed silent for 3 years. Can he sue now?

  1. 🎯 IssueHow long does a minor get after attaining majority?
  2. 📜 LawS.6(1): same period counted from cessation of disability. S.8: but never more than 3 years after it ends. Art. 65: 12 years for possession based on title.
  3. 🔍 ApplyHe gets whichever is later: (a) the ordinary 12 years counted from when the right accrued, or (b) 3 years after majority. He has used up the 3 years after majority.
  4. ✅ ConclusionHe can sue only if the ordinary 12 years from accrual have not yet run out. If they have, the suit is barred: S.8 allows no more than 3 years after majority. Show both outcomes with ages for full marks.

4. The limitation period ends on a day when the court is closed (summer holidays / a holiday). When can he file?

  1. 🎯 IssueIs the appeal or suit barred if filed after the holiday?
  2. 📜 LawS.4: if the period expires on a day the court is closed, it may be filed on the day the court reopens. S.12(2): for an appeal, exclude the day of judgment and the time for the certified copy.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe last day fell on a closed day, so the law moves the last day to the reopening day.
  4. ✅ ConclusionFiling on the first day the court reopens is within time. No condonation is needed.

5. Mr. Z lent money to Mr. G on a promissory note in 2020. Unpaid. Can Z file a recovery suit in 2025?

  1. 🎯 IssueIs a 2025 suit on a 2020 pro-note within limitation?
  2. 📜 LawArt. 35: pro-note payable on demand, 3 years from the date of the note. S.18 / S.19: acknowledgment or part payment before expiry gives a fresh period. S.3: a late suit is dismissed.
  3. 🔍 Apply3 years from 2020 would normally end in 2023. Bonus point most students miss: because of Covid, the Supreme Court excluded 15 Mar 2020 to 28 Feb 2022 from all limitation periods (In Re: Cognizance for Extension of Limitation, order of 10 Jan 2022). So the 3 years effectively ran from 1 Mar 2022 and ended by about the end of February 2025 at the latest. After that the suit is late, unless G acknowledged the debt in writing (S.18) or paid part, shown in his writing (S.19), before the period ended.
  4. ✅ ConclusionA suit filed in early 2025 may just be in time because of the Covid exclusion; one filed later in 2025 is time-barred and must be dismissed under S.3. If G acknowledged in writing or paid part before expiry, a fresh 3 years runs from that date. The debt itself survives PNB v. Surendra Prasad Sinha.

6. X owned land in Y's possession since 1998. X sues Y in 2012 for recovery on the basis of title. Maintainable?

  1. 🎯 IssueHas X's title been extinguished by Y's possession for 14 years?
  2. 📜 LawArt. 65: 12 years from when the defendant's possession becomes adverse. S.27: on expiry, the owner's right is extinguished. Adverse possession must be open, continuous, peaceful and hostile (nec vi, nec clam, nec precario).
  3. 🔍 ApplyPossession alone is not enough; time runs only from when Y's possession became adverse to X. If Y was a tenant, licensee or permitted occupant, it was never adverse. If Y openly claimed the land as his own from 1998, 12 years ended in 2010.
  4. ✅ ConclusionIf Y proves hostile possession from 1998, the 2012 suit is barred and X's title is extinguished (S.27); Y becomes owner Ravinder Kaur Grewal. If Y's possession was permissive or not proved adverse, the suit is maintainable.

7. X paid a time-barred debt to Y and now wants the money back. Can he recover it?

  1. 🎯 IssueIs payment of a time-barred debt recoverable?
  2. 📜 LawS.3 bars only the remedy, not the right. The debt continues to exist; S.25(3) Contract Act even makes a written promise to pay such a debt valid.
  3. 🔍 ApplyWhen X paid, he was paying a debt that legally still existed. Y received what was actually owed.
  4. ✅ ConclusionNo; the suit is not maintainable. Limitation bars the remedy, not the right, so the payment was a valid discharge PNB v. Surendra Prasad Sinha.

8. An ex parte decree was passed against Balu. Because of illness he could not apply to set it aside in time. What can he do?

  1. 🎯 IssueCan the delay in applying under O.IX r.13 CPC be condoned?
  2. 📜 LawArt. 123: 30 days from the decree (or from knowledge of it, if summons was not duly served). S.5 applies to applications, so delay can be condoned on sufficient cause.
  3. 🔍 ApplyGenuine illness, proved by medical records, is a recognised sufficient cause. The court looks at the honesty of the reason, not just the number of days.
  4. ✅ ConclusionHe should file the O.IX r.13 application with an S.5 application and a medical certificate; the delay can be condoned Katiji N. Balakrishnan, possibly on payment of costs to the other side.
