⚖️Fair Trial, Charge and Double Jeopardy

The mighty State against one person: the law hands him a shield. Presumed innocent, told the charge, tried openly and quickly, and never tried twice for the same offence.

🧠Mind map

1The big picture

A criminal trial is a contest between the mighty State and one individual. To keep it fair, the law gives the individual a shield: he is presumed innocent; he is told exactly what he is accused of (the charge); he is tried by an independent judge in open court, quickly, with a lawyer; and once acquitted or convicted, he is never tried again for the same offence.

In the Best Bakery case, 14 people died in a riot, the witnesses were frightened into silence and everyone was acquitted. The Supreme Court ordered a fresh trial in another State and said: a fair trial is fair to both sides, to the accused and to the victim and society.

2Fair trial

1

Meaning

A fair trial is a trial by an impartial judge, a fair prosecutor and an atmosphere of judicial calm, in which the accused has every reasonable opportunity to defend himself. It is the heart of criminal justice.

🎨 Picture it

A fair trial is a cricket match with a neutral umpire: the State bowls, the accused bats, and the umpire belongs to neither side.

🎬 Case link

Zahira Sheikh (Best Bakery): fair to the accused, the victim and society. A trial where witnesses are terrified is no trial; retrial ordered in another State.

2

Constitutional basis

Art. 14Art. 20Art. 21Art. 22Art. 39A

Art. 14 (equality), Art. 20 (no ex post facto punishment, no double jeopardy, no self-incrimination), Art. 21 (procedure must be fair, just and reasonable: Maneka Gandhi, 1978), Art. 22 (counsel, production), Art. 39A (legal aid).

🧠 Memory trick

The constitutional bricks: 14 · 20 · 21 · 22 · 39A, equality, the three shields of Art. 20, fair procedure (Maneka Gandhi), counsel, legal aid.

3

Principles

S.366S.341

(1) Presumption of innocence; (2) independent, impartial judge; (3) open court (S.366; in camera for rape cases); (4) knowledge of the accusation (charge) and copies of documents; (5) trial in the presence of the accused, evidence in his presence; (6) right to a lawyer and legal aid (S.341); (7) right to cross-examine and lead defence evidence; (8) no self-incrimination; (9) speedy trial; (10) no double jeopardy; (11) reasoned judgment and a right of appeal.

Case story Zahira Sheikh (Best Bakery): in the Best Bakery riot case in Vadodara, 14 people were killed; witnesses turned hostile under threats and all accused were acquitted. The Supreme Court ordered a retrial outside Gujarat, holding that a fair trial means fairness to the victim and society too; when witnesses are frightened, the trial is a farce.
🧠 Memory trick

Spell F-A-I-R: Free judge · Accused informed and defended · Innocent till proved guilty · Rapid and open trial.

🔢 Number trick

Eleven principles; quote at least the first five with sections: open court S.366, legal aid S.341.

4

Speedy trial

Art. 21S.346S.479

A part of Art. 21 Hussainara Khatoon. No fixed outer time limit is laid down by the courts (P. Ramachandra Rao, 2002), but delay is weighed against the State. The BNSS sets timelines: charge within 60 days, judgment within 30–45 days, day-to-day trial (S.346), release of undertrials after half (one-third for first offenders) of the maximum sentence (S.479).

🎬 Case link

Hussainara Khatoon: speedy trial is part of Art. 21; undertrials held too long must be released.

🔢 Number trick

BNSS clocks: charge within 60 days · judgment within 30–45 days · day-to-day trial (S.346) · undertrial release at ½ (first offender ⅓) (S.479).

5

Presumption of innocence

The accused is presumed innocent until proved guilty beyond reasonable doubt by the prosecution; the benefit of doubt goes to the accused. Exceptions: some laws shift a burden on the accused (e.g. presumption in dowry death, NDPS, POCSO), but only after the prosecution proves the basic facts.

🎨 Picture it

The burden is a heavy bag the prosecution must carry all the way: guilt beyond reasonable doubt. Any doubt drops in the accused's lap as a benefit.

🗣️ Jargon buster

Some laws shift a burden to the accused (dowry death, NDPS, POCSO), but only after the prosecution proves the basic facts.

🔁 Recall: Spell "F-A-I-R": Free judge, Accused informed and defended, Innocent till proved guilty, Rapid and open trial.

1

Meaning and object

S.274

A charge is the precise formulation of the specific accusation against the accused, framed in writing by the court and read and explained to him. Object: to tell him exactly what he must meet, so he is not taken by surprise. In summons cases no formal charge is framed; the particulars are stated (S.274).

📖 Word origin

Charge: the precise written accusation, read and explained to the accused, so he is not taken by surprise.

🗣️ Jargon buster

In summons cases no formal charge is framed; the particulars are just stated (S.274).

2

Contents

S.234S.235S.236

The offence and its name, the law and section, the facts making the offence; particulars of time, place and person (S.235); where needed, the manner of committing it (S.236); previous conviction if relevant for enhanced punishment.

🧠 Memory trick

What · When · Where · Whom · How: offence and section; time, place and person (S.235); manner, where needed (S.236).

3

Errors

S.238

No error or omission in the charge is material unless the accused was in fact misled and a failure of justice resulted.

Case story Willie Slaney: Slaney was charged with murder read with common intention, but convicted of murder simpliciter without a separate charge. The Supreme Court upheld the conviction: the accused knew exactly what case he had to meet, so there was no prejudice; procedure is not a "fetish".
🧠 Memory trick

Misled + failure of justice, both needed, before an error in the charge matters (S.238).

🎬 Case link

Willie Slaney: charged with murder with common intention, convicted of plain murder; upheld, since he knew exactly what he had to meet.

4

Alteration

S.239

The court may alter or add to the charge at any time before judgment, reading and explaining it to the accused; it may adjourn or order a fresh trial if the change prejudices him, and witnesses may be recalled.

🎨 Picture it

The charge is written in pencil, not ink, until judgment: the court may alter or add to it (S.239), then read it out again; witnesses may be recalled.

5

Basic rule

S.241S.242S.243S.244S.245

For every distinct offence there shall be a separate charge, and each charge tried separately. Exceptions: (1) S.242: up to five offences of the same kind within 12 months; (2) S.243: offences in the same transaction; (3) S.244: where it is doubtful which offence the facts make, alternative charges; (4) S.245: conviction for a minor offence included in the charged one; (5) S.246: persons who may be charged jointly (same offence, same transaction, abettors, theft and receivers, etc.); also the Magistrate may join with the accused's consent.

🧠 Memory trick

One offence, one charge (S.241).

🔢 Number trick

5 in 12 months for offences of the same kind (S.242). It was 3 under the old Code.

6

Exceptions

Exceptions to "one offence, one charge": (1) up to five offences of the same kind within 12 months (S.242); (2) offences in the same transaction (S.243); (3) alternative charges where it is doubtful which offence the facts make (S.244); (4) conviction for a minor offence included in the charged one (S.245); (5) persons who may be charged jointly (S.246).

🧠 Memory trick

Five exceptions: "Same Same Doubt Minor Joint" → same kind (242) · same transaction (243) · doubt (244) · minor offence (245) · joint charge (246).

🔁 Recall: Charge = "What, When, Where, Whom, How" (S.234–236); and the golden rule: one offence, one charge, with five exceptions (same kind, same transaction, doubt, minor, joint).

4Double jeopardy

Exam question: Double jeopardy: autrefois acquit and autrefois convict (S.337 BNSS, Art. 20(2))

🧠 Say it: “Mom Asked: Same Exam Count?”

  1. MMomMeaning
  2. AAskedArt. 20(2)
  3. SSameS.337 BNSS
  4. EExamExceptions
  5. CCountCase law
1

Meaning

Nemo debet bis vexari: no one should be tried twice for the same offence. Pleas: autrefois acquit (previously acquitted) and autrefois convict (previously convicted).

📖 Word origin

Nemo debet bis vexari: no one should be vexed twice. Autrefois acquit = "formerly acquitted"; autrefois convict = "formerly convicted" (French).

🎨 Picture it

Like a ball already given out: the umpire cannot call the batsman back to be given out again for the same ball.

2

Art. 20(2)

Art. 20

No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once. It needs a previous prosecution and punishment by a court; departmental or customs proceedings are not "prosecution" (Maqbool Hussain v. State of Bombay, 1953).

🧠 Memory trick

Art. 20(2) needs prosecution + punishment by a court. Departmental or customs proceedings are not "prosecution" (Maqbool Hussain, 1953).

3

S.337 BNSS

S.337

A person once tried by a court of competent jurisdiction and convicted or acquitted shall not, while it remains in force, be tried again for the same offence, nor for any other offence on the same facts for which a different charge could have been made or of which he could have been convicted.

🎨 Picture it

S.337 is the bigger umbrella: it covers the same offence and also any other offence on the same facts that could have been charged.

🎬 Case link

Kolla Veera Raghav Rao: convicted for a bounced cheque, then prosecuted for cheating on the same facts: barred.

4

Exceptions

S.337(2)

(1) A distinct offence for which a separate charge could have been made may be tried later, with the State Government's consent; (2) where the act causes consequences that happen later (e.g. the victim dies after a conviction for grievous hurt), a fresh trial for the graver offence is allowed; (3) where the first court was not competent to try the graver offence; (4) a discharge or dismissal of a complaint is not an acquittal.

(from the section): (a) convicted of grievous hurt; victim dies later: may be tried for culpable homicide. (b) convicted by the Sessions Court of culpable homicide: cannot be tried again for murder on the same facts. (c) A, B, C convicted by a Magistrate of robbing D: may later be tried for dacoity on the same facts (the Magistrate could not try dacoity).

🌍 Real life

R is convicted of grievous hurt. Weeks later the victim dies: R can be tried again for the graver offence, because the consequence happened later (S.337).

🧠 Memory trick

Doors that open again: new consequence · distinct offence (with consent) · first court not competent. And a discharge is not an acquittal.

5

Case law

S.300S.337Art. 20
  • Kolla Veera Raghav Rao v. Gorantla Venkateswara Rao: Second prosecution barred: the statutory bar applies to any offence on the same facts, wider than Art. 20(2).
Case story Kolla Veera Raghav Rao: a man was convicted for a bounced cheque under the Negotiable Instruments Act. He was then prosecuted for cheating on the same facts. The Supreme Court stopped it: S.300 (now S.337 BNSS) is wider than Art. 20(2); it bars a second trial on the same facts, even under a different law.
🧠 Memory trick

Same facts, same fight, one time.

🔁 Recall: "Same facts, same fight, one time": but a fresh death, a different, distinct offence (with consent), or a court that couldn't try the bigger offence opens the door again.

7Golden rules

  • ★ 1A fair trial is fair to the accused, the victim and society; a trial vitiated by intimidation can be held again. Zahira Sheikh (Best Bakery)
  • ★ 2Speedy trial is part of Art. 21. Hussainara Khatoon
  • ★ 3Every distinct offence needs a separate charge (S.241); exceptions in S.242–246.
  • ★ 4An error or omission in the charge is fatal only if it caused a failure of justice (S.238). Willie Slaney
  • ★ 5Double jeopardy bars a second trial on the same facts for the same offence, even under a different law. Kolla Veera Raghav Rao

8Part C: problem routes

Every problem takes the same four stops: issue → law → apply → conclusion.

1. An accused acquitted for lack of evidence is prosecuted again for the same offence because new evidence is found, and convicted. Legal?

  1. 🎯 IssueDoes new evidence allow a second trial after acquittal?
  2. 📜 LawArt. 20(2); S.337(1) BNSS: a person acquitted by a competent court shall not be tried again for the same offence while the acquittal stands. Remedy against a wrong acquittal is an appeal (S.419), not a fresh trial.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe acquittal by a competent court stands; new evidence is not an exception under S.337.
  4. ✅ ConclusionIllegal: the second trial and conviction are barred by S.337 (autrefois acquit); the State could only appeal against the acquittal.

2. R was convicted of grievous hurt to K. Later K died. Can R be tried for culpable homicide?

  1. 🎯 IssueDoes a later death allow a fresh trial?
  2. 📜 LawS.337 BNSS: a person convicted of an act causing consequences which together with the act make a different offence may be tried again for that offence if the consequences had not happened, or were not known to the court, at the time of the conviction (illustration (a)).
  3. 🔍 ApplyK's death happened after R's conviction for grievous hurt; it is a new consequence.
  4. ✅ ConclusionYes, R can be tried for culpable homicide (BNS 105); it is an exception to double jeopardy.

3. A was convicted by the Court of Session of culpable homicide of B. Can he be tried again on the same facts for murder? / A, B, C convicted by a Magistrate of robbing D: can they be tried for dacoity?

  1. 🎯 IssueWhen is a second trial for a graver offence on the same facts barred?
  2. 📜 LawS.337(1): no second trial for an offence for which a different charge could have been made at the first trial. S.337(4): a later trial is allowed if the first court was not competent to try the graver offence.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe Court of Session was competent to try murder, so a second trial for murder is barred. The Magistrate was not competent to try dacoity (a Sessions offence), so a trial for dacoity on the same facts is allowed.
  4. ✅ ConclusionMurder after culpable homicide: barred. Dacoity after a Magistrate's robbery conviction: allowed (the illustrations to the section say exactly this).

4. An undertrial prisoner has been in jail for three years without trial. Remedy under the BNSS?

  1. 🎯 IssueWhat relief is available for prolonged pre-trial detention?
  2. 📜 LawS.479: release on personal bond after half the maximum sentence for the offence (one-third for a first-time offender), except offences punishable with death or life; S.480(6): bail if a Magistrate trial is not concluded within 60 days of the first date for evidence; S.187(3): default bail; Art. 21 speedy trial Hussainara Khatoon.
  3. 🔍 ApplyIf three years is half (or one-third) of the maximum for his offence, he must be released; otherwise the delay is itself a strong ground for bail and for directions to expedite the trial.
  4. ✅ ConclusionApply for release under S.479 (or bail under S.480 / S.483 citing Art. 21); if denied, approach the High Court for bail and expeditious trial, or habeas corpus.

5. A was convicted of rape and murder of a 12-year-old girl and sentenced to death (confirmed by the High Court). His appeal took 20–25 years to reach the Supreme Court. Can the sentence be commuted for delay?

  1. 🎯 IssueDoes delay in the judicial process entitle a death convict to commutation?
  2. 📜 LawRape of a girl under 12: BNS 65(2) (minimum 20 years, up to life or death). Triveniben v. State of Gujarat (1989): delay during judicial proceedings is not counted; only delay after the final judgment, in disposing of mercy petitions, can be a ground. Shatrughan Chauhan v. Union of India (2014): unexplained, inordinate delay in deciding mercy petitions can justify commutation to life.
  3. 🔍 ApplyThe delay was in the appeal (judicial process), so it is not by itself a ground; but if there is later unexplained delay in deciding his mercy petition, he can seek commutation.
  4. ✅ ConclusionNo commutation merely for delay in the appeal; only unexplained delay in deciding a mercy petition after the final judgment can be a ground.

6. Four persons conspire in a hotel; one is caught with an unlicensed gun. What offence, how to frame the charge, which court?

  1. 🎯 IssueHow are joint offenders charged?
  2. 📜 LawCriminal conspiracy BNS 61(2); Arms Act S.25 for the gun. S.246: persons accused of the same offence in the same transaction, or of conspiracy, may be charged and tried together. S.243: several offences in one transaction tried together. Robbery BNS 309; murder BNS 103 with common intention BNS 3(5).
  3. 🔍 ApplyAll four are charged jointly with conspiracy; the gun-holder also under the Arms Act. Where robbery led to a killing, charge all four with robbery and with murder read with BNS 3(5); whether the common intention covered killing is decided on evidence.
  4. ✅ ConclusionFrame a joint charge under S.246 (conspiracy; robbery and murder with common intention), with a separate Arms Act charge for the gun-holder; the Court of Session tries it where any charge is a Sessions offence.
