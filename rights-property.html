Tenant Rights
Eviction notice · Security deposit · Rent increase · Utility cut
▼
ENTITLEMENT
Landlord Cannot Evict Without Legal Notice
A landlord cannot evict a tenant without serving proper legal notice — typically 1 to 3 months depending on state law and agreement terms. Grounds for eviction must be valid: non-payment of rent, misuse of premises, landlord's genuine personal need. Forcible eviction without court order is illegal and can result in criminal charges.
WHAT TO DO
1
Check: Is it in writing? Is the reason stated? Is the notice period as per agreement?
2
Rent Control Acts (where applicable) restrict eviction further — check state-specific rules.
3
If notice is illegal — file objection in writing to landlord. Consult lawyer.
4
File injunction petition in Civil Court if forcible eviction is attempted.
5
If landlord cuts electricity/water to force eviction — file criminal complaint (use of force).
⚠️ Landlord Cannot Cut Utilities
Cutting electricity or water supply to force a tenant out is use of criminal force. File police complaint + injunction. Courts take this seriously.
Legal basis: Transfer of Property Act 1882, Sections 105-117; State Rent Control Acts; Specific Relief Act 1963 — injunction against eviction
ENTITLEMENT
Security Deposit Must Be Returned Within 30 Days
Security deposit must be returned within 30 days of vacating (after deducting actual damage — normal wear and tear cannot be deducted). Landlord cannot make arbitrary deductions. Send a formal notice for return. If not returned — file civil suit or consumer complaint.
💬 What to Say
→Send written notice citing: "Please return my security deposit of ₹X within 7 days as I vacated on [date] with no damage."
→Get a signed inventory checklist when vacating — this limits deduction disputes.
→If refused — file Consumer Complaint (landlord providing rental service) or Civil suit.
→Also file complaint with Rent Authority if registered under state Rent Act.
Legal basis: State Rent Control Acts; Consumer Protection Act 2019 — landlord is service provider; Transfer of Property Act 1882

Builder & RERA Rights
Delay compensation · Refund · RERA registered · Structural defects
▼
ENTITLEMENT
Builder Cannot Delay Possession — RERA Protects You
All real estate projects above 500 sq m (or 8 apartments) must be RERA registered. Builder cannot delay possession beyond the agreement date without paying interest. You can claim: full refund + interest if you wish to exit, or continued interest on delay if you want to wait. File at State RERA Authority.
WHAT TO DO
1
Check if project is RERA registered at your State RERA portal.
2
File complaint at State RERA Authority — online, no lawyer needed.
3
Claim: refund OR interest @ SBI MCLR + 2% for delay period (whichever you choose).
4
RERA orders are enforceable as decrees of Civil Court.
5
For structural defects — builder liable for 5 years after handover.
Legal basis: Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act 2016, Sections 3, 12, 18, 19; RERA Rules; SC: Imperia Structures v. Anil Patni — RERA remedy is additional, not exclusive

Inheritance & Succession Rights
Will · Legal heir · Daughters equal rights · Partition
▼
ENTITLEMENT
Daughters Have Equal Inheritance Rights — Retroactive
Under the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act 2005 — daughters are coparceners by birth in ancestral property, equal to sons. The Supreme Court in Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma (2020) clarified this applies retroactively — even if the father died before 2005. A married daughter's rights are equal to an unmarried daughter.
WHAT TO DO
1
For ancestral property — file partition suit in Civil Court.
2
If excluded from will — contest within 12 years limitation period.
3
Apply for Legal Heir Certificate at District Tehsildar (for bank, property transfer).
4
Apply for Succession Certificate in Civil Court for movable assets (shares, FD).
5
Probate of will required for immovable property in some states.
Legal basis: Hindu Succession Act 1956, Section 6 (amended 2005); SC: Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma (2020); Indian Succession Act 1925 (Christians/Parsis); Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Act 1937
